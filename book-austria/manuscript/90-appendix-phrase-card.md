# Appendix A: German Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Austria. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, followed by the **German**, and a simple pronunciation guide in italics.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. If you can, say the first three or four out loud a few times. You will feel much less awkward using them when the moment comes.

A note on language: Austria's official language is German, and although Austrians speak a warm regional dialect among themselves, every adult understands and will respond to standard High German (*Hochdeutsch*) — which is what is written below. English is very widely spoken in tourist areas, hotels, and among younger Austrians, so you will rarely be stuck. But a few words of German change the temperature of an interaction immediately: they mark you as someone paying attention, which is exactly the wrong target for a scam.

A note on pronunciation: German is largely phonetic once you know a few rules. The letter combination *ei* sounds like English "eye" (*nein* = "nine"), while *ie* sounds like "ee" (*Polizei* ends "-tsye"). The letter *z* is always "ts" (*Quittung*'s cousin *zahlen* is "TSAH-len"), *w* is an English "v" (*wie* = "vee"), *v* is usually "f," and *ch* after a back vowel is a soft throat-clear (as in "Bach"). The *ß* (called *eszett*) is just a sharp "ss." Vowels with an umlaut — *ä, ö, ü* — are rounded; approximate *ü* as the "ew" in "few." None of this has to be perfect. Austrians are forgiving of an accent as long as the key word is clear.

A note on formality: the phrases below use the *Sie* (formal) form, which is correct with any stranger, official, waiter, or driver. *Lassen Sie* and *rufen Sie* are the polite *Sie* imperatives. They register as courteous but firm — exactly the right tone for anything defensive.

## Asking before you commit

**How much does it cost?**
Wie viel kostet das?
*Vee feel KOSS-tet dass?*

The single most useful question on this card. Ask it before you get in a taxi, before you sit down, before you accept any "tour," ticket, or service. A legitimate seller gives you a clear euro figure; a scammer stalls, deflects, or names a number that keeps changing.

**Is there a cover charge? Is the bread/water extra?**
Gibt es ein Gedeck? Kostet das Brot / das Wasser extra?
*Gipt ess ine guh-DECK? KOSS-tet dass broht / dass VAH-ser EX-trah?*

Use this before you sit at any tourist-zone restaurant. Austrian places may legally charge a *Gedeck* (cover), a *Besteckgebühr* (cutlery fee), for the bread basket, or for bottled water — all of which appear on the bill in this book's overcharging scams. Asking up front is the most reliable way to avoid the surprise.

**Is the (tap) water free?**
Ist das Leitungswasser gratis?
*Isst dass LYE-toongs-vah-ser GRAH-tiss?*

*Leitungswasser* is tap water. In a tourist café you may be served pricey bottled water by default; asking for *Leitungswasser* and confirming it is *gratis* heads off the padded-bill trick on the Zell am See Esplanade and elsewhere.

**Does the meter run? / Please switch on the meter.**
Läuft der Taxameter? / Schalten Sie bitte das Taxameter ein.
*Loyft dair TAX-ah-may-ter? / SHAHL-ten zee BIT-teh dass TAX-ah-may-ter ine.*

Say this the moment you get into any Austrian taxi. Fares are metered and regulated; a driver who "forgets" the meter or quotes a flat number is setting up an overcharge. If he refuses the meter, get out and find a marked car.

**In euros, please.**
Auf Euro, bitte.
*Owf OY-roh, BIT-teh.*

This is your defense against Dynamic Currency Conversion — the single most common scam in Austria. When an ATM screen or a card terminal offers to charge you "in your home currency," or shows a conversion rate, say this and choose the euro option. Being charged in euros means being charged the real rate.

**Do you accept credit cards?**
Akzeptieren Sie Kreditkarten?
*Ak-tsep-TEE-ren zee kreh-DEET-kar-ten?*

If the answer is yes but the reader later turns out to be "broken" and you're steered toward an ATM, you are being scammed. See "Responding to a scam in progress" below.

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**
Nein, danke!
*Nine, DAHN-keh!*

Said firmly and without slowing your pace. No elaboration is needed. *Nein, danke* is a complete refusal; saying it louder does not strengthen it, and saying it while still walking is stronger than stopping to say it.

**I'm not interested.**
Ich habe kein Interesse.
*Ikh HAH-beh kine in-teh-RES-seh.*

Stronger than *nein, danke*. Use it when a tout, a clipboard-holder, or a ticket-seller keeps talking after the first refusal.

**Leave me alone!**
Lassen Sie mich in Ruhe!
*LAH-sen zee mikh in ROO-eh!*

The correct firm-but-not-aggressive way to tell someone in your personal space to back off. *In Ruhe lassen* literally means "leave [me] in peace." It carries weight without being rude, and most distraction or bracelet teams disengage the moment they hear it.

**Don't touch me.**
Fassen Sie mich nicht an.
*FAH-sen zee mikh nikht ahn.*

The phrase that goes with the pickpocket pattern. Say it clearly and step back. Most distraction pairs will break off rather than escalate.

**I didn't order that.**
Das habe ich nicht bestellt.
*Dass HAH-beh ikh nikht buh-SHTELLT.*

Say this calmly about any unrequested bread, *Schnaps*, bottled water, or side that appears on your bill. You are not obliged to pay for items you did not order.

**That is not what we agreed.**
Das war nicht, was wir vereinbart haben.
*Dass var nikht, vass veer fair-INE-bart HAH-ben.*

A short, firm line that accuses nobody but puts the burden of explanation on the other party. Use it for taxi fares above a quoted price, hotel charges that differ from the booking, and tour-operator price-creep.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I'm calling the police.**
Ich rufe die Polizei!
*Ikh ROO-feh dee poh-lee-TSYE!*

The single most useful phrase in this appendix when something is going wrong. The Austrian police number is **133** (or **112** for all emergencies). The word *Polizei* changes the atmosphere immediately, and most scam operators back down the moment they hear it.

**I'll call my bank.**
Ich rufe meine Bank an.
*Ikh ROO-feh MY-neh bahnk ahn.*

Say this if a restaurant or driver disputes a card transaction or claims the reader is broken. Calling your card issuer from the table is a legitimate move and signals that the charge will be disputed.

**Please give me an itemized receipt.**
Geben Sie mir bitte eine detaillierte Rechnung.
*GAY-ben zee meer BIT-teh INE-eh deh-tye-LEER-teh REKH-noong.*

Or simply:
Ich möchte eine Quittung.
*Ikh MERKH-teh INE-eh KVIT-toong.*

A *Quittung* is a receipt; a *detaillierte Rechnung* is an itemized bill. Asking forces the establishment to account for every line, and it is the most polite version of "I do not accept this charge." A legitimate place hands it over without complaint; a tourist trap stalls.

**I was scammed. Please help me.**
Ich wurde betrogen. Bitte helfen Sie mir.
*Ikh VOOR-deh buh-TROH-gen. BIT-teh HELL-fen zee meer.*

Use this with a police officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, or any bystander you want to enlist. *Betrogen* is the past participle of *betrügen*, "to defraud" — it communicates precisely what happened. For pickpocketing specifically: *Ich wurde bestohlen* ("I was robbed / had something stolen").

**I'd like to file a report (a complaint).**
Ich möchte eine Anzeige erstatten.
*Ikh MERKH-teh INE-eh AHN-tsye-geh air-SHTAT-ten.*

An *Anzeige* is the formal police crime report — the Austrian equivalent of filing a complaint. You will need its reference for a card chargeback, a travel-insurance claim, or a replacement passport. Saying *eine Anzeige erstatten* to an officer tells them exactly what you need.

## Getting help

**Help! Thief!**
Hilfe! Dieb!
*HILL-feh! Deep!*

Shouted in a crowded street, this is understood by every bystander as "someone stole from me." *Hilfe* is the universal call for help; *Dieb* means thief.

**Where is the nearest police station?**
Wo ist die nächste Polizeiwache?
*Voh isst dee NEX-teh poh-lee-TSYE-vah-kheh?*

A *Polizeiwache* (or *Polizeiinspektion*) is a police station. Every Austrian town center has one within a short walk, and major railway stations have a police post on site.

**I'd like an official taxi.**
Ich möchte ein offizielles Taxi.
*Ikh MERKH-teh ine off-it-see-ELL-ess TAX-ee.*

Use this at airports and stations where drivers approach you. An official Austrian taxi is a marked car at the designated rank with a working meter — not a private car whose driver walked up to you in the hall.

**Please call 133.**
Rufen Sie bitte die 133.
*ROO-fen zee BIT-teh dee EYNS-dry-dry.*

Or, under stress, just shout:
Hilfe! Rufen Sie die Polizei!
*HILL-feh! ROO-fen zee dee poh-lee-TSYE!*

**133** reaches the Austrian police directly; **112** is the pan-European emergency number and routes you to whatever service you need. *Eins-drei-drei* (one-three-three) is easy to shout, and every Austrian adult recognizes it.

**Is there someone who speaks English?**
Spricht hier jemand Englisch?
*Shprikht heer YAY-mahnt ENG-lish?*

In practice, in any Austrian hotel, station, or tourist business, the answer is almost always yes.

## Reading euro amounts on Austrian bills

Austrian receipts and price tags use the comma as the decimal separator and the period (or a space) as the thousands separator — the reverse of American convention. This book writes prices in the English style (€1.50, €1,500), but the bill you're handed in Austria will use the local style — watch for the reversal and don't panic.

- On an Austrian bill, **€1,50** means one euro fifty cents — not one thousand five hundred.
- **€1.500** (or **€1 500**) means one thousand five hundred euros.
- **€1.500,00** is the fully-formatted "one thousand five hundred euros exactly" — the style you'll see on a restaurant tab.

You will also see prices written with a comma and a hyphen for round euros, e.g. **€12,–** meaning "twelve euros even."

| Amount | Written (German) | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| €1 | ein Euro | *ine OY-roh* |
| €5 | fünf Euro | *fewnf OY-roh* |
| €10 | zehn Euro | *tsayn OY-roh* |
| €20 | zwanzig Euro | *TSVAHN-tsikh OY-roh* |
| €50 | fünfzig Euro | *FEWNF-tsikh OY-roh* |
| €100 | hundert Euro | *HOON-dert OY-roh* |
| €500 | fünfhundert Euro | *FEWNF-hoon-dert OY-roh* |
| €1.000 | tausend Euro | *TOW-zent OY-roh* |

If a server or cashier tells you a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it down:

**Please write the number down.**
Schreiben Sie mir bitte die Zahl auf.
*SHRY-ben zee meer BIT-teh dee tsahl owf.*

They will almost always oblige.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Austria, calm firmness works far better than a raised voice — Austrian service culture is reserved and correct, and a composed *nein, danke* or *auf Euro, bitte* is already a complete position. If you need to escalate, *lassen Sie mich in Ruhe* and *fassen Sie mich nicht an* are both firm without crossing into rudeness, and *ich rufe die Polizei* is the firmest card in the deck.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar. Calm and firm beats loud and uncertain every time.
