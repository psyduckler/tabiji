# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The fifty-six scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: If a screen offers to charge you "in your home currency," always say no

This is the single most common scam in Austria, and it is the one almost every visitor walks into at least once. At an ATM, or when a waiter or shop assistant hands you a card terminal, a screen asks whether you would like to be charged in euros or in your home currency — dollars, pounds, Canadian dollars — and helpfully shows you a conversion rate. It feels like a courtesy. It is the opposite. This is Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC), and choosing your home currency hands the conversion to the machine's operator at a markup that typically runs 4 to 12 percent worse than your own bank's rate, plus a fixed fee on top.

The pattern is everywhere in this book. The bright orange-and-blue Euronet machines are the worst repeat offenders: they appear on Linzergasse and Getreidegasse in Salzburg, on Landstraße in Linz, on Herrengasse and the Hauptplatz in Graz, near the docks in Krems and Melk, by the station in Bad Gastein, and along Maria-Theresien-Strasse in Innsbruck. They sit in tourist sightlines on purpose, they are standalone (not attached to any bank branch), and they are tuned to push DCC and a withdrawal fee that a normal Austrian bank ATM — a Bank Austria, Erste/Sparkasse, or Raiffeisen machine — does not charge. The same screen appears on restaurant and shop card terminals across the 1st district in Vienna and in every tourist old town.

The defense is one word, applied automatically every single time: **always choose euros.** Austria is in the eurozone; a euro price is the real price. When an ATM screen shows you a "guaranteed rate" in your currency, decline the conversion and select "without conversion" or "continue in EUR." When a card terminal lights up with a dollar figure, tell the server "in euros, please" before you tap. Prefer a bank-branded ATM over a freestanding Euronet box, and where you can, pay by card and skip cash withdrawals altogether. There is never a situation in Austria where being charged in your home currency is in your interest.

*You will see this pattern in: the Euronet DCC traps in Salzburg, Linz, Graz, Innsbruck, Bad Gastein, and the Wachau (Krems/Melk), the Vienna and Kärntner Strasse card-terminal conversions, and the "broken card reader / let's go to the ATM" variants that show up inside the transport and restaurant scams.*

## Pattern 2: Transport starts at the official queue — never from a driver who approached you

The second pattern starts the moment you arrive, tired, at an airport or a station. Somewhere between baggage claim and the marked taxi rank, a friendly man steps into your path. He is not in a marked car. He says "taxi?" — sometimes he says he is "with the hotel taxis," sometimes he greets you warmly in your own language — and he quotes a flat fare that sounds fine. It will not be fine.

Austrian taxi fares are tightly metered and regulated, so any driver who "forgets" the meter or quotes a round number off the top of his head is steering you toward an overcharge. At Vienna Airport, curbside drivers quote off-meter "fixed" fares of 50 to 70 euros into town — both a rip-off and, for a curbside hail inside city limits, technically against Vienna's taxi ordinance. The same off-meter quote runs at Innsbruck Airport (a flat "airport price" of 30 to 35 euros), at Graz Airport and the late-night ranks by the bars, and from Linz the meter you trusted in town quietly stops protecting you the moment the cab crosses the city line toward Hörsching airport. Around Zell am See, unlicensed "black taxis" greet arrivals at the Bahnhof and at Salzburg Airport 90 kilometers away, offering to beat the marked cars; the long mountain transfers to Bad Gastein and Zell are exactly where a quoted "deal" balloons.

There is a public-transport version of the same trap. Vienna's airport sits in VOR tariff Zone 100, outside the city core, so a Wiener Linien 24-hour pass does **not** cover the airport train — and an ÖBB conductor will fine you 105 euros on the spot even though you were sure your ticket was valid. In the Wachau, a boat ticket bought at the wrong kiosk is for the other company's ship, and the crew will wave you off the gangway at departure time.

The defense, across all of it: **never take a ride from a driver who approached you first.** Walk past anyone who says "taxi" in an arrivals hall and go to the marked, metered rank, or pre-book a named transfer company with a price in writing. When you do take a taxi, confirm the meter is running, or for fixed-price airport runs get the total quoted and agreed before you close the door. For trains, check the zone: the airport train (CAT or the ÖBB S7/Railjet) needs its own ticket, not a city pass. And buy boat tickets at the official company counter, confirming the operator name and the ship before you pay.

*You will see this pattern in: the Vienna, Linz, Innsbruck, and Graz airport-taxi shakedowns, the Zell am See black taxis and no-meter day-trip quotes, the Salzburg-airport-to-Bad-Gastein and -to-Zell transfers, the Vienna airport-train 105-euro zone fine, and the wrong-boat ticket in the Wachau.*

## Pattern 3: If the table has a view, assume the extras are part of the price

The third pattern is the most common overcharge in this book, and across a full meal it is among the most expensive. Austria lets restaurants charge for some things that surprise visitors — a Gedeck or "cover," a Besteckgebühr (cutlery fee), a basket of bread, even bottled water where you expected free tap — and all of these are legal. They are simply rarely used outside tourist zones, and almost never disclosed in English before you order. The more spectacular the view, the more reliably they appear.

The mechanic is the same whether you are on a terrace over the lake or a square full of cruise passengers. In Hallstatt, a coffee, a couple of sandwiches, and an ice cream at a lakefront table on the Marktplatz "lands like a punch," and the card machine sometimes does its own conversion trick on top (see Pattern 1). On the Schlossberg terrace in Graz, beside the Uhrturm and the best panorama in the city, prices carry a quiet view-tax markup. In Dürnstein, when a cruise boat empties onto the single narrow Hauptstraße, the terraces rush the crowd through a schnitzel and a glass of Grüner Veltliner at inflated turnover prices. On Vienna's Kärntner Strasse and Graben, the bill arrives with a 3-to-8-euro-per-head "Besteckgebühr," "Gedeck," or "coperto." At a lakeside café on the Zell am See Esplanade, the glass of water you assumed was the free tap quietly is not. Above Bad Gastein, a mountain restaurant bills you for the bread basket you never touched and a cover you never agreed to. On Linz's Hauptplatz and outside the Getreidegasse in Salzburg, a warm "this way, please" from a man at the door is the opening move of an upsell and a padded bill.

The defense is a single habit, repeated before every sit-down meal. **Read the printed menu — including the small print — before you sit.** If you cannot see per-item prices, or if water, bread, and a cover are not spelled out, ask: "Was kostet das Gedeck? Ist das Leitungswasser gratis?" Decline the bread basket out loud if you do not want it. Be wary of any restaurant where someone is standing at the door waving tourists in — locals do not need to be lured. When the bill comes, check it line by line, and ask for an itemized receipt ("eine detaillierte Rechnung, bitte") if a charge is unexplained. A legitimate place hands it over without fuss.

*You will see this pattern in: the Kärntner Strasse cover-charge creep (Vienna), the Hallstatt lakeview bill, the Schlossberg view-tax (Graz), the Dürnstein cruise-crowd rush (Wachau), the Hauptplatz upsell (Linz), the Getreidegasse scam restaurant (Salzburg), the Esplanade water-and-bill padding (Zell am See), and the mountain-restaurant cover pile-on (Bad Gastein).*

## Pattern 4: Buy the ticket from the venue — never from a reseller, a concierge, or a man on the square

The fourth pattern is the markup that hides between you and the thing you actually want to see. Austria's attractions, tours, and lift tickets nearly all sell directly, at a fixed and published price. Any layer between you and that official counter — a costumed seller on a square, a third-party "skip the line" website, a hotel concierge with a commission, a tour desk in the old town — exists to take a cut, and sometimes to sell you something other than what you pictured.

The signature version is Vienna's fake Mozart sellers: men in powdered wigs and red frock coats on Stephansplatz and outside the Staatsoper, selling tickets to a grand-sounding "Hofburg concert" that turns out to be a student ensemble in a rented function hall. Salzburg runs its own variants — Sound of Music tours sold at a markup or rebadged as something they are not, and counterfeit "Mozart memorabilia" from street vendors. The reseller markup repeats across the country: third-party Melk Abbey "priority" tickets in the Wachau, marked-up Pöstlingbergbahn and attraction tickets in Linz, a Swarovski Crystal Worlds "package" from an Innsbruck tour desk at 38-to-41 euros for a trip you can do far cheaper yourself, a discount "ski pass middleman" near Innsbruck station, and a Kitzsteinhorn cable-car counter that sells you a 61-euro glacier return when your hotel could have handed you a Zell am See–Kaprun Summer Card for free. The Grossglockner and Krimml Waterfalls "private tours" out of Zell, and the "full day in fairytale Hallstatt" coach tours out of Vienna and Salzburg that give you two hours on the ground, are the same logic at excursion scale.

The defense is one rule: **find the official website or the venue's own ticket counter, and buy there.** Wiener Staatsoper, Musikverein, and Konzerthaus all sell online in English with seat maps — Musikverein standing-room is 6 to 10 euros at the door. Buy abbey, museum, railway, and cable-car tickets from the operator directly. Ask your hotel which free or discounted guest card (the Zell am See–Kaprun Summer Card, regional guest cards) you are already entitled to before you pay for a lift or a shuttle. Never buy a ticket from a costumed person on a square, and treat a concierge or tour-desk "package" as a convenience you are paying a premium for — check the do-it-yourself price first.

*You will see this pattern in: the fake Mozart ticket sellers (Vienna), the Sound of Music and Mozart-memorabilia overcharges (Salzburg), the Melk Abbey reseller markup (Wachau), the Pöstlingbergbahn/attraction resale (Linz), the Swarovski shuttle and ski-pass middleman (Innsbruck), the cable-car upsell over the free Summer Card and the Grossglockner/Krimml private tours (Zell am See), and the Hallstatt day-trip coach overselling.*

## Pattern 5: In a crowd, contact is a pickpocket tell — and a stranger's "excuse me" is the opener

The fifth pattern covers the small number of scams in Austria that put hands on you or your pockets — and in a country this safe, they cluster tightly in two places: the busiest transit pinch points, and the friendly approach on a famous square.

Vienna carries the weight of it: the capital records roughly 5,300 pickpocket cases a year and more than half of all of Austria's pickpocketing, concentrated on the U1 line, the escalators rising into Stephansplatz station, and the 1st-district tourist triangle. Graz has its own version at Jakominiplatz, where the doors of trams 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 open and the polite Austrian queue dissolves for a few seconds into a shoulder-to-shoulder crush — exactly the window a pickpocket needs. The approach version is the distraction play: a "free" flower pressed into your hand, a bracelet tied on your wrist, a clipboard petition "for deaf children," a stranger who needs exactly 47 euros for a "Munich ticket." All of them use one mechanic — occupy your hands or your conscience while a partner works your pockets — and they run on Stephansplatz and at the stations in Vienna, on the Hauptplatz and Landstraße in Linz, and along the Herrengasse in Graz.

A harder-edged cousin is the stranger who claims authority or friendship. Vienna's fake-police "ID check" is the one to know cold: men in plain clothes flash a fake badge, say counterfeit currency is circulating, and ask to "check" your wallet — and your PIN — a scheme Der Standard documented against tourists in the Innere Stadt. Innsbruck runs a "broken camera blame" (you are handed a camera, it is fumbled onto the cobbles, and you are pressed to pay) and a "friendly nightlife lure" toward a "much better place" that ends in a padded bar bill. Salzburg has a sympathy approach at the Hauptbahnhof built around a photo of a child.

The defense is two habits. First, in any crush — a tram door at Jakominiplatz, the U1 at Stephansplatz, a packed escalator — put your bag in front of you, both hands on it, wallet out of back pockets, until you are clear. Second, treat any unsolicited approach in a tourist zone as a transaction, not a kindness: do not take the flower or let a bracelet be tied on, do not sign the clipboard, do not hand your phone or camera to a stranger who offered, and never show your wallet or enter a PIN for anyone on the street. Real Austrian police do their checks at a station, never by examining your cash on the pavement; if someone insists, say "Gehen wir zur Polizei" — let's go to the police — and a fake one will melt away.

*You will see this pattern in: the U1 and Stephansplatz pickpockets and the flower/bracelet/petition/"Munich ticket" distraction theft (Vienna), the fake-police PIN extraction (Vienna), the Jakominiplatz tram-crush pickpocketing and Herrengasse clipboard distraction (Graz), the Hauptplatz petition distraction (Linz), the broken-camera blame and nightlife lure (Innsbruck), and the station sympathy approach (Salzburg).*

## Pattern 6: Money off the platform is money gone — and so is a fine you didn't know you'd earned

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch before you commit — because once the deposit is wired, the car is past the barrier, or the wheels are in the wrong parking bay, the terms turn out not to be what you thought.

The most painful version is the rental that does not exist. A belle-époque studio over the Gastein waterfall, or a cheap Vienna flat with beautiful photos, is listed well below every comparable apartment in town — and the person emailing you does not own it. They post stolen photos (sometimes staging a "viewing" in an apartment that is not theirs), then ask for a deposit and a "key delivery" transfer that vanishes. Austria's Federal Criminal Police put the Vienna apartment-fraud rate at roughly 30 cases a week, and Bad Gastein's seasonal-rental market runs the same play. The other version is the fine you trigger without realizing the rule. In Hallstatt, a man waves you past the village barrier as if it is fine to keep driving — into a no-car zone that fines you — and the steep pay-by-the-hour lots punish a long lunch and a lost ticket. Dürnstein funnels cars away from the abbey into a ring of numbered, app-only pay lots (P1–P9). Graz's "Blue Zone" short-term parking is stricter than it looks and leaves a 24-euro slip under your wiper. At Hallstatt's classic viewpoint, an offer to "send up a drone" for the postcard shot can earn you a fine in a controlled-airspace zone.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction before you commit. **Book accommodation only through platforms with pay-through and dispute protection** (Booking.com, Airbnb), never by wiring a deposit to an email address, and verify the property and address on Google Street View before you pay — a real apartment has signage or an intercom label; a scam listing priced far below the market does not exist. **Pay by credit card, not bank transfer or cash,** so a chargeback is possible. Before you drive into a historic core — Hallstatt, Dürnstein, the Salzburg and Innsbruck old towns — assume it is a restricted or no-car zone, park in the official signed lot outside, and read the parking machine's rules (many are app-only and pay-by-the-hour). And before you fly a drone anywhere photogenic, check that it is allowed; the famous viewpoints are often the ones where it is not.

*You will see this pattern in: the fake apartment and Airbnb deposit-wire scams (Vienna), the fake holiday-apartment listing in Bad Gastein, the drive-past-the-barrier and lost-ticket parking fines and the drone-photo fines (Hallstatt), the Dürnstein parking-maze app-only trap (Wachau), and the Blue Zone parking fine (Graz).*
