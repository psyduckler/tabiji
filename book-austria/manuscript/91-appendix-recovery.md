# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are ordered by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed, do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. A signed card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, this is a crime under Austrian law (*Nötigung*, coercion, or *Freiheitsentziehung*, unlawful detention), and the correct response is to dial **133** (police) or **112** and stay on the line. Austrian emergency operators handle English routinely.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection, the business details (Austrian businesses display a *Firmenbuch* number and address), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward. If a pickpocket team is still in the area, walk 100 meters in any direction before you pull out your phone.

If your bag or wallet was just stolen, shout *"Hilfe! Dieb!"* — "Help! Thief!" — and look for the nearest official. In a transit crush like Jakominiplatz in Graz or the U1 in Vienna, move immediately to the platform staff or a uniformed officer.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest police station (*Polizeiinspektion* or *Polizeiwache*). Most tourist districts have one within a short walk, every major railway station has a police post on site, and officers in tourist cities routinely deal with English-speaking visitors. Say *"Ich wurde betrogen"* ("I was scammed") or *"Ich wurde bestohlen"* ("I was robbed"). Ask to file an **Anzeige** — the formal crime report. You will need the Anzeige reference number for every subsequent step: the card chargeback, the travel-insurance claim, the embassy replacement passport. You do not need fluent German for this.

You can also report online via the Austrian police portal at **[bmi.gv.at](https://www.bmi.gv.at)** (look for *Online-Anzeige*) for theft and fraud, but for anything you need documented quickly, going to a station in person is usually faster.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from Austria and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call. If the scam was a restaurant overcharge or a taxi rip-off paid by card, also call your bank's fraud line directly — the number on the back of your card — because fraud desks sometimes freeze faster than the Visa or Mastercard emergency lines.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the Anzeige number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Under the European Payment Services Directive (PSD2), you have strong chargeback rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend credit over debit cards for every Austrian trip. This is also your route to clawing back a Dynamic Currency Conversion overcharge if you only noticed it after the fact.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can resurface in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. Most foreign embassies for Austria are in Vienna; contact details are in Appendix C.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the Anzeige number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position.

If the scam was an off-platform rental or a wire-transfer "deposit" — the Vienna and Bad Gastein apartment frauds in this book — open a dispute with your bank about the transfer immediately (a wire can occasionally be recalled if you act within hours), and report it to the platform if a cloned listing was involved. The sooner the bank receives a fraud report, the better the odds.

## Within the first week

File a complaint with Austria's consumer-protection bodies. The **VKI (Verein für Konsumenteninformation)** at **[konsument.at](https://www.konsument.at)** and the **Arbeiterkammer (AK)** at **[arbeiterkammer.at](https://www.arbeiterkammer.at)** both take consumer complaints and publish warnings; the AK in particular runs a consumer-advice line in each province. They do not recover individual losses directly, but they aggregate complaints that drive enforcement, and several of the overcharging patterns in this book (DCC ATMs, tourist-menu surprises, transfer overcharges) are exactly the kind of thing they track. For cross-border issues within the EU, the **European Consumer Centre Austria (Europäisches Verbraucherzentrum)** at **[europakonsument.at](https://europakonsument.at)** helps with disputes against businesses in other EU countries.

For a taxi overcharge, you can also complain to the city's licensing authority (in Vienna, the *Magistrat*; elsewhere, the *Bezirkshauptmannschaft* or municipal taxi office), quoting the cab's license number from the rear door or the receipt.

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business's name, the address, the exact transaction details, the Anzeige number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent visitors are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same trap. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name and location only.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of hospitals with English-speaking staff in each Austrian city is in Appendix C. At the hospital, ask specifically for a *"toxikologische Untersuchung"* (toxicology screen). Keep the hospital report; it is evidence.

In Austria, the ambulance number is **144** (or **112** for general emergencies, which will dispatch an ambulance). If you are inside a venue — for example, the "friendly nightlife lure" in Innsbruck — and drink-spiking is suspected, have a friend call for an ambulance *before* you leave, not after.

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, a replacement. Consular registration (STEP for US citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, and equivalents for other nationalities) is worth doing before you travel, specifically for situations like this.
