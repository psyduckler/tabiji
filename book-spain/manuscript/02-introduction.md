# How to Use This Book {-}

Spain is one of the most-visited countries on earth — and, statistically, the most pickpocket-prone major destination in Europe. Barcelona alone reports more than **140,000 pickpocket incidents a year** to the Mossos d'Esquadra, the overwhelming majority targeting foreign visitors on La Rambla, at Sagrada Família, in the Gothic Quarter, and on Line 3 of the metro toward Passeig de Gràcia. Madrid's Puerta del Sol and Gran Vía produce their own reliable stream of distraction-theft reports, and Seville's Plaza de España generates a steady trickle of *claveles* (rosemary-sprig) fortune-teller confrontations that end in a 20 € bill for an unsolicited "reading." The Spanish Interior Ministry publishes annual figures; the dedicated tourist-help service, **SATE** (*Servicio de Atención al Turista Extranjero*), logs tens of thousands of foreign-visitor complaints every year, most of them concentrated on a dozen neighborhoods and a handful of tourist landmarks.

We want you to come home with stories about the tapas, the Alhambra, the Camino, and the Mediterranean nights — not about a phone lifted on the Barcelona metro or a 400 € "damage deposit" on a scratched Ibiza scooter.

That is why this book is short and practical. It documents one hundred and three specific scams that trip up first-time visitors to Spain, and it tells you the exact moves that stop them. Nothing in it is theoretical. Every scam has been reported in the Spanish press, cross-referenced against Policía Nacional, Guardia Civil, Mossos d'Esquadra, or Ertzaintza records, and confirmed by recent traveler accounts on r/Spain, r/Barcelona, r/Madrid, r/travel, and the city-specific subreddits.

## How each scam is written

Every scam entry follows the same short format. First, the name of the scam, the category it falls into (pickpocket, transport, overcharging, tourist trap, and so on), and how serious and how common it is. Then two short sections: **How this scam works** — a plain narrative of how it unfolds in real time, with real euro amounts where we have them — and **How to avoid it**, the specific moves that shut it down. Each entry ends with where it happens and — for Spain — a four-panel comic showing the exchange so you can spot the pattern visually, not just in words.

## The single most important chapter

The next chapter, **The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns**, is the most important part of this book. Most scams in Spain are variations on six patterns. Learn the patterns and you will spot a new scam the moment it starts — even one that is not in this book. Spend ten quiet minutes on that chapter before your trip. The rest of the book will then read as practical examples of patterns you already understand.

## The three bonuses at the back

After the city chapters, you will find three appendices that we hope you treasure more than the city chapters themselves.

The first is the **Spanish exit-phrase card**. Short phrases you can say, word for word, at the moments when your politeness would normally get you into trouble. Spanish is forgiving — the pronunciation guides are approximate, but the key words will carry the meaning even with a foreign accent. Take a screenshot of it on your phone.

The second is the **recovery playbook**. A step-by-step flow of what to do in the first fifteen minutes, hour, day, and week after a scam. Most scam books only tell you how to avoid scams. If the worst happens, you want this.

The third is the **emergency contacts appendix**. Policía Nacional, Guardia Civil, Mossos d'Esquadra (Catalonia), Ertzaintza (Basque Country), ambulance, SATE tourist-help desks, embassies, and the nearest English-speaking medical care, organized so you can find what you need in seconds.

## A suggested way to use the book

A week or two before your trip, read the first two chapters and skim the three appendices. Read the chapters for the cities and islands you will be visiting. Take a screenshot of the phrase card and the emergency contacts on your phone. If anything in this book turns out to apply during your trip, open it again on the metro or in your hotel room and check what to do.

You will almost certainly not need most of it. That is the point.

*¡Hola, buena suerte!* — hello and good luck.
