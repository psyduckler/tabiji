# Appendix A: Spanish Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Spain. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, followed by the **Spanish** text and a simple pronunciation guide in italics.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. Say the first three or four out loud a few times. You will feel much less awkward using them when the moment comes.

## A note on pronunciation

Spanish is forgiving. Unlike Thai or Mandarin it is not tonal, and unlike French it has almost no silent letters. Roughly speaking, *every letter is pronounced the way it is spelled*, consonants are hard, and stress falls either on the second-to-last syllable (*sevilla* — seh-**VEE**-ya) or on the syllable marked with an accent (*Málaga* — **MAH**-la-ga). If you lean into the rhythm and pronounce the vowels cleanly, a Spanish listener will understand you even with a strong foreign accent.

A few pointers that will get you 80 percent of the way:

- Spanish vowels are clean and short, and each is always pronounced the same way: *a* is "ah," *e* is "eh," *i* is "ee," *o* is "oh," *u* is "oo." Never "uh." Never a schwa.
- The letter *h* is silent — *hola* is "OH-la," never "HO-la."
- The letter *j* (and *g* before *e* or *i*) is a throaty English "h" — *jamón* is "ha-MOHN," *Gijón* is "hee-HOHN."
- The letter *ñ* is "ny" — *mañana* is "ma-NYAH-na."
- The letters *ll* are roughly "y" in most of Spain — *llamo* is "YAH-moh," *paella* is "pa-EH-ya."
- In peninsular (mainland Spanish) Spanish, the *c* before *e* or *i* and the *z* are lisped as a soft "th" — *cinco* ("THEEN-koh"), *Barcelona* ("bar-theh-LOH-na"). This is the **ceceo** and is the standard in Madrid and most of Spain except Andalucía and the Canaries, where speakers use a softer "s" sound (the **seseo**). Either is understood everywhere.

## A note on politeness

Spanish is the most politeness-neutral of the major European tourist languages. You do not need to append a politeness particle to every sentence the way Japanese or Thai demand. But *por favor* ("please") and *gracias* ("thank you") at the start or end of a sentence make any refusal more comfortable, and a soft tone carries authority the way a raised voice does not.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**  
¿Cuánto es?  
*KWAN-toh ess?*

**How much does it cost?**  
¿Cuánto cuesta?  
*KWAN-toh KWES-tah?*

**Please turn on the meter.**  
El taxímetro, por favor.  
*El tahk-SEE-meh-troh, por fah-VOR.*

Say this the instant you get into a taxi. If the driver refuses, get out. Every licensed Spanish taxi has a meter and is legally required to use it.

**Can I see the menu, please?**  
¿Me enseña la carta, por favor?  
*Meh en-SEH-nya la KAR-ta, por fah-VOR?*

In Spain, "menu" is *carta* — the full list of dishes with prices. The *menú del día* is the fixed-price lunch special (a separate menu). Ask for the *carta* unless you specifically want the lunch deal.

**Is this the terrace price or the indoor price?**  
¿Este es el precio de la terraza o del interior?  
*ES-teh ess el PREH-thyoh deh la teh-RAH-tha oh del een-teh-RYOR?*

A critical Spain-specific question. Many cafés on tourist strips charge 30–50% more for terrace seating. The menu you read at the door may be the indoor price only.

**What is the price per 100 grams?**  
¿Cuál es el precio por cien gramos?  
*Kwal ess el PREH-thyoh por thyen GRAH-mohs?*

Fish and shellfish are priced by weight (*por 100 gramos*) in Spain. Confirm the per-100g price *and* the expected total before the fish goes in the pan.

**Do you accept credit cards?**  
¿Aceptan tarjetas de crédito?  
*Ah-THEP-tahn tar-HEH-tahs deh KREH-dee-toh?*

Card acceptance is near-universal in cities; cash-only is more common in small villages. If the answer is "yes" but the reader then breaks, you are being scammed.

**Is this per person or per table?**  
¿Es por persona o por mesa?  
*Ess por per-SOH-na oh por MEH-sa?*

For sangría pitchers, tasting menus, and set-menu dining.

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**  
No, gracias.  
*Noh, GRAH-thyas.*

The single most useful phrase in this appendix. Said firmly, without slowing your pace. Walking away is not rude; it is Spanish-normal.

**I'm not interested.**  
No me interesa.  
*Noh meh een-teh-REH-sah.*

Stronger than *no, gracias*. Use this when a tout, a clipboard-carrier, or a *clavel*-offering woman keeps talking after the first refusal.

**Please leave me alone.**  
Déjeme en paz, por favor.  
*DEH-heh-meh en path, por fah-VOR.*

Literally "leave me in peace, please." Used firmly but without raised voice.

**Don't touch me.**  
No me toque.  
*Noh meh TOH-keh.*

Say this clearly and step back. Most touts will disengage immediately rather than escalate.

**Excuse me, please let me through.**  
Perdón, déjeme pasar.  
*Per-DOHN, DEH-heh-meh pah-SAR.*

For clearing a path when a pickpocket team is crowding you on the metro or in La Rambla traffic.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I'm calling the police.**  
Voy a llamar a la policía.  
*Boy ah ya-MAR ah la poh-lee-THEE-ah.*

This is the firmest card in the deck. The Policía Nacional emergency number is **091**; **112** reaches the pan-European emergency line with English-speaking call-takers 24/7. Say the phrase, hold up your phone, and start dialing. Most scam operators back down the moment they hear *policía*.

**I'll call my bank.**  
Voy a llamar a mi banco.  
*Boy ah ya-MAR ah mee BAHN-koh.*

Say this if a restaurant, taxi driver, or jet-ski operator disputes a credit-card transaction. Calling your card issuer from the table signals that the charge is going to be disputed.

**I was scammed. Please help.**  
Me han estafado. Ayúdeme, por favor.  
*Meh ahn es-tah-FAH-doh. Ah-YOO-deh-meh, por fah-VOR.*

The word *estafado* is the specific Spanish word for "scammed" — it communicates what happened with more urgency than any English translation. Use this when speaking to a Policía Nacional officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, a fellow traveler, or any bystander you want to enlist.

**Please give me an itemized receipt.**  
La cuenta detallada, por favor.  
*La KWEN-ta deh-tah-YAH-da, por fah-VOR.*

In Spain, asking for *la cuenta detallada* (literally "detailed bill") forces the establishment to account for every item, and it is the most polite version of "I do not accept this charge." A legitimate place will hand it to you without complaint. A tourist trap will stall.

**I didn't order this.**  
Yo no he pedido esto.  
*Yoh noh eh peh-DEE-doh ES-toh.*

Say this calmly about any unrequested dish, bottle, or "service" that appears on your bill. In Spain you are not legally required to pay for food or drinks you did not order.

**This is not what we agreed.**  
Esto no es lo que acordamos.  
*ES-toh noh ess loh keh ah-kor-DAH-mohs.*

A short, firm phrase for taxi fares that exceed a quoted price, jet-ski "damage" claims, and tour-operator price-creep.

## Getting help

**Where's the nearest police station?**  
¿Dónde está la comisaría más cercana?  
*DOHN-deh es-TAH la koh-mee-sa-REE-ah mahs ther-KAH-na?*

"Comisaría" is the Policía Nacional station. In Catalonia ask for a "*comisaria dels Mossos*," and in the Basque Country for a "*comisaría de la Ertzaintza*" — both phrases are understood.

**I'd like to report a crime.**  
Quiero denunciar un delito.  
*KYEH-roh deh-noon-THYAR oon deh-LEE-toh.*

The word *denunciar* is the specific Spanish verb for filing a criminal report. The report itself is a *denuncia*. You will walk out of the station with a printed, stamped *denuncia* — this is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

**Is there someone who speaks English?**  
¿Hay alguien que hable inglés?  
*Eye AHL-gyen keh AH-bleh een-GLESS?*

Every Policía Nacional comisaría in a major Spanish city has at least one English-speaking officer on duty or a translator on call; the SATE (tourist-help) desks are English-by-default.

**Please call 112.**  
Llame al 112, por favor.  
*YAH-meh al ooh-noh-ooh-noh-DOHS, por fah-VOR.*

The pan-European emergency line **112** works on every mobile phone in Spain — even without a SIM, even on a locked screen. Dispatchers speak English. For Policía Nacional specifically, dial **091**. For Guardia Civil, **062**. For ambulance, **061**.

**Help! Thief!**  
¡Socorro! ¡Al ladrón!  
*Soh-KOH-rroh! Al lah-DROHN!*

Shouted in a crowded market or street, this is understood by every bystander as "someone stole from me, help me stop them." The word *ladrón* (thief) carries immediate legal weight in Spain.

**Please help me.**  
Ayúdeme, por favor.  
*Ah-YOO-deh-meh, por fah-VOR.*

## Reading Spanish euro amounts

Spanish euro prices are written with the symbol **€** either before or after the number. Decimal conventions are European — a comma is the decimal separator (3,50 € = three euros fifty cents) and a dot or space is the thousands separator (1.500 € or 1 500 €). This is the opposite of English conventions.

A few helpful markers:

- Small-denomination coins: 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 cents; 1 €; 2 €.
- Paper notes: 5 €, 10 €, 20 €, 50 €, 100 €, 200 €, 500 € (the 500 is no longer printed but remains legal tender).
- The US dollar and the euro trade near parity and drift a few percent in either direction over the course of a year — verify on xe.com in the week before you travel.

| Amount | Written | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 euro | un euro | *oon eh-OO-roh* |
| 5 euros | cinco euros | *THEEN-koh eh-OO-rohs* |
| 10 euros | diez euros | *dyeth eh-OO-rohs* |
| 20 euros | veinte euros | *BEN-teh eh-OO-rohs* |
| 50 euros | cincuenta euros | *theen-KWEN-ta eh-OO-rohs* |
| 100 euros | cien euros | *thyen eh-OO-rohs* |
| 500 euros | quinientos euros | *kee-NYEN-tohs eh-OO-rohs* |
| 1.000 euros | mil euros | *meel eh-OO-rohs* |

If a vendor quotes a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it out:

**Please write the number down.**  
¿Me lo puede escribir, por favor?  
*Meh loh PWEH-deh es-kree-BEER, por fah-VOR?*

Almost every vendor will oblige, often on the back of a receipt or a paper napkin.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Spain, polite firmness works better than raised voices — *gritar* (shouting) is read as losing your composure and rarely helps your case. A calm *no, gracias* is already a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *déjeme en paz* ("leave me in peace") and *no me toque* ("don't touch me") are both firm without being rude, and *voy a llamar a la policía* ("I'm calling the police") is the firmest card in the deck.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar — and a calm *no, gracias* with a *por favor* at the end is, in itself, proof you know enough Spanish that the interaction is going to be harder than they expected.
