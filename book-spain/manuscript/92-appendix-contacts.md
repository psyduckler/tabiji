# Appendix C: Emergency Contacts {-}

Every phone number in this appendix was verified current at the time of publication, but phone numbers do change, consulates move, and hospitals occasionally renumber their switchboards. If a number below fails to connect, search the organization's name plus *España* or the city name, and you will almost always find the current line on the first page of results.

**Before you travel**, we strongly recommend you double-check your own embassy or consulate's current number directly on its official website — the US State Department's travel.state.gov, the UK's gov.uk, Canada's travel.gc.ca, Ireland's dfa.ie, Australia's smartraveller.gov.au. Save the main switchboard and the after-hours duty-officer line on your phone before departure, and screenshot this appendix so you can read it on the metro or a ferry where your signal cuts out. Even a verified number can become a wrong number between editions.

## The numbers that work everywhere in Spain

These are the lines to dial from any phone — mobile or landline, Spanish SIM or roaming — anywhere in the country. All of them are free.

- **Pan-European emergency (all services):** **112**. Dispatchers speak English, French, German, and Spanish 24/7. This is the single number to call if you are not sure which service you need; the operator will route you to police, ambulance, or fire as appropriate. 112 works on any mobile phone in Spain, even without a SIM and even on a locked screen.
- **Policía Nacional (national police):** **091**. The main national police force, responsible for most crime reports in cities. For foreign-visitor issues, ask for the **SATE** (*Servicio de Atención al Turista Extranjero*) — specialized tourist-help desks with English-speaking officers, in every major tourist city.
- **Guardia Civil (rural and highway police):** **062**. Covers the countryside, highways, airports' exterior zones, and smaller towns. Also handles border crossings and parts of the Canary Islands and Balearics.
- **Policía Local / Policía Municipal:** Number varies by city; **092** is the most common municipal line. Handles municipal-ordinance violations, local traffic issues, and some tourist-area patrols.
- **Regional forces (where they operate):** **Mossos d'Esquadra (Catalonia)** dial **112** (the preferred 24/7 line; the legacy 088 short code still routes in most carriers but Mossos have migrated to 112); **Ertzaintza (Basque Country)** dial **112** (legacy 094 likewise consolidated onto 112); **Policía Foral de Navarra** dial **948 429 600**; **Policía Canaria** coordinates with Policía Nacional.
- **Ambulance / medical emergency:** **061** or **112**. Both reach the same dispatch. English-speaking operators on the line.
- **Fire:** **080** or **112**.
- **Consumer Affairs complaint (nationwide entry point):** dial **010** within most large Spanish cities for municipal consumer info; **Consumo autonómico** (regional Consumer Affairs) addresses are published at each autonomous community's official site.
- **Spanish tax residency, SIM registration, and banking-fraud questions** route through the **Banco de España Departamento de Conducta de Entidades** complaint line at **900 545 454**.

When you call, have your card number (or the last four digits), the approximate transaction amount, and the time of the fraudulent charge ready.

- **Visa Global Customer Assistance (collect from Spain):** +1 303 967 1096. 24/7, English. Visa also maintains a Spain toll-free line at 900 991 124.
- **Mastercard Global Service (collect from Spain):** +1 636 722 7111. Spain toll-free: 900 971 231.
- **American Express International Service Center (collect):** +1 336 393 1111. Spain toll-free (for Amex-Spain issued cards): 902 375 637.

That line routes you to your own issuer's fraud desk and they can freeze the card in under a minute. Save this number in your phone under a searchable contact name before you travel — hunting for a number on a stolen card is impossible.

## If you lose your passport

1. **Report it first** — go to the nearest Policía Nacional station and file a *denuncia* for a lost or stolen passport. You will leave with the signed and sealed report. This is the document your embassy wants to see before issuing an emergency replacement. The pre-filing portal at **denuncias.policia.es** accelerates the process.
2. **Call your embassy's main switchboard** — or its after-hours line if it is a weekend — and request an emergency passport appointment. US, UK, Canadian, Irish, and Australian embassies in Madrid (and consulates in Barcelona and elsewhere) all issue same-day or next-day emergency travel documents; the fee varies.
3. **Bring** the *denuncia*, any photo ID you still have (a driver's license photo on your phone counts), passport photos (photo booths are common in metro stations and in the basement of El Corte Inglés department stores), and the payment method your embassy specifies.
4. **Border-control clearance for departure**: before flying out with an emergency travel document, airlines departing Madrid-Barajas, Barcelona-El Prat, Málaga, Palma, Alicante, and the Canary Islands see emergency documents regularly; Schengen border police know the format. For non-Schengen destinations, your embassy will advise on any additional step.

## If your phone is stolen

1. **Freeze the SIM** by calling your carrier's customer-service line from any other phone. Spanish carriers: **Movistar** 1004 (in Spain) or +34 913 392 211 (from abroad); **Vodafone** 22123 or +34 607 123 000 (from abroad); **Orange** 1414; **Yoigo** 622. If you use a foreign SIM, call your home carrier's international line — most have a 24/7 theft-reporting number that forwards from Spain. Tourist eSIMs (Airalo, Saily, Holafly) can be suspended in-app.
2. **File a *denuncia*** at the nearest Policía Nacional station with your device's IMEI (if you recorded it) and its serial number. Insurance claims require the sealed report. The online pre-filing at denuncias.policia.es accelerates this.
3. **Lock and wipe remotely** via Apple's Find My or Google's Find My Device from any browser. Do this before filing the *denuncia* — a wiped phone cannot leak your email and banking apps while you wait in line.
4. **Change passwords immediately** for any account whose app was installed on the phone: email, banking, social media. Start with email; everything else resets off it.

## If your credit card is stolen or skimmed

1. **Freeze the card** by calling the number on the back (or use the issuer's app's "lock card" feature, which works in seconds). This matters more than filing a police report first — every minute you wait is a minute the thief can use the card.
2. **Report to police** with a *denuncia*. Insurance reimbursement and the card issuer's fraud-dispute process both want the sealed report.
3. **Dispute the charges** with your issuer. Most European, US, Australian, and Japanese card issuers reverse fraudulent charges fully; the *denuncia* speeds the process.
4. **Watch for follow-on phishing** — a skimmed card often leads to email and SMS phishing pretending to be your bank. Never click a link in one of these messages; go to your bank's app or website directly.

## A closing note

This appendix is meant to be read once on the plane, screenshot for offline use, and forgotten about for the rest of your trip. Phone numbers change. Consulates move. Hospitals renumber their switchboards. If any number in this list fails, **112** — the pan-European emergency line — will still work, and your embassy's website will still have the current line for your country.

Travel safely, travel calmly, and if the worst happens, remember: a stolen phone is an inconvenience, not a crisis. Filing a *denuncia*, freezing a card, and calling an embassy are ordinary procedures — Policía Nacional officers and consular staff in Spain handle these cases every day, and most travelers resolve everything within a few hours and return to their trip. Spain is not a dangerous country; it is a practiced one. Help is close, and English is closer than you think.
