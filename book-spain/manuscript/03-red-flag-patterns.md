# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The one hundred and three scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: A stranger who approaches you first is almost certainly running a script

This is the single most important habit you can take to Spain. The instant someone steps into your path uninvited near Sagrada Família, the Royal Palace, Plaza de España, the Alhambra ticket office, or a Barcelona metro turnstile, the interaction that follows is a rehearsed script. You are not being greeted. You are being sorted.

Spain's scam ecosystem is unusually warm, and the openers almost always sound friendly or charitable. On Las Ramblas a stranger suddenly points to a (nonexistent) stain on your jacket and, while "helping" to wipe it, lifts your phone from your bag. Outside Sagrada Família a woman offers a sprig of rosemary "for good luck" — an unsolicited *clavel* that transitions into a forced palm-reading and a 20 € demand. At the Plaza Mayor in Madrid a man in a sports jersey produces a clipboard for a "deaf-and-mute petition"; while you're reading, his partner is inside your back pocket. At Puerta del Sol the classic three-card *trile* game is set up by four to five people working in a tight cluster — the player, the tout, and the spotters who will crowd you if you win so you can be pickpocketed on the way out. In Granada a woman approaches at the Mirador de San Nicolás viewpoint offering to read your palm; the 5 € "gift" becomes 50 € by the end of the reading.

None of these interactions start with you asking for help. That is the tell. Spaniards are famously warm, but they do not generally accost foreign tourists on the street to volunteer flowers, petitions, palm readings, or card-game invitations.

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: when any stranger starts a conversation with you near a famous site or on the metro, reply "*no, gracias*" — firmly — and keep walking. Do not break stride. Do not engage. If the person follows you, repeat the phrase once, louder, and step into the nearest shop or café. Spanish politeness conventions do not require you to listen to a sales pitch, and walking away is not rude.

*You will see this pattern in: the La Rambla pickpocket gangs (Barcelona), the Sagrada Família rosemary scam, the deaf-and-mute petition (Madrid, Barcelona, Seville), the Puerta del Sol three-card monte, the Alhambra fake-guide approach (Granada), the Mirador de San Nicolás palm-reading (Granada), the Plaza de España _claveles_ (Seville), and almost every approach scene in the 103 scam entries that follow.*

## Pattern 2: "The meter is broken" means you are being overcharged

The second pattern is taxis. Spain has strict taxi regulation — meters are mandatory in every licensed cab, and two dedicated ride-hail apps (Cabify, Free Now) publish fares up front. But a meaningful share of tourist rides begin with the driver refusing the meter or declining the app and quoting a flat price — always two to four times what the meter would charge.

In Madrid, the flagfall from Barajas Airport is a flat 33 € into central Madrid (a published, regulated rate that drivers must honor). Any "negotiation" above 33 € from Terminal 1, 2, or 4 to anywhere inside the M-30 ring is an overcharge. In Barcelona, the flagfall from El Prat Airport is roughly 30–40 € metered; the freelance-tout price is 80 €. At Atocha, Sants, and Santa Justa stations, freelance drivers outside the official rank quote flat fares two to three times the regulated price. On Mallorca and Ibiza the airport-taxi rank is metered and cheap — but a small number of drivers "forget" to start the meter and collect a flat fare in cash.

The "meter is broken" phrase — in Spanish, "*el taxímetro está roto*" — is the specific tell. Spanish meters are not broken. Refusing to use the meter is refusing to charge the legal price.

The defense is short and universal. Take a taxi only from the official rank, where an inspector is often present. Before the car pulls away, say calmly: "*Taxímetro, por favor*" — "meter, please." If the driver refuses, get out and take the next cab. If you are at an airport, the published flat airport fare is on a printed card at the rank and at the official website of the municipal taxi authority; insist on that. For predictable, up-front pricing use Cabify or Free Now — both apps quote the fare before you book and the payment runs through the app, so there is no "meter-is-broken" conversation on arrival. If a driver runs a tourist route (detouring through the Plaza de España just to add 3 km to a 2-km fare), use Google Maps in real time from the back seat and ask calmly why the route is not matching.

*You will see this pattern in: the Barajas Airport flat-rate quote (Madrid), the El Prat freelance taxi tout (Barcelona), the Atocha station detour, the Palma Airport "forgotten" meter, the Ibiza sunset-bar overcharge, the Málaga Airport off-rank pickup, and the Seville Santa Justa "special flat rate" pitch.*

## Pattern 3: Prices that aren't posted will surprise you later

The third pattern is the unposted price. Spanish restaurants along La Rambla, on tourist promenades in Marbella, Ibiza, and Mallorca, and at beach-chair rentals on the Costa del Sol and the Canaries will sometimes — not always — quote you a price that is deliberately vague. "Precio especial." "Menú del día." "Muy buen precio." "Ya hablamos al final." Each of these phrases hides a later surprise.

This pattern accounts for a meaningful share of the overcharging scams in this book. On La Rambla, cafés charge a visible indoor price and a separate — often triple — terrace price that isn't on the menu. On Barceloneta, beach-front seafood restaurants price fish *per 100 grams* and the size of the fish is not confirmed out loud; a "small" lubina arrives at 1.4 kg and 140 €. On the Costa del Sol, beach-chair vendors offer "10 € per chair" — then bill 40 € at sunset because the rate was actually 10 € per chair per hour. On Mallorca and Ibiza, sangría by the pitcher is sometimes priced "per person" in a footnote; four friends think they are splitting one pitcher and end up paying for four. On the Gran Canaria sunset-cruise pitch, "champagne included" in fact means a 12 € supplement per glass.

The defense is a single habit, repeated before every transaction: ask for the price in writing, or point to a posted menu. For fish by weight, ask to see the scale and get the total in euros confirmed before it is cooked. For taxis, confirm the meter is on and the fare display is facing you. For beach chairs, jet skis, and parasols, confirm the per-hour rate and the return time, and photograph the equipment from every angle before you use it — date and time visible on the phone's clock. Photos are your contract.

*You will see this pattern in: the La Rambla terrace-price surprise (Barcelona), the Barceloneta seafood per-gram trap, the Costa del Sol beach-chair escalation, the Ibiza sunset-bar cover charge, the Mallorca sangría "per person," the Gran Canaria catamaran champagne supplement, and the Valencia paella-for-two that arrives priced as four.*

## Pattern 4: The "scratch on the scooter," the "damage deposit," the "broken snorkel mask"

The fourth pattern is the damage claim. You rent something — a moped, a jet ski, a snorkel set, a beach umbrella, a rental car — and when you return it, the operator points at an imaginary or pre-existing scratch and demands hundreds of euros for "damage."

Ibiza, Mallorca, Gran Canaria, and Tenerife are the epicenters of the scooter-scratch variant. The rental shop takes your passport or a 400 € cash deposit, points at a hairline scratch near the mirror that was already there, and tells you it will cost 300 € to fix — or you can buy "insurance" for 80 €. The jet-ski variant on Playa de Palma and Playa del Inglés has the same structure scaled up: 1,500 € for a scratch that the next tourist will also be blamed for. Car-rental shops at Málaga and Alicante airports run an "extra fuel surcharge" at return where the tank-gauge is said to read 7/8 instead of full even when you filled up two minutes away. On Lanzarote, buggy-rental shops in Playa Blanca use the "underneath-damage" gambit: they crawl under the vehicle at return and produce a dent that nobody saw at pick-up.

The defense is photographic, and it is mandatory. Before you accept the rental, take six to ten date-stamped photos of the item from every angle: front, back, left, right, the specific areas where scratches typically appear, the underside (for buggies and cars), and a close-up of the odometer or fuel gauge. Hand over a photocopy of your passport identification page rather than the real passport; keep the real passport in the hotel safe. For rental cars, photograph the fuel gauge at pick-up and at return, and keep the final gas-station receipt. Pay every rental with a credit card, never a debit card — only a credit card gives you chargeback rights if a fraudulent "damage" charge appears. If a damage claim is made at return, produce the photos on the spot. If the operator still refuses to return the passport or deposit, call **091** for Policía Nacional (or **062** for Guardia Civil on the islands) and wait for them to arrive.

*You will see this pattern in: the Ibiza scooter-scratch scam, the Playa de Palma jet-ski damage demand, the Lanzarote buggy-underside gambit, the Gran Canaria parasol deposit-hold, the Málaga Airport rental-car fuel-surcharge, the Tenerife moped-insurance upsell, and the Mallorca diving-mask "you broke it" script.*

## Pattern 5: An "official" is an official only at an official office

The fifth pattern is the stranger in authority. Real Spanish police — Policía Nacional in navy blue with yellow bands, Guardia Civil in green with the distinctive tricorne hat, Mossos d'Esquadra (Catalonia) in navy with red trim, Ertzaintza (Basque Country) in dark-red uniforms, and Policía Local in various municipal liveries — operate only from official stations, checkpoints, and their marked patrol cars. They do not demand to see the cash in your wallet on the street. They do not ask for a "fine" payable in cash on the spot. They do not conduct drug checks on sidewalks in tourist areas.

The *falsos policías* pattern runs across Spanish cities in two forms. The first, documented repeatedly by Policía Nacional Madrid, is the Gran Vía "plainclothes check": two men in polo shirts flash unofficial laminated cards, claim they are checking for counterfeit currency after a nearby bank report, and ask to "see" the bills in your wallet — at which point they lift the notes during the examination. The second, concentrated in Barcelona's Gothic Quarter and around Sagrada Família, is the fake traffic-stop or fake-ID check: a man with a high-visibility vest tells tourists their passport copy "isn't valid" and a fine must be paid to avoid a problem with Immigration. Real Spanish Immigration does not do this. Real arrests happen at stations, not on sidewalks.

The defense is simple and universal. If anyone on the street identifies themselves as an official and wants to examine your wallet, passport, pocket contents, or phone, refuse and walk into the nearest well-lit shop or hotel lobby. Say calmly: "Let's go to the comisaría" — "*Vamos a la comisaría*." Dial **091** (Policía Nacional) or **112** (pan-European emergency) from your phone and put the call on speaker if the person has not already left. Real officers have no problem with this. Fake ones leave immediately. For tourist-specific support, major Spanish cities operate a **SATE** — *Servicio de Atención al Turista Extranjero* — with English-speaking officers; the addresses and numbers are in the appendix.

*You will see this pattern in: the Gran Vía fake-plainclothes scam (Madrid), the Gothic Quarter fake-ID check (Barcelona), the Puerta del Sol fake-officer approach, the Plaza de España "identification please" script (Seville), the Ibiza Dalt Vila fake-inspector gambit, and the general _falsos policías_ pattern across every major tourist city.*

## Pattern 6: If you pay before you have the thing, you probably won't get the thing

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch by reading the fine print after you have committed — so this pattern is really about what to pin down before you click *Confirm* or hand over cash.

You book an Alhambra tour online, pay full price by bank transfer, and the "operator" turns out to be a Facebook page with stolen photos; on arrival, there is no ticket and no tour. You book a Barcelona apartment via a slick-looking website and arrive to find the building doesn't exist — the listing was a duplicate photo set from a legitimate flat two blocks over. You hand 200 € to a street agent on Plaza Catalunya for a "skip-the-line" Sagrada Família ticket that was never purchased. You agree to an "all-inclusive" Ibiza beach-club package that excluded transport, a bottle minimum, the locker fee, and the boat home once you arrived. You pay a deposit to a Lanzarote dive shop for a PADI Open Water certification, but the card never arrives because the instructor was not certified.

All of these work because you committed before you knew the terms — and then the terms, the vendor, the venue, or the platform were not what you thought they were.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction you make in Spain. Book accommodation only through platforms that offer pay-through-platform guarantees and dispute resolution (Booking.com, Airbnb, Agoda, Expedia) — never by Facebook, WhatsApp, or direct wire transfer to a private IBAN. Verify any property on Google Street View before you depart — a legitimate Spanish apartment has visible signage and appears on at least one established booking platform. Pay every rental, excursion, or hotel by credit card (Visa, Mastercard, American Express) rather than debit, cash, or SEPA transfer — only a credit card gives you a chargeback right, and you can reverse a fraudulent charge within thirty days. For Alhambra tickets, book only through **tickets.alhambra-patronato.es** (the official Patronato site); anything else is a reseller. For Sagrada Família, book only through **sagradafamilia.org**. For PADI certifications, book only with shops listed on padi.com. For Balearic and Canary Islands ferries, buy at the actual pier (Balearia, Trasmediterranea, Fred. Olsen Express) or through their official websites. For high-speed trains, use renfe.com or Omio — never a third-party agent at the station.

### One more: the "skip-the-line ticket" and fake-tour-guide scams

Spain has its own set of ticketing traps. Private "skip-the-line" resellers charging 80 € for a ticket that the monument's own website sells for 25 €. Fake tour guides at the Alhambra, Sagrada Família, and the Prado offering "private tours" that include no actual ticket, or tickets for a different day. Official entry is always through the monument's own website, clearly branded, with a named Patronato or foundation behind it.

*You will see this pattern in: the Alhambra fake-ticket reseller (Granada), the Sagrada Família skip-the-line scam (Barcelona), the Prado "private guide" approach (Madrid), the Ibiza club-package hidden fees, the Mallorca ghost-apartment OTA listing, the Lanzarote fake-dive-certification trap, and the all-platform "wire a deposit" fraud that hits every Spanish destination.*
