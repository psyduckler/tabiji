# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are organized by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — a jewelry shop, a cellar tour, a bar with a suddenly inflated bill, a taxi that has detoured to a "friend's" showroom — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. A signed credit-card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, dial **091** (Policía Nacional), **112** (pan-European emergency, English by default), or the regional force — **088** for Mossos d'Esquadra in Catalonia, **094** for Ertzaintza in the Basque Country. For medical emergencies, **061** reaches the national ambulance dispatch.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection (Google Maps dropped-pin is ideal), the shop's business-license certificate (which Spanish law requires every retail establishment to display), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward. If a pickpocket team is still in the area, walk 100 meters in any direction before you pull out your phone to screenshot.

If your bag or wallet was just stolen in the street, shout — Spanish shopkeepers and passers-by will often help, and a visible commotion is the fastest way to draw attention. If you are in a Madrid, Barcelona, or Valencia metro station, the *jefe de estación* (station supervisor) office is the closest authority; station staff can call police and hold security-camera footage for you.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest *comisaría* — the Policía Nacional station — if one is within ten minutes; otherwise, to the nearest Mossos / Ertzaintza / Policía Local station. Every major Spanish city has at least one tourist-focused SATE (*Servicio de Atención al Turista Extranjero*) office — Madrid's is at C/ Leganitos 19 in Plaza de España, Barcelona's is at C/ Nou de la Rambla 76–78, Seville's is at Avenida del Cid 24. Say *"Quiero poner una denuncia"* ("I'd like to file a report"). You will walk out with a stamped *denuncia* (police report) that includes a reference number. This is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

Spain also operates a nationwide **online pre-filing system** — the *denuncia telemática* at **denuncias.policia.es** — which lets you pre-fill the report online, receive an appointment, and walk into the comisaría with less paperwork to do in person. Pre-filings must still be signed in-person to become valid *denuncias*, but they speed up the process considerably, especially at busy stations on summer weekends.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from Spain and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call. If the charge was a restaurant overcharge, a gem-shop "deposit," or a scooter-rental advance, also call your bank's fraud line directly — the number on the back of your card — because fraud desks sometimes freeze faster than the Visa or Mastercard emergency lines. If the card is Spanish-issued, the major banks (Santander, BBVA, CaixaBank, Sabadell, Bankinter) all run 24/7 card-loss lines.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the *denuncia* reference number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Visa and Mastercard both offer strong chargeback rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend credit over debit for every Spain trip.

For tourism-specific complaints against a registered business (a hotel that charged a phantom "city tax," a restaurant that padded a bill, a tour operator that didn't deliver), file a *Hoja de Reclamaciones* — the Consumer Complaint Form. Every Spanish business is legally required to keep blank complaint forms on the premises and to produce them on request. Ask for it in Spanish: "*Quiero la hoja de reclamaciones.*" The business must fill in its side, you fill in yours, you keep one copy, and the Consumer Affairs office processes the complaint. Refusing to provide the form is itself a violation that regional Consumer Affairs offices will sanction.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. US citizens should contact the US Embassy in Madrid or the US Consulate General in Barcelona; UK, Canadian, Australian, Irish, French, and German citizens have consulates in Madrid, Barcelona, and most major cities — full list in Appendix C.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the *denuncia* reference number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position. The major dispatchers serving the European market — Allianz Global Assistance, AXA Partners, World Nomads, Chubb, Zurich — all operate 24/7 English-language lines for travelers and have local medical-provider networks throughout Spain. Spanish private hospitals (Quirónsalud, HM Hospitales, Ruber Internacional) typically bill your insurer directly once a *Guarantee of Payment* is in place; public hospitals treat everyone under the Spanish National Health Service for emergency cases and bill insurance afterward.

If the scam involved an off-platform rental or a wire-fraud hotel booking, open the dispute on the booking platform (Booking.com, Airbnb, Agoda, Expedia) as well as with your card issuer. Platforms can sometimes recover deposits the card company cannot, and Airbnb in particular has a dedicated Spain fraud team given how many bookings involve Spanish hosts.

## Within the first week

File a formal consumer-protection complaint with the relevant regional **Dirección General de Consumo** — Spain's consumer-affairs regulator, which operates at the level of each autonomous community. Madrid's is **Consumo Madrid**, Catalonia's is **Agència Catalana del Consum**, Andalucía's is **Consumo Andalucía**. All accept complaints in English and all publish online forms. Regulators do not recover individual losses but their regulatory actions against repeat-offender tour operators, rental shops, and hotels are one of the few effective pressures on the scam economy. Tourist-facing businesses know which complaints reach the regulator; a filed complaint sometimes produces a restitution offer that the shop refused to make on the day of the incident.

For overcharging by licensed taxis, file a parallel complaint with the **municipal taxi authority**. Madrid's is **Instituto de Taxi**, Barcelona's is **Institut Metropolità del Taxi**, Seville's, Valencia's, Bilbao's, and Palma's all have published complaint forms. With the license number (the "licencia de taxi" number on the rear door of every legal cab in Spain, always photograph it before getting out), the authority can identify the driver, issue fines, and sometimes produce compensation.

For banking and payment disputes — a charge your Spanish bank refuses to reverse, an ATM skim you consider the bank should cover, a disputed wire-transfer fraud — escalate to the **Banco de España Departamento de Conducta de Entidades** at **bde.es/reclamaciones**. The Banco de España is the financial-sector ombudsman and publishes a standard complaint form. For securities and investment-related fraud, the regulator is the **CNMV** (Comisión Nacional del Mercado de Valores).

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business's name, the address, the exact transaction details, the *denuncia* reference number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name and location only. Spanish defamation law (both civil and criminal) is strict — stick to verifiable facts and receipts.

## If your phone or SIM was stolen

Call your carrier immediately to suspend the line. The four major Spanish carriers — **Movistar** (dial **1004**), **Vodafone** (dial **22123**), **Orange** (dial **1414**), and **Yoigo** (dial **622**) — all operate 24/7 stolen-device lines. Suspending the line prevents the thief from using your number to receive SMS two-factor-authentication codes and from running up international-roaming charges. If you are on a tourist eSIM (Airalo, Saily, Holafly, etc.), the app will let you suspend the line in a few taps; do that before the *denuncia*.

File a *denuncia* at the nearest Policía Nacional station and include your phone's **IMEI** number (the fifteen-digit serial; find it on the original box, in your Apple or Google account, or by dialing \*#06# *before* the theft). The IMEI lets police flag the device and is required for any insurance claim. If your SIM was the target — for example, a SIM-swap scam to intercept your bank's one-time codes — contact your bank's fraud desk before anything else; SIM-swap fraud moves faster than theft recovery, and the first hour is decisive.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking, drug-testing, or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of 24/7 hospitals with English-speaking staff in each city is in Appendix C. At the hospital, ask for a toxicology screen; Spanish private hospitals such as Quirónsalud, HM Hospitales, and Ruber Internacional run these routinely and will print a report you can use as evidence. Spanish public hospitals (run under the National Health Service) will treat every emergency case without up-front payment and bill insurance or, for EU/EEA nationals with a European Health Insurance Card, cover treatment directly.

For drug-testing or drug-planting scams — where a stranger, a bar staffer, or occasionally someone in uniform claims to have "found" a substance in your possession — stay calm, do not resist, and dial **091** (Policía Nacional) or **112** before any money changes hands. Real narcotics cases in Spain go through official processes with written documentation, photographed evidence, and a *denuncia* you sign. Demands for cash to "make it go away" are the signature of a scam, not of legitimate enforcement.

In Spain, the medical-emergency number is **061** or **112**; both reach the same dispatch with English-speaking operators on the line. For fire, dial **080**. For any incident involving a foreign tourist, **112** is the most reliable fallback with English-speaking dispatchers. If you are inside a venue and drink-spiking is suspected, have a friend call for an ambulance *before* you leave, not after — sometimes the aggressive bill pressure escalates once the scammer realizes you are leaving.

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate a replacement. Consular registration — STEP for US citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, LOCATE for UK, ROCA for Canadians, and the Irish equivalent — is worth doing before you travel, specifically for situations like this.
