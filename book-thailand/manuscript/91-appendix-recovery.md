# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are organized by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — a gem shop, a tailor, a bar with a suddenly inflated bill, a tuk-tuk that has detoured to a “friend’s” showroom — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. A signed credit-card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, dial **1155** — the Tourist Police, staffed 24/7 with English-speaking dispatchers nationwide. If 1155 is slow to connect, **191** is the Royal Thai Police general emergency line; the first operator may speak Thai, so repeat *“Tourist Police, please”* or *“English, please”* and you will be routed. For medical emergencies, **1669** reaches the national EMS ambulance dispatch.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection (Google Maps dropped-pin is ideal), the shop’s business registration certificate (usually displayed on the wall and carrying a Department of Business Development seal), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward. If a pickpocket team is still in the area, walk 100 meters in any direction before you pull out your phone to screenshot.

If your bag or wallet was just stolen in the street, shout — shopkeepers and motorbike taxi drivers in Thailand will often help, and a visible commotion is the fastest way to draw attention. If you are at a BTS, MRT, or Airport Rail Link station, the station supervisor’s office is the closest authority; station staff can call police and hold security-camera footage for you.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest *Tourist Police office* if one is within ten minutes; otherwise, the nearest *Royal Thai Police station* (*sathanee tamruat*). Tourist Police offices exist in every major tourist district — Sukhumvit and Khao San Road in Bangkok, the old city moat in Chiang Mai, Patong and Phuket Town, Pattaya Beach Road, Ao Nang in Krabi, the main piers on Koh Samui and Koh Phangan. Say *“I want to file a report”* or use the Thai phrase *plaeng kwaam* (file a complaint — *แจ้งความ*). You will walk out with a *bai jaeng kwaam* (police report) printed with an official seal. This is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

There is no nationwide online pre-filing portal like France’s *pré-plainte en ligne*. In Thailand, the *bai jaeng kwaam* must be filed in person, and almost always on the same day as the incident — delayed reports raise suspicion and slow the paperwork. If you are on a tight schedule (a flight or an island transfer the same day), go to the police *first*, then to the airport or pier; dispatchers and travel insurers will ask why you waited if you do not.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from Thailand and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call. If the charge was a restaurant overcharge, a gem-shop “deposit,” or a tailor advance, also call your bank’s fraud line directly — the number on the back of your card — because fraud desks sometimes freeze faster than the Visa or Mastercard emergency lines. If the card is Thai-issued, the major banks (Bangkok Bank, Kasikornbank, SCB, Krungthai, Krungsri) all run 24/7 card-loss lines.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the *bai jaeng kwaam* reference number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Visa and Mastercard both offer strong chargeback rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend credit over debit for every Thailand trip.

Call **1672** — the Tourism Authority of Thailand (TAT) *Tourist Assistance* line — for advice on next steps if you are not sure how to escalate. TAT 1672 is not a fraud-report line and cannot initiate an investigation, but the dispatchers are trained on the common tourist scams (gem, tailor, jet-ski, tuk-tuk scenic tour, duplicate-terminal card skim) and will direct you to the correct Tourist Police office and tell you what additional evidence to preserve. For disputes with registered tour operators, hotels, or overpriced transport, the dedicated tourist-complaint hotline is **1584**, operated by the Department of Land Transport and widely used for overcharging complaints against licensed taxis and buses.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. US citizens should contact the US Embassy in Bangkok; the US Consulate General in Chiang Mai (Amity Hall) handles northern Thailand. UK, Canadian, Australian, and Irish citizens must contact the Bangkok embassy directly — there are no regional consulates for those countries in Thailand.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the *bai jaeng kwaam* reference number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position. The major dispatchers serving the Asia-Pacific market — Allianz Global Assistance, AXA Partners, World Nomads, Chubb, Zurich — all operate 24/7 English-language lines for travelers and have local medical-provider networks throughout Thailand. Thai private hospitals will typically bill your insurer directly once a *Guarantee of Payment* is in place; public hospitals usually require you to pay up front and reclaim later.

If the scam involved an off-platform rental or a wire-fraud hotel booking, open the dispute on the booking platform (Agoda, Booking.com, Airbnb, Hotels.com) as well as with your card issuer. Platforms can sometimes recover deposits the card company cannot, and Agoda in particular has a dedicated Thailand fraud team given how many bookings on the platform involve Thai hotels.

## Within the first week

File a formal consumer-protection complaint with the **Office of the Consumer Protection Board (OCPB)** — *Samnak-ngan Khana Kammakan Khum-khrong Phu-boriphok* — via its hotline **1166** or its website **ocpb.go.th**. OCPB is the Thai analog of France’s DGCCRF or the UK’s Trading Standards. It does not recover individual losses, but its regulatory actions against repeat-offender gem shops, tailors, and unlicensed tour operators are one of the few effective pressures on the scam economy. Tourist-facing businesses know which complaints reach OCPB; a filed complaint sometimes produces a restitution offer that the shop refused to make on the day of the incident.

For overcharging by licensed taxis, tuk-tuks, *songthaews*, and public transport operators, file a parallel complaint with the **Department of Land Transport** via **1584**. DLT keeps the license records of every metered taxi and registered tuk-tuk in the country; with the license-plate number (always photograph it before getting out), DLT can identify the driver, issue fines, and sometimes produce compensation. For tour-operator disputes, the Tourism Authority of Thailand (**1672**) will route you to the Tourist Business and Guide Registration Office.

For banking and payment disputes — a charge your Thai bank refuses to reverse, an ATM skim you consider the bank should cover, a disputed online-transfer fraud — escalate to the **Bank of Thailand Financial Consumer Protection Center** at **1213**. The Bank of Thailand is the financial-sector ombudsman and publishes a standard complaint form at **bot.or.th**. For securities and investment-related fraud, the regulator is the **SEC Thailand** (Securities and Exchange Commission).

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business’s name, the address, the exact transaction details, the *bai jaeng kwaam* reference number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name and location only. Thai defamation law (both civil and criminal) is strict — stick to verifiable facts and receipts.

## If your phone or SIM was stolen

Call your carrier immediately to suspend the line. The three major Thai carriers — **AIS** (dial **1175**), **True** (dial **1242**), and **DTAC** (dial **1678**) — all operate 24/7 stolen-device lines. Suspending the line prevents the thief from using your number to receive SMS two-factor-authentication codes and from running up international-roaming charges. If you are on a tourist eSIM (Airalo, Saily, etc.), the app will let you suspend the line in a few taps; do that before the *bai jaeng kwaam*.

File a *bai jaeng kwaam* at the nearest Tourist Police or Royal Thai Police station and include your phone’s **IMEI** number (the fifteen-digit serial; find it on the original box, in your Apple or Google account, or by dialing \*#06# *before* the theft). The IMEI lets police flag the device and is required for any insurance claim. If your SIM was the target — for example, a SIM-swap scam to intercept your bank’s one-time codes — contact your bank’s fraud desk before anything else; SIM-swap fraud moves faster than theft recovery, and the first hour is decisive.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking, drug-testing, or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of 24/7 hospitals with English-speaking staff in each city is in Appendix C. At the hospital, ask for a toxicology screen; Thai private hospitals such as Bumrungrad, Bangkok Hospital, Samitivej, and BNH run these routinely and will print a report you can use as evidence.

For drug-testing or drug-planting scams — where a stranger, a bar staffer, or occasionally someone in uniform demands a roadside urine test or “finds” a substance in your possession — stay calm, do not resist, and call **1155** (Tourist Police) before any money changes hands. The Tourist Police will accompany you for support; real narcotics cases in Thailand go through official processes with written documentation, photographed evidence, and a police report you sign. Demands for cash to “make it go away” are the signature of a scam, not of legitimate enforcement.

In Thailand, the medical-emergency number is **1669** (the national EMS ambulance line; English available at major dispatch centers). For fire, dial **199**. For any incident involving a foreign tourist, **1155** (Tourist Police) is the most reliable fallback with English-speaking dispatchers. If you are inside a venue and drink-spiking is suspected, have a friend call for an ambulance *before* you leave, not after — sometimes the aggressive bill pressure escalates once the scammer realizes you are leaving.

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate a replacement. Consular registration — STEP for US citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, LOCATE for UK, ROCA for Canadians, and the Irish equivalent — is worth doing before you travel, specifically for situations like this.
