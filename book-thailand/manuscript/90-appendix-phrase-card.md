# Appendix A: Thai Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Thailand. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, followed by the **Thai** (in Thai script), a **Romanized** version (the most common Royal Thai General System transliteration), and a simple pronunciation guide in italics.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. Say the first three or four out loud a few times. You will feel much less awkward using them when the moment comes.

## A note on pronunciation

Thai is a **tonal language**. The same syllable spoken with different pitch contours means different words — *mai* can mean “no,” “new,” “silk,” “burn,” “mile,” or “wood” depending on tone. Carrying every tone correctly is genuinely hard for adult learners. The good news: for defensive phrases, you do not need to. Thai people are used to foreigners butchering the tones, and the key words of a polite refusal carry their meaning from context alone. If you say “*mai ao krap*” with any tone, any competent vendor will understand “don’t want, thanks.”

A few pointers that will get you 80 percent of the way:

- Thai vowels are clean and short, and each is always pronounced the same way: *a* is “ah,” *i* is “ee,” *o* is “oh,” *u* is “oo,” *e* is “eh.”
- The *r* in most Thai words is soft, often rolled slightly, and frequently collapses into an *l* sound in casual speech.
- Final consonants are unvoiced — *krap* ends with an abrupt stop, not a released *p* sound.
- The letter *ng* at the start of a word (as in *ngern*, “money”) is the same *ng* sound as at the end of “singing” — uncommon in English at the beginning of words, but worth practicing.

## A note on politeness

Thai defensive phrases end in a politeness particle — **krap** (pronounced “krap” with a short a, from a male speaker) or **kha** (pronounced “ka” with a falling tone, from a female speaker). These particles are not optional. A sentence without them sounds rude. Memorize the one that matches your gender and attach it to every phrase below.

Throughout this appendix, the male form is shown first; the female form is in parentheses.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**  
ราคาเท่าไหร่  
Rakhaa thaorai krap (kha)?  
*Rah-kah tao-rai, krap (kah)?*

**Please use the meter.**  
เปิดมิเตอร์ด้วยครับ (ค่ะ)  
Bpert miter duai krap (kha).  
*Bpert MEE-ter duay, krap (kah).*

Say this the instant you get into a tuk-tuk or taxi. If the driver refuses, get out. The phrase is spelled here with the English word “meter” because Thai taxi drivers will understand the English perfectly well — the Thai verb *bpert* (“to open, to turn on”) is the part that makes it a request.

**Can I see the menu please?**  
ขอดูเมนูหน่อยครับ (ค่ะ)  
Kho duu menuu noi krap (kha).  
*Kor doo meh-nu noi, krap (kah).*

Use this before you sit down at any restaurant. The words that matter are *menuu* (menu — same as English) and *kho duu* (may I see). If the owner brings a clipboard without prices, ask for the *paper* menu — the written one — before you order.

**What is the price per piece? (for seafood, fruit, or items priced by weight)**  
ราคาต่อชิ้นเท่าไหร่  
Rakhaa dtor chin thaorai krap (kha)?  
*Rah-kah dtor chin tao-rai, krap (kah)?*

Fish, prawns, and other items priced by weight should be confirmed in writing or on a phone calculator before cooking. A legitimate restaurant will weigh the whole item on a visible scale, quote a total in baht, and write it down. Demand this — the per-gram surprise bill is one of the most common scams in this book.

**Do you accept credit cards?**  
รับบัตรเครดิตไหม  
Rap bat credit mai krap (kha)?  
*Rap baht kredit mai, krap (kah)?*

If the answer is “*rap*” (“yes, we accept”) but the card reader later turns out to be “broken,” you are being scammed. See “Scam in progress” below.

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**  
ไม่เอาครับ (ค่ะ)  
Mai ao krap (kha).  
*My ow, krap (kah).*

Literally “don’t want.” Said firmly and without slowing your pace. This is the single most useful phrase in this entire appendix. Thai people understand it as a complete refusal; you do not need to explain why.

**I’m not interested.**  
ไม่สนใจครับ (ค่ะ)  
Mai son jai krap (kha).  
*My sohn jai, krap (kah).*

Stronger than *mai ao*. Use this when a tuk-tuk tout, a gem-shop recruiter, or a bracelet-seller keeps talking after the first refusal.

**Please leave me alone.**  
ไปเถอะครับ (ค่ะ)  
Pai ther krap (kha).  
*Pie ter, krap (kah).*

Literally “please go.” Gentle but clear. Use this for persistent touts.

**Don’t touch me.**  
อย่าจับผมครับ (male) / อย่าจับฉันค่ะ (female)  
Yaa jap phom krap / Yaa jap chan kha.  
*Yah jop PHOM krap (yah jop chahn kah).*

Say this clearly and step back. The word *yaa* (don’t) is emphatic. Most touts will disengage immediately rather than escalate.

**Excuse me, please let me through.**  
ขอทางหน่อย  
Kho thang noi krap (kha).  
*Kor tahng noi, krap (kah).*

The *kho* (“may I”) softens it so the person blocking your path hears a polite request. Say it clearly and keep moving forward.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I’m calling the Tourist Police.**  
ผมจะโทรหาตำรวจท่องเที่ยว (male) / ฉันจะโทรหาตำรวจท่องเที่ยว (female)  
Phom ja tho ha tamruat thong-thiao krap / Chan ja tho ha tamruat thong-thiao kha.  
*Pohm ja toh hah dtam-roo-at tong-tee-ow, krap (chahn ja toh hah dtam-roo-at tong-tee-ow, kah).*

This is the firmest card in the deck. The Tourist Police number is **1155** and it operates 24/7 with English-speaking dispatchers nationwide. Say the phrase, hold up your phone, and start dialing. Most scam operators back down the moment they hear *tamruat thong-thiao*.

**I’ll call my bank.**  
ผมจะโทรหาธนาคาร (male) / ฉันจะโทรหาธนาคาร (female)  
Phom ja tho ha thanakaan krap / Chan ja tho ha thanakaan kha.  
*Pohm ja toh hah tah-nah-kan, krap (chahn ja toh hah tah-nah-kan, kah).*

Say this if a restaurant, taxi driver, or jet-ski operator disputes a credit-card transaction. Calling your card issuer from the table signals that the charge is going to be disputed.

**I was scammed. Please help.**  
ผมโดนโกงครับ (male) / ฉันโดนโกงค่ะ (female) ช่วยด้วยครับ (ค่ะ)  
Phom don gong krap / Chan don gong kha. Chuay duay krap (kha).  
*Pohm dohn gohng, krap — chuay duay, krap (chahn dohn gohng, kah — chuay duay, kah).*

The word *don gong* is the specific Thai word for “was scammed” — it communicates what happened with more urgency than any English translation. Use this when speaking to a Tourist Police officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, a fellow traveler, or any bystander you want to enlist.

**Please give me an itemized receipt.**  
ขอใบเสร็จแบบละเอียด  
Kho bai set baep la-iat krap (kha).  
*Kor bai set baep la-iat, krap (kah).*

In Thailand, most legitimate businesses will provide an itemized receipt on request. Asking for *bai set baep la-iat* (literally “detailed receipt”) forces the establishment to account for every item, and it is the most polite version of “I do not accept this charge.” A legitimate place will hand it to you without complaint. A tourist trap will stall.

**I didn’t order this.**  
ผมไม่ได้สั่งอันนี้ (male) / ฉันไม่ได้สั่งอันนี้ (female)  
Phom mai dai sang an nee krap / Chan mai dai sang an nee kha.  
*Pohm my die sahng ahn nee, krap (chahn my die sahng ahn nee, kah).*

Say this calmly about any unrequested dish, bottle, or “service” that appears on your bill. In Thailand you are not legally required to pay for food or drinks you did not order, though you will usually need to say so explicitly.

**This is not what we agreed.**  
นี่ไม่ใช่ที่ตกลงกันไว้  
Nii mai chai thii tok long gan wai krap (kha).  
*Nee my chai tee tok long gahn wai, krap (kah).*

A short, firm phrase for taxi fares that exceed a quoted price, jet-ski “damage” claims, and tour-operator price-creep.

## Getting help

**Where’s the nearest Tourist Police office?**  
สถานีตำรวจท่องเที่ยวที่ใกล้ที่สุดอยู่ไหน  
Sathanee tamruat thong-thiao thii klai thii sut yoo nai krap (kha)?  
*Sah-tah-nee dtam-roo-at tong-tee-ow tee glai tee soot yoo nai, krap (kah)?*

Alternatively, just dial **1155**. The dispatcher will give you the nearest station address in English.

**I want an official taxi.**  
ผมต้องการแท็กซี่ทางการ (male) / ฉันต้องการแท็กซี่ทางการ (female)  
Phom tong gan taxi thang-gaan krap / Chan tong gan taxi thang-gaan kha.  
*Pohm tohng gahn taxi tahng-gahn, krap (chahn tohng gahn taxi tahng-gahn, kah).*

Or simpler: open Grab on your phone. Grab taxis are metered, tracked, and generally free of the “broken meter” scam.

**Please call 1155.**  
โทร 1155 ให้หน่อยครับ (ค่ะ)  
Tho nung nung haa haa hai noi krap (kha).  
*Toh nung nung haa haa hai noi, krap (kah).*

Thai numbers: *nung* (1), *haa* (5). 1155 is *nung nung haa haa*. Every Thai adult recognizes the Tourist Police number immediately.

**Help! Thief!**  
ช่วยด้วย! ขโมย!  
Chuay duay! Khamoi!  
*Chuay duay! Ka-MOY!*

Shouted in a crowded market or street, this is understood by every bystander as “someone stole from me, help me stop them.” The word *khamoi* (thief) carries legal weight in Thailand.

**Does anyone speak English?**  
มีใครพูดภาษาอังกฤษได้ไหม  
Mii khrai phuut phasa angkrit dai mai krap (kha)?  
*Mee kry poot pah-sah ahng-grit die mai, krap (kah)?*

**Please help me.**  
ช่วยผมหน่อย (male) / ช่วยฉันหน่อย (female)  
Chuay phom noi krap / Chuay chan noi kha.  
*Chuay pohm noi, krap (chuay chahn noi, kah).*

## Reading Thai baht amounts

Thai baht prices are usually written with either the Thai symbol **฿** or with “THB.” Decimal conventions match English (3,000.50 means three thousand point fifty). A few helpful markers:

- Small-denomination coins: 1, 2, 5, 10 baht. The 10-baht coin is silver with a gold center and easy to spot.
- Paper notes: 20 (green), 50 (blue), 100 (red), 500 (purple), 1,000 (gray). The portrait on every note is King Vajiralongkorn (Rama X).
- One US dollar is roughly 35 baht as of late 2025 — verify on xe.com before travel since the rate drifts.

| Amount | Written | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 baht | nung baht | *nung baht* |
| 10 baht | sip baht | *sip baht* |
| 20 baht | yiisip baht | *yee-sip baht* |
| 50 baht | haa-sip baht | *haa-sip baht* |
| 100 baht | nung roi baht | *nung roy baht* |
| 500 baht | haa roi baht | *haa roy baht* |
| 1,000 baht | nung phan baht | *nung pahn baht* |

If a vendor quotes a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it out:

**Please write the number down.**  
เขียนตัวเลขให้หน่อย  
Khian tua-lek hai noi krap (kha).  
*Kian dtua-lek hai noi, krap (kah).*

Almost every vendor will oblige, often on the back of a receipt or a paper napkin.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Thailand, polite firmness works better than raised voices — raising your voice is read as a loss of composure (*jai ron*, “hot heart”) and rarely helps your case. A calm *mai ao krap* is already a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *pai ther* (“please go”) and *yaa jap phom* (“don’t touch me”) are both firm without being rude, and *phom ja tho ha tamruat thong-thiao* (“I’m calling the Tourist Police”) is the firmest card in the deck.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar — and a calm *mai ao krap* with a *krap* (or *kha*) on the end is, in itself, proof you know enough Thai that the interaction is going to be harder than they expected.
