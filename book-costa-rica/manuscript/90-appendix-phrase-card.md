# Appendix A: Costa Rican Exit-Phrase Card {-}

Costa Rica's official language is Spanish, and while most hotel and high-end tour staff speak serviceable English, the phrases that end a scam in progress — the *Fuerza Pública* call, the *Ley 7472* invocation, the refused cash demand on the beach — need to happen in Spanish to work. The phrases below are short scripts you can say word for word at the moments when ordinary politeness would cost you money. They are organized by situation.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone and bookmark the two or three sections you think you are most likely to need. Say the first few lines out loud a few times so they feel natural. Costa Rican Spanish is softer than Mexican or Spanish-Spain speech — Ticos use the formal *usted* widely (even with close friends), rarely drop consonants, and lean on *"pura vida"* as a universal filler for "hello," "goodbye," "thanks," "you're welcome," and "it's fine." A warm *"pura vida"* at the start of an interaction — and a firm *"no, gracias"* to decline one — signals a traveler who is tuned in rather than a mark.

## Core phrases (memorize these five)

| Situation | Phrase (Spanish) | Pronunciation | Context |
|---|---|---|---|
| Declining a tout / vendor | *No, gracias.* | noh GRAH-syahs | The baseline refusal for every unsolicited approach — Route 618 khaki crew, Jacó beach vendor, Puerto Viejo drug dealer, Tamarindo *guachimán*. Keep walking, do not slow down, do not smile. |
| Buying time to get to safety | *No hablo español bien.* | noh AH-bloh es-pah-NYOHL byen | "I don't speak Spanish well." Used to disengage from a pressured conversation without escalation — follow with *"un momento"* (one moment) while you walk to a lit restaurant. |
| Emergency — police | *Llame a la policía, por favor.* | YAH-meh ah lah poh-lee-SEE-ah por fah-VOR | "Call the police, please." | Refusing a scam fee | *No voy a pagar esto.* | noh voy ah pah-GAHR ES-toh | "I'm not going to pay this." For the $200 surf-board-damage invoice at Playa Tamarindo, the ₡30,000 beach-parking escalation at Playa Espadilla, the "mandatory insurance" $2,032 ambush at LIR. Say it calmly. |

## Asking before you commit

**¿Cuánto cuesta?** (KWAN-toh KWES-tah) — "How much is it?"

The basic opener. Use it before sitting down at any *soda* or restaurant, before accepting any "helpful" transport offer, before any activity you did not initiate. If the vendor answers in a different currency than the menu, or dodges the question with *"barato, barato"* (cheap, cheap), leave.

**¿Está incluido el IVA y el servicio?** (es-TAH een-kloo-EE-doh el EE-vah ee el sehr-VEE-syoh) — "Is the VAT and service charge included?"

Costa Rica's Ley 7472 (consumer protection law) requires all prices to be displayed as *precio total* — tax and service included. Menus without printed prices are illegal. In Manuel Antonio's strip restaurants and the Jacó tourist-menu zone, 13% IVA plus a 10% "service" charge is routinely added to pre-tax quoted prices, pushing the real cost 23% above menu. A legitimate operator answers *"sí, todo incluido"*. A scammer stalls or says *"lo añadimos después"* (we add it later) — that is the cue to leave.

**¿Está puesta la María?** (es-TAH PWES-tah lah mah-REE-ah) — "Is the meter on?"

Red-taxi drivers in Costa Rica call the meter *María*. Legitimate drivers turn it on before the car moves; the base fare should read ₡650–₡900 in 2025/2026. "*La María está dañada*" ("the meter is broken") is the scam — get out and flag another cab. If a driver insists on a flat rate, agree in US dollars aloud ("FIFTY-FIVE dollars US"), write it on a napkin, and have them initial it.

**¿Me puede mostrar el carné ICT, por favor?** (meh PWEH-deh mohs-TRAR el kar-NEH ee-seh-TEH por fah-VOR) — "Can you show me the ICT license card, please?"

For any tour guide, surf instructor, or *"certified"* guide at Manuel Antonio, Monteverde, Tamarindo, or La Fortuna. Every legitimate ICT-registered guide carries a laminated *carné* with a photo, registration number, and expiration date, verifiable at visitcostarica.com's directory of 2,500+ certified guides. A refusal or stall is the cue to walk away.

**¿Puede enviarme la confirmación por Airbnb o Booking?** — "Can you send me the Airbnb or Booking.com confirmation through the app?"

Before paying any short-stay deposit. A legitimate host sends it inside five minutes. A scammer invents reasons why the app is broken, the account is under review, or "platform fees" mean you should pay direct. That invention is the scam.

## Declining and leaving

**No, gracias. / No me interesa.** (noh meh een-teh-RES-sah) — "No, thanks. / I'm not interested."

Said at normal volume, without slowing down, without making eye contact for more than one second. The default refusal for the Route 618 "ranger," the Calle 217 dealer, the Avenida Pastor Díaz golf-cart tout, the Tamarindo surf-instructor approach, the Parque Central *cambista*. Two uses in a row end almost any street approach.

**Lo pienso y regreso.** (loh PYEN-soh ee reh-GREH-soh) — "I'll think about it and come back."

The polite exit for any high-pressure sales pitch — the Monteverde hostel-desk upsell, the downtown La Fortuna storefront cash-deposit push, any "last golf cart today only" WhatsApp bait. Commits to nothing and gives you the off-ramp.

**No tengo efectivo.** (noh TEN-goh eh-fek-TEE-voh) — "I don't have cash."

For shakedowns where the demand is cash-only — the Playa Espadilla beach crew, the Jaco Walk parking "fine," the Delta gas-station colón short-change, the *cambista* "swap." Shuts down every scam that depends on untraceable bills.

**Necesito un minuto para llamar a mi banco.** — "I need a minute to call my bank."

For any counter dispute where you need to buy time — the Avis LIR insurance ambush, the SJO Expedia $2,032 counter, the Monteverde "package $100" pressure. A legitimate operator will wait. A scammer will escalate ("the offer ends now," "my manager is going home") — which is itself the tell.

## Scam in progress

**Necesito un recibo.** (neh-seh-SEE-toh oon reh-SEE-boh) — "I need a receipt."

For every transaction, always. Costa Rica mandates electronic invoicing (*factura electrónica*); any restaurant, tour, taxi, or gas station that "can't print one" is evading tax — and overcharging you at the same time. A legitimate operator produces it on demand. A scammer stalls or refuses, which gives you grounds to dispute on the card.

**¿Dónde está la Fuerza Pública?** (DOHN-deh es-TAH lah FWEHR-sah POO-blee-kah) — "Where's the police station?"

*Fuerza Pública* is Costa Rica's national uniformed police — dark-blue uniforms, shield patch. Say this to a hotel front desk, a restaurant host, or any local when you need to escalate. Major town Fuerza Pública stations are signposted from central plazas.

**Esto es un fraude. Voy a reportarlo.** — "This is fraud. I'm going to report it."

For a disputed bill that escalates past conversation — the Tamarindo $200 surf-board-damage invoice, the Avis Liberia mandatory-insurance ambush, the Manuel Antonio "gringo menu" stack. Say it calmly, pay credit card under protest (write "under protest" on the receipt), and file a Visa / Mastercard / Amex chargeback within 60 days plus a complaint at **consumidor.go.cr**. The word *fraude* tends to produce a revised invoice.

**Voy a disputar esto con mi tarjeta.** — "I'll dispute this with my card."

The phrase that most efficiently ends a Costa Rican overcharge dispute. Capital One Venture X, Chase Sapphire Reserve, and Amex Platinum handle CR disputes well; documented Reddit cases confirm chargebacks on monteverdetourscr.com ($1,000), Hotel Societal La Fortuna ($550 CAD), Avis Liberia phantom damage, and Uber SJO fare gouges all succeeded. Mentioning *tarjeta* (card) and *disputa* (dispute) often produces an immediate refund offer.

**Estoy llamando al 911.** — "I'm calling 911."

Use this for genuine threats, coercive payment demands, drink-spiking symptoms, or restraint of your freedom. Dial **911**. English dispatch is available through the Tourist Police Operations Center (launched September 2025 with hubs in San José and Jacó). For non-emergency fraud and theft, call OIJ tip line **800-8000-645** ("800-8000-OIJ") or Tourist Police 24/7 **2258-1008** / **2258-1022**.

**Fui víctima de un robo. ¿Me puede ayudar?** — "I was robbed. Can you help?"

Use this with a police officer, a hotel front desk, a tourism information officer, or any bystander you want to enlist. *Robo* is the Spanish word for theft. A softer alternative for overcharges and fraud without violence is *"Me estafaron"* (I was scammed) — useful with a restaurant manager or ICT officer. Ticos will usually help; the *pura vida* ethic cuts both ways.

## Numbers and currency

**₡500** — *quinientos colones* (kee-NYEN-tohs koh-LOH-nehs)
**₡1,000** — *mil colones* (meel koh-LOH-nehs)
**₡5,000** — *cinco mil colones*
**₡10,000** — *diez mil colones*
**₡20,000** — *veinte mil colones*
**$100 USD** — *cien dólares* (syen DOH-lah-rehs)

Costa Rican colón notes in circulation: ₡1,000 (red), ₡2,000 (blue), ₡5,000 (yellow), ₡10,000 (green), ₡20,000 (orange). Every note has a watermark, a color-shifting security thread that flips green/blue when tilted, and raised-print texture. The ₡20,000 carries Carlos Luis Fallas Sibaja. Counterfeits feel plastic-smooth and lack the color-shift thread.

**Always pay in colones, not USD.** Costa Rican merchants accept both, but paying in USD triggers an ad-hoc "rate" 5–10% worse than the Banco Central's posted rate (gee.bccr.fi.cr). ATM "dynamic currency conversion" (the "Would you like to pay in USD?" prompt) is the same skim in a different jacket — always decline.

## Acronyms worth knowing

- **Fuerza Pública** — national uniformed police (non-emergency).
- **OIJ** (*Organismo de Investigación Judicial*) — the investigative police, analogous to the FBI; you file a *denuncia* (formal report) here after any crime.
- **ICT** (*Instituto Costarricense de Turismo*) — the national tourism board; licenses guides and tour operators.
- **SINAC** (*Sistema Nacional de Áreas de Conservación*) — the national park service; tickets at sinac.go.cr.
- **MOPT** (*Ministerio de Obras Públicas y Transportes*) — transport ministry; regulates taxi fares.
- **SINPE Móvil** — Costa Rica's instant bank-transfer app. Ticos use it constantly; **do not send a SINPE deposit to anyone you do not know by their full registered name**. Costa Joe / Joseph Goodwin and the monteverdetourscr.com family all run on SINPE and Zelle advance-fee fraud.

## Cultural notes

**"Pura vida" is universal.** Use it to say hello, goodbye, thanks, you're welcome, and "I'm fine." Spoken at the start of an exchange with a *soda* owner or a taxi driver, it signals a traveler who has done homework.

**"Tico" and "Tica"** are what Costa Ricans call themselves — not derogatory, widely used.

**Tipping is modest.** A 10% service charge is required by law on all restaurant bills and already appears on the final receipt. Above that, ₡1,000–₡2,000 (or $2–$3) extra for good service is appreciated; in taxis, round up to the nearest ₡500 or ₡1,000. Hotel porters and housekeepers: ₡1,000 per bag or per night. No tip culture in *sodas* or bakeries.

**"Con gusto"** (with pleasure) is the standard reply to *"gracias"* — softer than *"de nada."* Ticos lean on *"con mucho gusto"* for warmth.

**Directions are given by landmarks, not addresses.** "*Cincuenta metros al sur del parque central*" (fifty meters south of the central park) is a normal address. GPS in town centers is often wrong by a block or two; the landmark method is faster. Ask for the *punto de referencia* (reference point).

**Avoid asking for a vendor's nickname.** If you are reporting a scammer to Fuerza Pública or OIJ, ask for their full legal name and *cédula* (national ID number) — nicknames like "Costa Joe" (Joseph Goodwin's alias) are often a cue that the operator is laundering identities.
