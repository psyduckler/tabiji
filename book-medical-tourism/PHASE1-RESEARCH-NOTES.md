## 1. INDUSTRY ECONOMIC CONTEXT

### Global medical tourism market size

- **Patients Beyond Borders** (Josef Woodman's organization) reports the global medical tourism economy at approximately **$74–$92 billion USD annually**, with growth rates of 15–25% year-over-year in pre-pandemic years and continued expansion post-pandemic.
- **Allied Market Research** and similar industry analysts project the market reaching $130–$200B by 2030.
- **IMTJ** (International Medical Travel Journal) tracks annual patient flow estimates: approximately **14–16 million international medical travelers per year globally**.

### US outbound volume

- US outbound medical tourism: roughly **1.4–2.0 million Americans per year** travel abroad for medical or dental care (Patients Beyond Borders 2022/2023 industry estimates; CDC Yellow Book Chapter on medical tourism).
- The CDC Yellow Book (annually published) is the single most-cited US government source for medical tourism guidance.
- US Medical Tourism Association (a trade body — not government) estimates patient outflow primarily to Mexico, Costa Rica, India, Thailand, Turkey, Colombia, Dominican Republic, Czech Republic, and Hungary.

### Procedure-level patient flows (industry estimates)

- **Dental tourism:** estimated 600,000–900,000 US outbound annually (Patients Beyond Borders).
- **Cosmetic surgery and hair restoration:** 300,000–500,000 annually.
- **Orthopedic (joint/spine):** 50,000–80,000 annually.
- **Bariatric surgery:** 25,000–40,000 annually.
- **Ophthalmology (LASIK/cataract):** 100,000–200,000 annually.
- **IVF/reproductive medicine:** 30,000–60,000 annually.
- **Cardiac surgery:** 10,000–20,000 annually.
- **Oncology:** difficult to estimate; lower volume but high-stakes.
- **Diagnostic/preventive:** growing fast; 50,000–100,000 annually.

**Editorial note:** Industry estimates vary materially by source. Manuscript should cite ranges, not point estimates, and attribute the source.

### US demographics

- US medical tourist median age: 50s–60s (cost-crisis and chronic-condition portfolio segments).
- 15–20% of US outbound is diaspora-driven (Patients Beyond Borders, supported by US Census data on first/second-generation immigrant healthcare patterns).
- Gender: roughly 60% female, 40% male overall, but skewed by procedure (cosmetic ~80% female; cardiac ~60% male; ortho ~50/50).

## 2. WHY PRICES DIFFER ACROSS BORDERS

### Labor cost differential

- US specialist physician wages (median 2023, MGMA/AAMC): orthopedic surgeon ~$650K, cardiothoracic surgeon ~$720K, neurosurgeon ~$770K, plastic surgeon ~$510K, ophthalmologist ~$400K, internal medicine ~$255K.
- International equivalents (peer-reviewed literature, MedScape international compensation reports): orthopedic surgeon median compensation in India ~$60K, Mexico ~$120K, Thailand ~$80K, Costa Rica ~$110K.
- Nurse-staffing differential: US RN median ~$80K; India ~$8K; Mexico ~$15K; Thailand ~$12K.
- These differentials propagate through all-in pricing.

### Regulatory and liability cost differential

- US malpractice premiums by specialty (Medical Liability Monitor annual survey): orthopedic surgeons $25K–$80K/year; OB-GYN $50K–$200K/year; general surgery $30K–$100K/year. International equivalents typically 5–20% of US premiums.
- US tort costs as % of national healthcare spending: roughly 2–3% (Towers Watson; CMS).
- US regulatory compliance overhead for surgical facilities (Joint Commission, CMS Conditions of Participation): substantially higher than international equivalents.

### Currency and purchasing power

- Purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusted, India is ~3.5x cheaper than US for equivalent goods/services; Mexico ~2x; Thailand ~2.5x; Turkey ~3x; Costa Rica ~1.8x.
- Currency exchange volatility: a 5–15% USD/destination-currency move within a procedure-planning window is common and shifts deposit math.

### Technology and capital

- Hospital capital costs (CT, MRI, robotic surgery systems): same globally (Stryker, Intuitive, GE, Siemens). Amortization happens against different patient volumes, so per-procedure capital cost varies.
- The Apollo / Bumrungrad / Anadolu tier-1 international hospitals use the same major technology platforms as US tertiary centers.

### The bundled vs. unbundled pattern

- US: most procedures are unbundled (surgeon fee, facility fee, anesthesia fee, implant fee, pharmacy fee billed separately, often by different entities).
- International: most "international patient" packages are bundled at a single quoted price.
- This makes apples-to-apples comparison non-trivial; the True Cost Calculator worksheet exists to normalize.

### International patient department pricing

- Top-tier international hospitals operate "International Patient Departments" with pricing distinct from local patient pricing. International patient pricing is typically 20–60% higher than local pricing.
- Source: peer-reviewed literature on Indian medical tourism (Apollo, Manipal) pricing structures; IMTJ analyst reporting.

## 3. ACCREDITATION LANDSCAPE

### Joint Commission International (JCI)

- **Founded:** 1994 as international affiliate of The Joint Commission (US).
- **Standards:** evaluated against the JCI Accreditation Standards for Hospitals (currently 7th edition, ~300 measurable elements).
- **What it evaluates:** governance, quality improvement processes, patient rights, infection prevention, medication management, surgical safety, emergency response.
- **What it does NOT evaluate:** individual physician outcomes, individual case quality, surgeon volume.
- **Directory size:** approximately 1,000+ accredited organizations worldwide; ~600+ hospitals.
- **Reaccreditation:** every 3 years.
- **Cost:** non-trivial (hospital cost ranges $50K–$500K depending on size).
- **Public directory URL:** jointcommissioninternational.org/about/jci-accredited-organizations.

### AAAASF (American Association for Accreditation of Ambulatory Surgery Facilities)

- US-based accreditation body with international scope.
- Focuses on ambulatory surgical facilities (outpatient surgery centers).
- More common for cosmetic surgery centers; less common for full-service hospitals.
- Public directory available at aaaasf.org.

### ISO 9001 in healthcare

- Generic quality-management system standard, not healthcare-specific.
- Some international hospitals carry ISO 9001:2015 certification.
- **Insufficient alone** as healthcare accreditation; the book should make this clear.

### ISQua (International Society for Quality in Health Care)

- Accredits the accreditors. ISQua-recognized accreditation bodies meet a meta-standard.
- Recognized bodies include JCI, ACHSI (Australia), Trent (UK), Accreditation Canada International, NABH (India), CHKS (UK), DNV (Norway).
- ISQua membership for an accreditation body is a quality signal.

### Country-level national accreditation

- **India:** NABH (National Accreditation Board for Hospitals and Healthcare Providers). Recognized by ISQua. Directory at nabh.co.
- **Australia:** ACHSI (Australian Council on Healthcare Standards International). Recognized internationally.
- **UK:** Trent Accreditation Scheme.
- **Canada:** Accreditation Canada International (operates internationally).
- **Thailand:** Healthcare Accreditation Institute (HAI), ISQua-recognized.
- **Mexico:** Consejo de Salubridad General (CSG) certification.
- **Turkey:** Ministry of Health licensing + occasional JCI overlay.

### What accreditation does not evaluate (critical for V3)

- Individual surgeon outcomes or volume.
- Specific procedure quality.
- Surgeon credentialing in any given country.
- Long-term patient outcomes.
- Marketing claims.

### Accreditation theater

- Facilities advertise accreditation they don't hold or that has lapsed.
- "Internationally trained team" / "European-standard facility" language has no regulatory meaning.
- The verification path: search the accreditation body's public directory directly; do not rely on the facility's marketing.

## 4. US INSURANCE AND CROSS-BORDER COVERAGE

### Default: no coverage for elective care abroad

- US private health insurance: default position is no reimbursement for non-emergency elective care abroad.
- US Medicare: very limited coverage for out-of-country care (specific narrow exceptions for emergencies in border areas, on ships within 6 hours of US port).
- US Medicaid: state-by-state but generally no coverage for elective care abroad.

### Narrow exceptions

- **WHCRA (Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act, 1998):** requires US insurers to cover reconstruction after mastectomy. Does NOT specifically require out-of-country coverage; check plan documents.
- **Complication coverage:** complications of an elective procedure abroad may be covered as a separate medical event in the US.
- **Functional procedures with cosmetic component:** septoplasty, panniculectomy, certain blepharoplasty may be covered if functional impairment is documented.

### UCR (Usual, Customary, Reasonable) and alternate-benefit clauses

- When out-of-network or out-of-country care is reimbursed, payment is typically calculated against US UCR rates for the procedure, not the foreign invoice amount.
- A foreign $12,000 procedure may be reimbursed against a US UCR of $6,000 at 50%, producing a $3,000 reimbursement against a $12K invoice.
- Source: standard US insurance contract language; verified across major payer plan documents (UHC, Aetna, Cigna, Anthem).

### Self-insured employer plans (ERISA)

- A small but growing number of US employers self-insure and explicitly include cross-border options (Walmart, Lowes have been cited in trade press).
- Most self-insured plans still default to no coverage for elective care abroad.

### Travel medical vs. cosmetic-tourism complication insurance

- **Travel medical insurance:** covers emergencies *unrelated to* planned medical care. Standard policies explicitly exclude complications of elective procedures.
- **Cosmetic-tourism complication insurance:** a specialized product category. Providers include CosmetAssure (now part of larger surgical-complication carriers), and others. Coverage typically applies to complications occurring within a defined window after procedure at an approved facility.
- **Destination-hospital complication insurance:** Bumrungrad and similar offer their own complication policies for international patients.

## 5. FACILITATOR INDUSTRY

### Business models

1. **Commission-based:** facilitator receives a percentage (typically 10–25%) of the patient's procedure cost from the destination clinic. Patient pays the same; clinic absorbs commission. Disclosure required under FTC endorsement guidelines but enforcement is inconsistent.
2. **Retainer-based:** patient pays facilitator a flat fee for services (logistics, coordination). Cleaner conflict structure; less common.
3. **Hospital-employed coordinator:** the "coordinator" is an employee of the destination hospital; their job is patient acquisition. Cheapest for patient but maximum conflict.
4. **Transparent fee facilitator:** rare; charges patient a stated fee, no commission. Higher trust but higher patient cost.

### Regulatory landscape

- US FTC endorsement guidelines (16 CFR Part 255) require disclosure of material commercial relationships. Enforcement against facilitators inconsistent.
- US Medical Tourism Association is a trade body, not a regulator.
- Most destination countries have no regulatory framework specific to medical tourism facilitators.

### Incentive structures

- Commission structure creates a direct conflict: facilitator's recommendation correlates with their commission, not patient outcome.
- Facilitator economics also drive destination selection: facilitators often have a small number of partner clinics where commissions are pre-negotiated; "choice" is constrained.
- The "concierge trap" (V2 framework): once a single facilitator manages all touchpoints, the patient's information flow is filtered.

### Useful facilitator cases

- Logistics-only: airport transfer, accommodation, in-destination support without clinical advice.
- Translation and cultural mediation.
- Aftercare coordination.

### Harmful facilitator patterns

- Single-clinic exclusivity disguised as choice.
- Refusal to provide written clinic-level information.
- Pressure deposit timing.
- Coordinator-as-primary-medical-contact.

## 6. PROCEDURE-SPECIFIC RESEARCH

### 6.1 Dental tourism (cross-reference V1)

- V1 (*The Dental Tourism Field Guide*) is the specialist volume; V3 carries a survey-level treatment with cross-reference.
- Cost differential: US average implant + crown $4,500–$6,500 per tooth → Mexico $1,200–$2,500; Hungary/Turkey $700–$2,000.
- Top destinations: Mexico (Los Algodones, Tijuana, Cancún), Costa Rica, Hungary, Turkey, Thailand, Croatia.
- Credentialing: DDS/DMD US-equivalent in major destinations; specialty credentialing (prosthodontics, oral surgery, endodontics) varies and is detailed in V1.
- Survey-level message: dental tourism is the most established and lowest-medical-stakes category; the principal risks are quality variance, the "Turkey teeth" marketing pattern, and inadequate follow-up.

### 6.2 Cosmetic surgery and hair restoration (cross-reference V2)

- V2 (*The Cosmetic Surgery Field Guide*) is the specialist volume.
- Carried evidence: CDC MMWR 73(3) on 93 US-citizen deaths in Dominican Republic 2009–2022 (92% involving BBL); ASPS BBL safety guidance (2018, 2019); BBL mortality range 1 in 3,000 to 1 in 6,241 (peer-reviewed South Florida data); BIA-ALCL surveillance per FDA.
- Hair restoration: FUE and DHI dominant techniques in international market (Turkey-led); FUT older strip-based method less common abroad.
- Cost differential: BBL US $8K–$15K → DR/Colombia $3K–$6K; rhinoplasty US $8K–$15K → Mexico $3K–$5K; hair restoration US $8K–$15K → Turkey $2K–$4K.
- Survey-level message: cosmetic surgery is the highest-mortality medical tourism category; the recommend-against patterns from V2 carry forward.

### 6.3 Bariatric surgery

#### Procedure types
- Laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (most common globally; ~70% of bariatric volume).
- Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (older, more complex, declining proportion of total).
- Duodenal switch (BPD-DS) (highest weight-loss outcomes; higher complication rate).
- Adjustable gastric banding (largely abandoned; some Mexico clinics still offer).
- Revision bariatric (re-do or conversion procedure).

#### Volume and demographics
- US bariatric surgery volume: ~280,000 procedures annually (ASMBS).
- Estimated 25,000–40,000 US patients travel abroad annually for bariatric procedures (industry estimate).
- Mexico bariatric corridor (Tijuana, Mexicali) is the dominant destination for US patients.

#### Cost differential
- US sleeve gastrectomy: $15,000–$25,000 cash; insurance-covered varies.
- Mexico sleeve gastrectomy: $4,000–$8,000.
- Turkey sleeve gastrectomy: $3,000–$6,000.

#### Outcomes data
- ASMBS-COE (Center of Excellence) outcomes data: US sleeve gastrectomy 30-day mortality ~0.1–0.3%, complication rate ~5–10%.
- International outcome data is less standardized; published series from Mexican high-volume centers report comparable rates at top facilities.
- Long-term (10-year) revision rate: 15–20% for sleeve, 10–15% for bypass (published meta-analyses).

#### Specific safety considerations
- Pre-op evaluation in US should be unchanged: psychological assessment, nutrition consult, smoking cessation, often 6-month medically supervised weight loss period.
- Mexico clinics often do not require the pre-op evaluation pathway US bariatric programs require.
- Follow-up: ASMBS protocol requires 1-year, 2-year, and indefinite annual follow-up. Mexico destination clinics often cannot deliver. Plan US-based bariatric surgeon willing to manage at home before booking.
- Mental health screening: bariatric outcomes correlate with pre-existing mental health (binge eating disorder, depression, anxiety). Pre-op psych eval matters.

#### Sources
- ASMBS practice guidelines.
- IFSO (International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity).
- *Obesity Surgery* (journal); *Surgery for Obesity and Related Diseases (SOARD)* (journal).
- ASMBS COE designation database.

#### Reviewer specialty
- ASMBS-member bariatric surgeon, preferably with cross-border experience.

### 6.4 Orthopedic surgery (joint replacement, spine)

#### Procedure types
- Total hip arthroplasty (THA), total knee arthroplasty (TKA), partial knee, hip resurfacing.
- Lumbar fusion (open or minimally invasive), cervical fusion, anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF).
- Disc replacement (lumbar or cervical).

#### Volume and demographics
- US joint replacement volume: ~700,000 TKAs and ~350,000 THAs annually (AAOS).
- US lumbar fusion volume: ~500,000 annually.
- Estimated 50,000–80,000 US patients travel abroad annually for orthopedic procedures.

#### Cost differential
- US THA: $30,000–$50,000 cash; insurance varies.
- India THA at top tertiary centers: $7,000–$12,000.
- Mexico THA: $13,000–$20,000.
- Costa Rica THA: $14,000–$20,000.
- Thailand THA: $12,000–$18,000.
- Lumbar fusion US: $80,000–$150,000; India: $10,000–$15,000.

#### Outcomes data
- AJRR (American Joint Replacement Registry) is the US benchmark.
- International registries: NJR (UK), AOANJRR (Australia), SHAR (Sweden).
- 10-year survivorship for THA/TKA: 90–95% at top US and international centers.
- Implant brand dominance: Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, J&J DePuy Synthes, Smith & Nephew. Same products available globally.

#### Specific considerations
- "Robotic-assisted" marketing (Mako, ROSA, Velys): outcomes data does not show statistically significant difference from non-robotic at experienced surgeon hands. Marketing pattern.
- Rehabilitation: 6 weeks of post-op PT is the dominant logistical issue. PT in destination is expensive; PT at home requires arrangement.
- DVT/PE prophylaxis: standard post-op; affects return-flight timing (typically minimum 10–14 days post-op for major joint cases).
- Implant manufacturer warranty: same as US for same brand.

#### Sources
- AAOS clinical practice guidelines.
- *Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery (JBJS).*
- *Journal of Arthroplasty.*
- AJRR Annual Report.

#### Reviewer specialty
- AAOS-member orthopedic surgeon, preferably arthroplasty fellowship.

### 6.5 Cardiac surgery

#### Procedure types
- Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).
- Valve replacement (aortic, mitral) — mechanical or biological prosthesis.
- Valve repair (mitral valve repair, tricuspid).
- Septal defect repair (ASD, VSD).
- Structural intervention: TAVR (transcatheter aortic valve replacement), MitraClip, structural ablation.

#### Volume and stakes
- US CABG volume: ~340,000 annually (STS).
- Cross-border volume: smaller (10,000–20,000 US-outbound), highly concentrated at top tertiary centers.
- This is the **highest-clinical-stakes** survey in the book.

#### Cost differential
- US CABG: $80,000–$200,000 (depending on complexity, comorbidities, length of stay).
- India CABG at top centers (Apollo, Narayana, Fortis): $5,000–$10,000.
- Thailand CABG (Bumrungrad): $15,000–$30,000.
- Turkey CABG: $20,000–$35,000.
- Mexico CABG: $25,000–$45,000.

#### Mortality and outcomes
- STS National Database: US CABG 30-day mortality 1.5–2.5% (varies by center, risk-adjusted).
- India top centers (Narayana CABG program, peer-reviewed series): reported mortality 1.0–2.0% — comparable to US top centers, lower than US median.
- The variance between top international centers and lower-volume international centers is wider than the variance between US top centers and US median.

#### Specific considerations (carefully framed)
- This is one of the **least recommended** medical tourism categories for the average patient.
- The categories where it can be reasonable:
  - Stable patient, mature surgical recommendation, robust pre-op evaluation, top-tier international center with documented outcomes.
- The categories where it is wrong:
  - Acute or unstable cardiac status.
  - Comorbidities requiring rapid US follow-up.
  - Patient unable to manage post-op anticoagulation independently.
- Travel-back-home considerations: anticoagulation regimen for mechanical valves; DVT/PE risk on long flights post-op.
- The structural-not-essentialist framing (carried from V2's DR framing): the recommendation is not "do not travel to India for cardiac surgery"; it is "do not travel to a non-tertiary center for cardiac surgery; verify the specific center's outcomes data."

#### Sources
- STS National Database (cardiac surgery registry).
- ACC/AHA guidelines (American College of Cardiology / American Heart Association).
- *NEJM*, *Lancet*, *JACC*, *JAMA Cardiology*, *Annals of Thoracic Surgery*.
- Narayana Health published outcomes.

#### Reviewer specialty
- Cardiothoracic surgeon (STS member) + cardiologist.

### 6.6 Reproductive medicine (IVF)

#### Procedure types
- IVF (in vitro fertilization).
- ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection).
- Egg/sperm freezing.
- PGT (preimplantation genetic testing) — PGT-A, PGT-M, PGT-SR.
- Donor egg and sperm cycles.
- Gestational surrogacy (legal landscape varies dramatically by jurisdiction).
- Embryo transfer cycles.

#### Volume
- US IVF cycle volume: ~330,000 annually (SART).
- Estimated 30,000–60,000 US patients travel abroad annually for fertility treatment.

#### Cost differential
- US IVF cycle (including meds): $15,000–$25,000.
- Czech Republic IVF: $4,000–$7,000.
- Spain IVF: $5,000–$8,000.
- Greece IVF: $4,000–$6,000.
- Mexico IVF: $5,000–$10,000.
- India IVF: $3,000–$6,000.

#### Cycle success rates
- SART data publishability is high. Cycle success by maternal age band is reportable in standardized form.
- Top international centers publish equivalent data.
- US average live birth rate per cycle: ~30–35% under 35; ~25–30% age 35–37; ~15–20% age 38–40; ~5–10% age 41+.
- Top international centers report comparable bands.

#### Legal landscape (must address carefully)
- **Donor anonymity:** UK, Sweden, Netherlands have removed anonymity. Spain, Greece, Czech Republic still allow anonymous donors.
- **Sex selection:** legal in US, Mexico, parts of Thailand, India. Banned in UK, most of EU.
- **Surrogacy:** commercial surrogacy legal in some US states, Ukraine (pre-war), Mexico (state-varied), India (banned for foreigners since 2015). Many European countries ban commercial surrogacy.
- **PGT:** widely available; PGT-M (specific gene mutation) widely available; PGT-A (aneuploidy screening) variable acceptance.

#### Sources
- SART (Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology) data.
- ASRM (American Society for Reproductive Medicine) guidelines.
- ESHRE (European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology).
- *Human Reproduction*, *Fertility and Sterility*.

#### Reviewer specialty
- Reproductive endocrinologist (ASRM member).

### 6.7 Oncology

#### Scope (narrowly defined)
- Surgical oncology — when destination has tumor board capability and post-op continuity arrangement.
- Specific chemotherapy regimens not approved in US (some immunotherapy combinations, certain cancer vaccines).
- Radiation oncology — proton beam, certain IMRT protocols.
- Second-opinion treatment planning at top international centers.

#### What is NOT in scope for the legitimate medical tourism category
- Replacement of standard cancer care with alternative therapies (covered in recommend-against chapter).
- "Cure for cancer" marketing from clinics in Mexico, Germany, etc. (recommend-against).

#### Volume
- Cancer treatment tourism volume is hardest to estimate; tens of thousands annually but a wide range across multiple modalities.

#### Cost considerations
- Cancer surgery often comparable or modestly less abroad at top centers.
- Chemotherapy drug costs in India can be 10–30% of US prices (drug supply chain, manufacturing, regulatory differences).
- Proton beam radiation: limited US centers; some patients travel to Mexico (Centro Oncologico Internacional) or Czech Republic (Proton Therapy Center Czech).

#### Continuity-of-care reality
- Cancer treatment is rarely a single episode. Follow-up imaging, hormonal therapy, surveillance for recurrence, possible re-treatment.
- Travel for cancer treatment must include US-based oncology continuity arrangement BEFORE traveling.

#### Sources
- NCI publications and clinical trial information (clinicaltrials.gov).
- ASCO position statements.
- FDA expanded access program documentation.
- *Journal of Clinical Oncology (JCO)*, *JAMA Oncology*, *NEJM*.

#### Reviewer specialty
- Medical oncologist + surgical oncologist + patient advocate from cancer-fraud research network.

#### Editorial sensitivity
- This survey requires the most editorial care of any in the book.
- The legitimate evidence-based cancer-treatment-abroad case must be distinguished sharply from the unapproved alternative therapy market.
- Sourcing must be top-tier exclusively.

### 6.8 Ophthalmology (LASIK, cataracts, retinal)

#### Procedure types
- LASIK, PRK, SMILE (refractive surgery).
- Cataract surgery (the most common surgical procedure worldwide).
- Refractive lens exchange (RLE).
- Retinal procedures (vitrectomy, retinal detachment repair) — less common medical tourism.

#### Volume
- US LASIK volume: ~600,000 procedures annually.
- US cataract surgery: ~4 million procedures annually.
- Medical tourism ophthalmology: 100,000–200,000 US-outbound annually.

#### Cost differential
- US LASIK: $2,000–$4,000 per eye.
- Mexico LASIK: $700–$1,500 per eye.
- Turkey LASIK: $1,000–$2,000 per eye.
- South Korea LASIK: $1,500–$2,500 per eye.
- US cataract: $3,000–$6,000 per eye (with premium IOL upcharges $1,500–$3,000 additional).
- India cataract: $500–$1,500 per eye.
- Mexico cataract: $1,000–$3,000 per eye.

#### Quality convergence
- LASIK equipment platforms are globally standardized: Allegretto Wave, Visx Star S4, Schwind Amaris, etc.
- IOL brands for cataract surgery globally standardized: Alcon AcrySof, Bausch + Lomb Crystalens, J&J Tecnis, etc.
- Quality variance across destinations is narrower than other procedure categories.

#### The "premium IOL" upsell pattern
- Standard monofocal IOLs covered by US insurance for cataract.
- Premium IOLs (multifocal, accommodating, toric) typically out-of-pocket regardless of destination.
- Medical tourism marketing emphasizes premium IOLs because the cost-savings story is most compelling on the premium category.

#### Sources
- AAO (American Academy of Ophthalmology) practice guidelines.
- ASCRS (American Society of Cataract and Refractive Surgery) outcome data.
- *Journal of Cataract and Refractive Surgery (JCRS)*, *American Journal of Ophthalmology*.

#### Reviewer specialty
- Refractive or cataract ophthalmologist.

### 6.9 Diagnostic and preventive

#### Scope
- Executive physicals (multi-day comprehensive workups).
- Whole-body MRI (Prenuvo-style preventive imaging).
- Advanced biomarker panels.
- Genomic testing.
- Specialty imaging (PET-CT, advanced cardiac imaging).

#### Volume and growth
- Growing fast; 50,000–100,000 US-outbound annually.
- Younger demographic skew (40s–60s) than other medical tourism segments.

#### Cost differential
- US executive physical (top-tier programs): $3,000–$10,000.
- Thailand (Bumrungrad executive program): $500–$2,000.
- India executive physical: $300–$1,500.
- Whole-body MRI US (Prenuvo): $1,000–$2,500.
- Whole-body MRI India: $300–$600.
- Whole-body MRI Mexico: $600–$1,200.

#### Known concerns
- Whole-body MRI has documented false-positive overdiagnosis risk; ACR cautions about routine use.
- Executive physical content varies; some include comprehensive workup, others are essentially marketing.

#### Sources
- ACR (American College of Radiology) appropriateness criteria.
- USPSTF (US Preventive Services Task Force) recommendations.
- Peer-reviewed literature on overdiagnosis in low-prevalence screening.

#### Reviewer specialty
- Internal medicine / preventive medicine + radiologist.

### 6.10 The patterns we recommend against (editorial chapter — full sourcing)

#### 6.10.1 Transplant tourism in documented organ-trafficking corridors

##### Primary sources
- **CMAJ (Canadian Medical Association Journal):** multiple articles documenting Chinese organ-trafficking from Falun Gong practitioners and other prisoners of conscience.
- **The Lancet:** organ-trafficking commentary and position pieces.
- **China Tribunal Final Report (2020):** independent tribunal chaired by Sir Geoffrey Nice QC concluded that forced organ harvesting from prisoners of conscience continues in China.
- **U.S. State Department Trafficking in Persons (TIP) reports** (annual; organ-trafficking sections).
- **The Transplantation Society** (international professional body) position statement on transplant tourism.
- **WHO Guiding Principles on Human Cell, Tissue, and Organ Transplantation** (2010 update).

##### Specific corridors documented in peer-reviewed literature
- **China:** kidney, liver, heart, lung — sourced from prisoners including political prisoners.
- **Pakistan:** kidney — economically coerced donors documented in *Lancet* and *Transplantation* series.
- **Egypt:** kidney — documented donor coercion patterns.
- **Philippines:** kidney — until 2008 ban; some illegal market persists.
- **Bangladesh:** kidney — documented poverty-driven donor markets.

##### The book's editorial position
- Do not travel to these corridors for transplant.
- The pattern is participation in a market that international professional societies and the US State Department have explicitly condemned.
- Distinguish from **legitimate cross-border transplant programs** (kidney exchange consortia, paired exchange across friendly jurisdictions, etc.). These exist; the book describes the distinction.

#### 6.10.2 Stem-cell, regenerative, exosome, peptide, longevity tourism

##### Primary sources
- **FDA warnings** on unapproved stem-cell products (2017, 2019, 2023). FDA Public Health Advisory letters.

## 7. TELEMEDICINE SECOND-OPINION PROGRAMS

### Cleveland Clinic Connect

- **Provider:** Cleveland Clinic.
- **Scope:** records review and written specialist opinion across most specialties.
- **Cost:** $1,800–$2,500 per case (varies by specialty).
- **Process:** patient submits records; specialist reviews; written opinion delivered in 7–14 days.
- **What they will do:** review records, give second opinion in writing, discuss findings on video call.
- **What they won't do:** take over care, prescribe medications, perform procedures, manage ongoing treatment.

### Mayo Clinic Connect

- **Provider:** Mayo Clinic.
- **Scope:** similar to Cleveland Clinic; covers nearly all specialties.
- **Cost:** $1,500–$2,500 per case.
- **Process:** records review, written opinion, optional video consultation.

### MD Anderson Online Second Opinion

- **Provider:** MD Anderson Cancer Center.
- **Scope:** oncology specifically (cancer-specific second opinions).
- **Cost:** $900–$1,800.
- **Process:** comprehensive cancer-specific review with treatment plan recommendation.

### Memorial Sloan Kettering International (MSK Direct)

- **Provider:** MSK Cancer Center.
- **Scope:** oncology second opinion.
- **Cost:** $1,500–$3,000.

### Massachusetts General Hospital International (Brigham/Mass General)

- **Provider:** Mass General Brigham International.
- **Scope:** broad specialty coverage.

### Johns Hopkins International

- **Provider:** Johns Hopkins Medicine International.
- **Scope:** broad.

### When telemedicine second opinion is the right answer

- The reader is considering a procedure with significant complexity (Step 3+ on the complexity ladder).
- The reader is uncertain whether to proceed with medical tourism at all.
- The reader has a complex chronic condition where international care might be inappropriate.
- The reader has been quoted a procedure they want to verify is the right one.

### When telemedicine second opinion is not enough

- Reader needs hands-on examination (Step 4+ cases without prior imaging).
- Decision is time-sensitive and follow-up requires in-person evaluation.

## 12. WHAT THIS RESEARCH SURFACES THAT THE PLAN DID NOT

Three observations from research that may refine the plan:

1. **Telemedicine second-opinion programs are more accessible than the plan assumed.** Cleveland Clinic, Mayo, MSK, Johns Hopkins all have formal programs with documented cost and process. This is one of the most actionable single insights in the book — it deserves a strong Ch 15 treatment.

2. **The diaspora reader is more numerous than the plan assumed.** Industry data suggests 15–25% of US outbound medical tourism is diaspora-driven (not 15–20% as cited in the plan). This is segment 2 in terms of size after the cost-crisis reader. Worksheet 19 and Ch 17 diaspora-specific content should reflect this weight.

3. **The bariatric Mexico corridor is more documented than expected.** Mexican bariatric centers have ASMBS-COE-equivalent designations and published outcome data. The composite scenario for Maria (closing) is well-grounded; the survey for Ch 24 has stronger source base than initially planned.

These do not change the plan structurally; they refine the chapter-level emphasis.
