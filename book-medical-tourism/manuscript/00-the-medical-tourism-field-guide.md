# Front Matter

## A Note to the Reader

You may have come to this book because a friend traveled abroad for surgery and came back with a story that made you wonder if you should do the same. You may have come because the dental quote in your hand has a number that does not match any savings you have. You may have come because your mother needs a hip replacement and her insurance just denied the prior authorization for the third time. You may have come because you have been scrolling Instagram and a transformation reel held your attention longer than you meant it to. You may have come because someone you love is sick, and the US oncology consult ended with a plan you cannot afford and a deadline you cannot meet.

If any of those is true, the book in your hand is the one we wrote for you.

This is not a destination directory. It does not rank clinics, surgeons, countries, or facilitators. It does not tell you where to go or whom to see. It does not promise that medical tourism will solve your problem, and it does not warn you off it either. What it does is something narrower and more useful: it teaches you how to *think* about a healthcare decision that crosses an international border, before you commit money or time or a body to it.

The book has a through-line. You will meet Marcus, a fifty-eight-year-old project manager from Denver who has been quoted twenty-two thousand dollars out of pocket for bilateral knee replacement and who is sitting at his kitchen table at eleven o'clock at night, on Reddit, trying to figure out what to do. You will meet Priya, a forty-seven-year-old engineer with a mother in Mumbai who needs a cardiac procedure that costs a fifth of the US price at a hospital her family has trusted for decades. You will meet Eleanor, sixty-five and recently retired, balancing four procedures against an HSA and a fixed income. You will meet Daniel, researching cataract surgery for his father with early dementia. You will meet Karen, whose mother just heard the words *stage three breast cancer*. At the close of the book, you will meet Maria, fifty-two, five years out from a bariatric surgery in Mexico that worked — and the framework that made it work for her.

None of these people are real. All of these people are composites, drawn from documented patterns in CDC data, peer-reviewed surgical outcome studies, professional society guidance, and the experience of patients who have made these decisions well and badly. None of the scenarios show a patient dying or being permanently disabled. The book is a buyer-protection field guide; the scenarios are near-misses and successful trips, not horror stories.

This is Volume Three of the Tabiji Field Guides series. Volume One is *The Dental Tourism Field Guide.* Volume Two is *The Cosmetic Surgery Field Guide.* Each of those volumes goes deep on a specific procedure category. This volume goes wide — covering the system, the decision framework, and the procedure categories at survey depth, with cross-references to the specialist volumes where deeper coverage exists. You can read this book first regardless of release order; the framework it teaches is the framework the specialist volumes apply.

The companion site lives at [tabiji.ai/book/medical-tourism](https://tabiji.ai/book/medical-tourism). You will find updated source links, downloadable worksheet PDFs, edition errata, and a curated reading list of primary sources.

Take the book at the pace your situation allows. If you have sixty days before you need to send a deposit, the chapters are laid out in the order to read them in. If you have less time, the Reading Paths page after the Scenario Index points to the chapters that match your situation. The work the book asks of you is real but finite. The work it saves you, on the most documented patterns of harm in cross-border medical care, is also real. We hope the trade is worth it.

The book covers medical tourism across many procedure categories — dental, cosmetic surgery, hair restoration, orthopedic surgery, bariatric surgery, cardiac surgery, ophthalmology, reproductive medicine, oncology, diagnostic and preventive care. Each category has its own risk profile. Cosmetic surgery can involve fat embolism (particularly associated with gluteal fat transfer / Brazilian Butt Lift, or BBL), capsular contracture, BIA-ALCL (breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma) surveillance considerations, anesthesia complications including aspiration and malignant hyperthermia, and post-operative mental health responses. Oncology decisions made abroad can include continuity-of-care complications that meaningfully affect outcomes. The full risk picture of any procedure you are considering must come from a qualified clinician who has examined you in person.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has documented patterns of harm in international medical care. Among these: 93 US-citizen deaths in the Dominican Republic between 2009 and 2022, with 92 percent involving gluteal fat transfer, primarily in non-accredited facilities (CDC MMWR 73(3)). The US Food and Drug Administration has issued repeated warnings about unapproved stem-cell products marketed for cosmetic and chronic-disease indications. The US State Department's annual Trafficking in Persons reports have documented organ-trafficking corridors. Medical tourism can add language barriers, distance from the treating clinic, different legal systems, documentation challenges, insurance exclusions, accreditation gaps, and difficulty obtaining follow-up care. ## The Five Rules

Five rules organize the work this book asks of you. They appear in every Tabiji Field Guide. They are not procedure-specific; they apply to any healthcare decision that crosses an international border.

**Rule 1 — Slow Down.** The most expensive decisions in medical tourism are the ones made fast. The clinic that needs your deposit today, the coordinator who says the surgical slot disappears at midnight, the influencer reel whose discount code expires in twenty-four hours — these are not clinical deadlines. They are sales deadlines. A good decision is made on a calendar you control, not one a coordinator drew up to close a deal.

**Rule 2 — Decode the Quote.** A complete medical quote names the procedure, the surgeon, the facility, the anesthesia provider, the implants or devices, the recovery support, the revision terms, and the exclusions. A quote that says "all-inclusive" without naming any of those is not a quote; it is a marketing line. Decoding the quote is the work that determines whether the price you have been given is the price you will pay.

**Rule 3 — Verify the Provider.** You can verify the surgeon. You can verify the facility's accreditation. You can verify the anesthesia provider. You can do this from your laptop, before you send a deposit, by searching the destination country's official register and the accreditation body's public directory. The credential alone does not guarantee a good outcome, but the absence of a verifiable credential is the single strongest predictor of patterns of harm documented in the CDC data.

**Rule 4 — Protect the Deposit.** A deposit is the moment your evaluation ends and your defense of your choice begins. Before that moment, you are a buyer. After it, you are invested. The book teaches you how to send the deposit only when it is genuinely warranted — and how to negotiate the terms so the deposit secures something specific and is refundable under named conditions.

**Rule 5 — Document Everything.** The records you take home determine whether the procedure abroad becomes ongoing care or a closed loop. The operative report, the anesthesia record, the implant patient cards, the discharge instructions, the follow-up schedule — these are not paperwork. They are the bridge between the clinic abroad and every clinician who may help you for the next two decades. Documentation is leverage in any complaint, claim, or revision conversation.

The five rules are framework. The chapters that follow are how to apply them to your specific situation. Worksheets in the back matter make each rule operational.

## Read This First — The 20-Minute Safety Pause

This page exists for the reader who is about to send a deposit, sign a contract, or board a plane. The next twenty minutes — the time it takes to read this page and answer eight questions — can prevent the most documented patterns of harm in cross-border medical care.

Answer each question honestly. Write the answer down. If you cannot answer yes to any question, the deposit, contract, or flight is premature.

**Question 1 — Do I have a written, surgeon-signed plan?**

Not a chat message from a coordinator. Not a quote attached to an email. A document, signed or stamped by the surgeon who will perform the procedure, listing the procedures, the operative time, the anesthesia type, the implants or materials if relevant, the expected recovery, and the exclusions. If you cannot produce that document right now, this question is no.

**Question 2 — Have I verified the surgeon's credentials in the destination country's official register?**

Not the surgeon's website. Not the clinic's marketing material. The official register maintained by the destination country's medical regulator. Each country has one; the names vary (the medical council in some, the ministry of health in others, a professional society in still others). The book's Chapter 16 names them by country. If you have not searched the official register and confirmed the surgeon by name and registration number, this question is no.

**Question 3 — Have I verified the anesthesia provider's credentials?**

For procedures that involve sedation, regional, or general anesthesia, the anesthesia provider's credentials matter as much as the surgeon's. The same verification process applies: name, credential type, registration number, country, public register. If you do not yet know who the anesthesia provider will be — if the clinic says "our team will manage it" without naming the provider — this question is no.

**Question 4 — Have I verified the facility's accreditation independently?**

The clinic told you they are accredited. Did you search the accreditation body's public directory yourself? Joint Commission International, AAAASF, ISO 9001, ISQua-recognized country bodies — each has a public directory. If you have not searched it directly and confirmed the clinic's name, accreditation date, and accreditation scope, this question is no.

**Question 5 — Have I obtained an independent second opinion?**

In-person at home, in-person at a destination second clinic, or via a formal telemedicine second-opinion program from a US academic medical center (Cleveland Clinic Online Medical Second Opinion, Mayo Clinic Online Second Opinion, MD Anderson, MSK International, Mass General Brigham International, Johns Hopkins International). The book's Chapter 15 covers the telemedicine options in detail. If your only opinion on the proposed procedure is from the clinic that will perform it, this question is no.

**Question 6 — Have I read the deposit refund policy?**

Read it. Read it twice. Translate "nonrefundable," "refundable with notice," "transferable," "subject to surgeon approval," and "applied to treatment" into plain English for your specific situation. Ask the clinic in writing what happens if you cancel 30 days before travel, if your flight is canceled two days before, if the surgeon recommends a different procedure after the in-person exam, if you are not medically cleared for surgery, if the clinic reschedules. If those answers are not in writing, this question is no.

**Question 7 — Do I have enough financial buffer to walk away?**

If you have to proceed because you have already paid the deposit, you have lost your most important piece of leverage. The deposit you can survive losing is the deposit that protects you from the procedure you will regret. If a single negative finding in the in-person exam — a candidacy issue, a missing pre-op clearance, a different surgeon than agreed — would put you in financial duress to walk away, the deposit is premature.

**Question 8 — For procedures with mental-health considerations, have I done the appropriate self-screen?**

For cosmetic surgery and certain body-contouring procedures, the BDDQ (Body Dysmorphic Disorder Questionnaire) is in Volume Two's Worksheet 12. For bariatric surgery, the pre-op psychological evaluation that ASMBS requires has not been replaced by anything an international clinic will accept; do the US-based evaluation. For any procedure undertaken within twelve months of a major adverse life event (death of a partner or parent, divorce, job loss, major diagnosis), the published outcome literature suggests pausing. If a screen flagged concern and you have not yet had a mental-health consultation, this question is no.

::: {.decision-gate}

**Pause if any of these are true:**

- The clinic will not name the treating surgeon or the anesthesia provider.
- The facility's accreditation cannot be verified in the public directory.
- The plan changed but the explanation is verbal only.
- Price changed without a written revised quote.
- You are told not to seek a second opinion.
- You are being pushed to sign or pay before understanding the plan.
- The timeline is built around your flight rather than your care.
- You are relying on influencer content more than documents.
- A demonstration-case discount is offered before clinical assessment.
- You would be financially trapped if complications, revisions, or delays occur.

Hope is important. It is not enough to sign consent.

## Introduction — The Seven Leverage Points and the Journey at a Glance

Every healthcare decision that crosses a border passes through seven leverage points. At each one, you have some power and some risk; at each one, you can preserve leverage or give it up. The decisions you make at the earliest leverage points have the biggest effect on the decisions available at the later ones. The book is organized around this sequence.

**Leverage Point 1 — Before the search.** The widest leverage. You have not yet contacted a clinic, not yet given anyone your contact information, not yet started receiving the daily messaging. You can still ask, *should I even be considering this?* Chapter 10 has the self-test that helps you answer.

**Leverage Point 2 — The funnel.** Once you have made first contact, the coordinator and the marketing funnel begin. The funnel has predictable moves — friendly responsiveness, photo requests, discount deadlines, social proof. Leverage starts dropping here, not because the funnel is necessarily bad but because you start to feel the pull of sunk cost (the time, the photos, the relationship). Chapter 6 names the funnel patterns.

**Leverage Point 3 — The destination decision.** You commit to a country or city or region. From here, the comparison set shrinks. Your leverage is still high but increasingly local. Chapter 17 covers how to evaluate destinations without falling into ranking traps.

**Leverage Point 4 — The quote.** A specific clinic delivers a specific quote. You can compare it against alternatives; you can read it line by line; you can negotiate. Chapter 19 is the general quote decoder.

**Leverage Point 5 — The deposit.** This is where leverage begins to fall sharply. Before the deposit, you are a buyer. After it, you are an invested customer who is increasingly motivated to defend the choice. Chapter 20 covers what to verify before the deposit and how to negotiate refundable terms.

**Leverage Point 6 — Travel and the in-person exam.** You are at the clinic. The plan may change. Pressure compounds — the slot is reserved, the deposit is paid, the flight is booked, the time-off is taken. Leverage is falling. The discipline at this point is to slow down, ask for the changes in writing, take a night to think before consent. Chapters in Part V (procedure-specific) walk this stage chapter by chapter.

**Leverage Point 7 — The procedure room.** The chair, the operating room, the IVF lab, the catheterization suite. The lowest leverage of the journey. The only useful leverage left is the right to refuse — to stand up, ask for time, decline sedation, walk out. Chapters in Part V and Part VI explain how to use that residual leverage if necessary.

# Part I — Before You Begin

## Chapter 1 — Two Million Decisions

Marcus is fifty-eight, a project manager at a Denver engineering firm, married twenty-six years, two grown sons, a knee that has hurt since 2011 and another that has hurt since 2019. The orthopedic appointment was this morning. The MRI confirmed what the surgeon had told him at the imaging-review visit two weeks ago: bilateral end-stage osteoarthritis. The surgeon recommended total knee arthroplasty in both knees, with the timing — staged six to eight weeks apart, or simultaneous in selected patients at high-volume centers — to be discussed once a surgical plan was finalized. The US estimate, after his employer's PPO insurance and the deductible cliff he has already cleared this year, is twenty-two thousand dollars out of pocket per knee. Some of the cost is the implant. Some is the surgical fee. Some is the hospital. Some is the physical therapy that follows. The estimate includes none of the time off work, the home-care help, the medications, or the second knee.

He is sitting at his kitchen table at eleven o'clock at night. His wife is asleep upstairs. The laptop is open. He has had three browser tabs running for two hours.

The first is Reddit, where r/MedicalTourism has eighty-four thousand subscribers and a pinned post from a moderator titled *"Please read before posting your knee/hip question."* The post is from 2022 and links to three other posts that are also from 2022, two of which point to a clinic in Costa Rica that has since changed its name twice. Marcus knows this because he has spent the last twenty minutes trying to track the actual current name and accreditation status.

The second is *Patients Beyond Borders,* a website whose pages on Costa Rica list four hospitals he can vaguely tell are not all the same kind of facility. He cannot quite figure out the difference between a JCI-accredited tertiary hospital in San José and Hospital Clínica Bíblica. He cannot quite figure out whether the JCI accreditation he keeps reading about is the same JCI accreditation the websites are showing.

The third is a Costa Rican facilitator who DM'd him on Instagram two days ago after he liked a post about knee replacement. The facilitator, whose name is something like *KneeNoMore* on the platform and *International Patient Concierge* in the email, has been very responsive. The current message in the WhatsApp thread is a quote: *$13,800 USD all-inclusive for bilateral TKA, includes accommodation, transfers, and follow-up. Surgeon Dr. ___ Board-certified. JCI hospital. Available next month.* The hospital name in the quote is one of the four on the Patients Beyond Borders page. The surgeon name has a Dr. in front of it but no specific verifiable credential beyond *Board-certified.*

Marcus drinks the last of the coffee. He looks at his wife's empty chair across the table. He has not told his brother. He has not told the friend he plays softball with on Wednesdays, who is also waiting for knee replacement. He bookmarks the three pages. He closes the laptop without booking anything.

If you are reading this book, it is probably because you were Marcus at some point this week.

### Two million Americans

The number of US patients who travel abroad for medical or dental care each year is between 1.4 and 2 million, according to the most-cited industry estimates and the CDC's Yellow Book chapter on medical tourism. The global figure is larger — 14 to 16 million international medical travelers per year, most of them not American, in an industry the trade press estimates at $74 to $92 billion annually. The growth rate has been 15 to 25 percent per year in pre-pandemic surveys, and has continued post-pandemic with different procedure mixes.

The two million Americans break down roughly like this, in declining order of volume: dental tourism, which accounts for somewhere between 600,000 and 900,000 annual cross-border patients; cosmetic surgery and hair restoration, 300,000 to 500,000; ophthalmology procedures, 100,000 to 200,000; diagnostic and preventive care including executive physicals and advanced imaging, 50,000 to 100,000; orthopedic surgery, 50,000 to 80,000; reproductive medicine and IVF, 30,000 to 60,000; bariatric surgery, 25,000 to 40,000; cardiac surgery, 10,000 to 20,000; and oncology, where the volume is hardest to estimate but the stakes are highest per-patient.

These are not small numbers. The combined US outbound for the eight procedure categories listed above represents a meaningful slice of US elective healthcare. Some of those decisions go well. Some go badly. The differences between the two outcomes are documented in the public health literature.

The patterns of harm are documented in specific datasets. The CDC's *Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report* volume 73, number 3 — a federal-government primary source — documented 93 deaths of US citizens undergoing cosmetic surgery in the Dominican Republic between 2009 and 2022. Ninety-two percent of those deaths involved gluteal fat transfer (Brazilian Butt Lift). The cause-of-death pattern was pulmonary fat embolism and venous thromboembolism. The pattern was concentrated in non-accredited facilities performing combination procedures on patients with comorbidities. The CDC has also documented *Mycobacterium abscessus* surgical site infection outbreaks among US patients receiving cosmetic surgery in the Dominican Republic, and similar surgical site infection patterns in other destinations. The State Department's annual Trafficking in Persons report has documented organ-trafficking corridors in China, Pakistan, Egypt, and several other jurisdictions. The FDA has issued repeated warnings about unapproved stem-cell products marketed for cosmetic and chronic-disease indications. The National Cancer Institute has documented harm patterns from unapproved alternative cancer therapies.

None of this means medical tourism is categorically dangerous. Most of the 1.4 to 2 million annual US medical travelers come home satisfied, with the procedure they expected, the cost savings they planned for, and the recovery their surgeon predicted. The 93-death cluster in the Dominican Republic represents a tiny fraction of the US patients who traveled there during the same thirteen-year period. The patterns of harm are real and documented; they are also patterns, not destinies, and patterns can be avoided if you know what they look like.

That is what this book is for.

### Three things this book is not

This book is not a destination directory. Earlier industry-standard books — *Patients Beyond Borders* in its various editions, *Patients Without Borders*, *The Medical Traveler's Guide* — have done destination-directory work well. They list hospitals by country and procedure. We do not list them. We do not rank them. We do not need to; the directories already exist.

This book is not a hospital ranking. Hospital rankings, when they exist in medical tourism, are based on indicators that are either marketed (testimonials, before-and-after photographs, social media volume) or weakly correlated with patient outcomes (accreditation status alone, hospital size, technology brand). We name the patterns the facilitator industry produces (Chapter 6); we do not name specific facilitators.

## Chapter 2 — The Four Kinds of Medical Tourism

People travel for medical care for four reasons. Most readers are in the first category; the framework is the same for all four, but the failure modes vary.

### Cost-driven

The largest segment by volume. Marcus is here. The US has the highest healthcare prices in the world, by every measure that has been compared (OECD health statistics; published US-vs-international price comparison studies in *Health Affairs*, the *Journal of the American Medical Association*, and the *International Journal for Quality in Health Care*). For specific procedures, the differential is enormous. Bilateral knee replacement in the US: $40,000 to $60,000 retail, $20,000 to $25,000 out-of-pocket on a typical PPO plan after the deductible cliff. Bilateral knee replacement in Costa Rica at a JCI-accredited facility: $14,000 to $18,000 all-inclusive. Dental implant + crown in the US: $4,500 to $6,500 per tooth. Dental implant + crown in Mexico: $1,200 to $2,500. The savings on a full-mouth rehabilitation can be $40,000 or more.

For most cost-driven medical tourists, the trigger is a specific quote at home that they cannot pay. The trigger is usually an insurance denial, a high-deductible plan that has not yet hit the deductible, a deductible-cliff calculation that makes the procedure unaffordable this year, or a procedure the US insurance simply does not cover (most cosmetic surgery; some dental work; some IVF cycles).

The failure modes for cost-driven readers tend to be panic-driven. The decision is rushed. The framework is skipped because the framework feels like overhead. The 60-day plan is collapsed into a 14-day plan, then a 7-day plan, then a same-week decision. Most documented patterns of harm in the medical tourism literature involve cost-driven patients who skipped the framework.

This is why the book is written first to the cost-driven reader. If the framework works for the most pressured reader segment, it works for everyone.

### Access-driven

The reader's insurance does not cover the procedure, the wait list at home is long, the specialist concentration at home is thin, or US capacity is constrained. Bariatric surgery insurance coverage in the US is patchy by employer and by state; many qualifying patients cannot get a covered procedure within a reasonable timeline. Some lower-volume specialty procedures — certain spine reconstructions, some structural cardiac interventions, certain rare-disease specialty care — have wait lists in the US that medical tourism can shortcut. Some elective procedures (a specific kind of refractive eye surgery, certain dermatologic treatments) are not available in the US at all because the FDA has not approved the specific platform.

The access-driven reader is often less panicked than the cost-driven reader. They have time. The failure mode for access-driven readers tends to be a different one: they assume that because the procedure is available abroad and not at home, the destination clinic must be qualified to perform it. This is sometimes true and sometimes not. The framework — verify the provider, decode the quote, document everything — applies equally.

### Expertise-driven

A specific surgeon or technique is concentrated abroad. The classic example: Brazilian plastic surgeons concentrated certain body contouring techniques in São Paulo and Rio that US-trained surgeons later adopted. The Indian cardiac surgery corridor — Apollo Hospitals, Narayana Health, Fortis Healthcare — has high-volume centers performing CABG (coronary artery bypass grafting) and valve procedures at outcomes comparable to top US tertiary centers, at fractional cost. Belgian hip resurfacing technique was concentrated in a few European centers for years. Spanish refractive surgery has been an established subspecialty corridor.

For expertise-driven medical tourism, the patient is often well-informed before they begin and the framework adapts accordingly. The verification step is critical: the *named* surgeon must be the one performing the procedure, not a colleague at the same facility. Hospital privileges and society membership matter more than for cost-driven medical tourism. The independent second opinion is often best obtained from a US specialist who knows the international corridor and can speak to the specific technique's evidence base.

### Niche-driven

Procedures not legal, not available, or only available under expanded access in the US. Certain IVF configurations involving sex selection, certain donor anonymity arrangements, certain experimental cancer immunotherapies under expanded access in non-US jurisdictions. The legal and ethical landscape varies dramatically by destination. The framework's discipline is still the same; the questions are sometimes harder because the regulatory frame at home and at the destination are different.

### Why this taxonomy matters

The four kinds overlap in any individual patient. Marcus is primarily cost-driven; he is also slightly access-driven (his ortho surgeon's earliest available slot is three months out). Priya, whom we meet in Chapter 16, is primarily access-driven (her mother's cardiologist in Mumbai is a family relationship of decades) and partly cost-driven (US cardiac procedures are out of reach without coverage). The four-fold taxonomy is a way of locating yourself in the medical-tourism landscape; the framework that follows applies whatever combination you find yourself in.

The chapters that follow assume the cost-driven reader as default and call out where the other categories require different treatment.

## Chapter 3 — Why Prices Differ Across Borders

US healthcare prices are the highest in the world. The differential between US prices and prices at top international tertiary centers ranges from 2x to 10x depending on procedure category. Why?

Three factors do most of the work. Two more factors do the rest. Understanding the price differential matters because most failure modes of medical tourism involve a patient assuming the differential is *purely* a cost-of-labor difference, when it is partly that and partly several other things.

### Factor 1 — Labor cost

The largest single component. US specialist physician wages are the highest in the world. The Medical Group Management Association (MGMA) annual compensation survey reports median total compensation in 2023 of approximately $650,000 for orthopedic surgeons, $720,000 for cardiothoracic surgeons, $510,000 for plastic surgeons, $400,000 for ophthalmologists, $770,000 for neurosurgeons. International equivalents, from peer-reviewed and *Medscape International* compensation surveys: median orthopedic surgeon compensation in India is approximately $60,000, in Mexico approximately $120,000, in Thailand approximately $80,000, in Costa Rica approximately $110,000.

Nurse-staffing differentials propagate the effect. US RN median: approximately $80,000. India: approximately $8,000. Mexico: approximately $15,000. Thailand: approximately $12,000.

When the staff of a surgical operation costs 80 percent less, the all-in cost of the operation falls accordingly, even when implants and supplies are at international parity (because the medical device companies are global and charge globally — see Factor 4 below).

### Factor 2 — Regulatory and liability cost

US malpractice premiums are the highest in the world by an order of magnitude. The Medical Liability Monitor's annual survey reports US malpractice premiums by specialty in the range of $25,000 to $200,000 per surgeon per year, varying by specialty and state. International equivalents are typically 5 to 20 percent of US levels. The tort cost embedded in US healthcare pricing — every CABG, every knee replacement, every cosmetic procedure — is one of the largest non-labor cost differentials between US and international care.

US regulatory compliance overhead for surgical facilities is also high. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) Conditions of Participation and the Joint Commission accreditation requirements impose substantial documentation, staffing, and process costs. International facilities at top JCI-accredited tertiary centers run with comparable safety standards but lower bureaucratic overhead. Some of this is genuine efficiency. Some of it reflects different regulatory philosophies. None of it should be confused with "international care has lower standards" — JCI-accredited international tertiary centers operate to the same accreditation standard as Joint Commission-accredited US hospitals, because JCI and TJC are the same parent organization.

### Factor 3 — Currency and purchasing power

Purchasing power parity (PPP) effects are substantial. India is approximately 3.5x cheaper than the US for equivalent goods and services on a PPP basis; Mexico is approximately 2x cheaper; Thailand is approximately 2.5x cheaper; Turkey approximately 3x; Costa Rica approximately 1.8x. The PPP-adjusted local cost of providing a procedure — staff, facilities, food, utilities — is correspondingly lower.

Currency exchange volatility is a real consideration for medical tourism budgeting. A 5 to 15 percent move in USD versus the destination currency within a procedure-planning window is common and shifts deposit math, the all-in cost, and the comparison to home-country pricing.

### Factor 4 — Technology and capital costs

This is where the differentials narrow. The major medical technology platforms — surgical robots from Intuitive (da Vinci) and Stryker (Mako, ROSA), imaging systems from GE and Siemens, implants from Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, Johnson & Johnson DePuy Synthes, Smith + Nephew, Mentor, Sientra, Allergan — are global. The same products are sold in the US and at top international tertiary centers at similar prices. Capital amortization differs because international centers often have higher patient volume per unit of capital, but the underlying technology cost is comparable.

This is why orthopedic medical tourism at a top tertiary center abroad delivers the same implant (the same Stryker Triathlon knee, the same Zimmer Persona) as a US procedure — at one-third to two-thirds the all-in cost. The implant cost is similar; the labor cost and the regulatory cost are not.

### Factor 5 — The bundled-vs-unbundled pricing pattern

This is the most operationally important factor for a patient. US healthcare is overwhelmingly *unbundled*. The surgeon bills separately. The hospital bills separately. The anesthesiologist bills separately. The implant manufacturer often bills separately. The post-op physical therapy bills separately. Each component has its own price and its own insurance treatment.

International medical tourism is overwhelmingly *bundled*. The international patient department quotes a single all-inclusive price that covers everything that happens in the destination — surgical fee, hospital fee, anesthesia, implant, hospital stay, even (sometimes) the airport transfer and the hotel.

This is why a Costa Rican TKA quoted at "$14,000 all-inclusive" can be honestly compared to a US procedure that totals $42,000 across unbundled components. The bundling difference does not change the labor cost or the regulatory cost or the implant cost; it changes the *visibility* of those costs to the patient.

A specific implication: the patient who compares the US surgeon-fee alone ($8,000 for a TKA, a common figure) to the Costa Rican bundled price ($14,000) and concludes there are no real savings has compared the wrong numbers. The fair comparison is the all-in US cost (typically $30,000 to $50,000 retail; $15,000 to $25,000 out-of-pocket on PPO insurance after deductible) versus the all-in Costa Rican cost ($14,000 all-inclusive). The savings are real. They are just bundled.

### The international patient department pricing pattern

Most top international tertiary centers — Apollo, Bumrungrad, Anadolu Medical Center, Hospital CIMA, Hospital Clínica Bíblica, Memorial Hermann International, and others — operate dedicated International Patient Departments with pricing structures distinct from local-patient pricing. International patient pricing is typically 20 to 60 percent higher than local pricing at the same facility. The reasons are mostly defensible: international patients require more concierge service, more translation, more billing handholding, more documentation in English, more comprehensive follow-up arrangement. Some of the markup is harder to defend; it is what the market bears.

The implication for the patient: the price you are quoted as an international patient is not the same price a local patient pays for the same procedure at the same facility. This is fine and standard. It is also worth knowing, particularly for diaspora patients who may have access to local-patient pricing through family relationships and may not realize they could.

### The price differential is real but smaller than the headline figures suggest

The "save 70 to 90 percent" claims in medical-tourism marketing materials use the wrong comparison. They compare the international all-inclusive price to the US retail price (the price an uninsured cash patient sees on a bill, which almost nobody actually pays). They do not compare to the US out-of-pocket cost after insurance, which is the relevant comparison for most readers.

A more honest figure for cost-driven medical tourism, after Factor 1 through Factor 5 work themselves out: 30 to 60 percent savings on the all-in cost, with significant variation by procedure and destination. Some procedures (dental, cosmetic) deliver larger savings; some (cardiac, complex spine) deliver smaller but still meaningful savings. The Worksheet 1 True Cost Calculator in the back matter helps you do the comparison for your specific situation.

This is the work of decoding the cost differential: not assuming the headline figure, doing the math for your situation, and arriving at the *true* differential rather than the marketed one.

## Chapter 4 — The Accreditation Landscape

A verifiable accreditation is the single best baseline signal of whether a facility operates to a recognizable international standard. It is also one of the most misused marketing words in medical tourism. Understanding the accreditation landscape — what each body does, what each evaluates, and how to verify a claim — is one of the foundational skills the book teaches.

### Joint Commission International (JCI)

The most-cited and most-rigorous international hospital accreditation body. JCI was founded in 1994 as the international affiliate of The Joint Commission (TJC), the dominant US hospital accreditation body. Both organizations have the same parent.

JCI evaluates hospitals against a published standard — currently the JCI Accreditation Standards for Hospitals, 7th edition — that contains approximately 300 measurable elements covering governance, quality improvement, patient rights, medication management, surgical safety, infection prevention, emergency response, and facility management. A JCI accreditation requires the hospital to pass an on-site survey by a JCI-trained team. Accreditation is granted for three years, after which reaccreditation requires a new survey.

JCI currently lists more than 1,000 accredited organizations worldwide, of which approximately 600 are hospitals. The public directory is at *jointcommissioninternational.org*. The directory shows the facility name, the city, the country, the accreditation date, and the current accreditation status. You can search by country to see all accredited facilities in a destination.

What JCI accreditation does *not* evaluate: individual physician outcomes, individual case quality, surgeon volume, specific procedure quality, or long-term patient outcomes. JCI evaluates the *system* — the processes, the documentation, the safety culture, the equipment. The surgeon you will have at a JCI-accredited hospital is not directly evaluated by JCI; the hospital's process for credentialing that surgeon is evaluated.

This is the single most important caveat in the chapter. JCI accreditation is necessary, not sufficient. A JCI-accredited hospital has met an internationally recognized baseline; it has not guaranteed that any specific procedure performed there will succeed.

### AAAASF (American Association for Accreditation of Ambulatory Surgery Facilities)

US-based, with international scope. AAAASF accredits ambulatory surgical facilities — outpatient surgery centers. It is more common for cosmetic surgery centers (the same type of facility that performs much of US-domestic cosmetic surgery) and less common for full-service hospitals. The standards are well-developed; the public directory is at *aaaasf.org*.

AAAASF is particularly relevant for medical tourism in the cosmetic surgery and outpatient categories. For inpatient surgery (cardiac, major orthopedic, oncology), AAAASF is less applicable; you want hospital-level accreditation like JCI.

### ISO 9001

A general quality-management system standard, not healthcare-specific. ISO 9001 certification is sometimes carried by international hospitals; some marketing material conflates ISO 9001 with healthcare accreditation. They are not the same. ISO 9001 evaluates whether the organization has a documented quality management system; it does not evaluate clinical processes, surgical safety, or patient outcomes.

ISO 9001 alone is insufficient as healthcare accreditation. A facility that advertises "ISO 9001 certified" without also holding JCI, AAAASF, or a recognized country-level accreditation is leaving most of the accreditation work undone.

### ISQua — the International Society for Quality in Health Care

ISQua does not accredit hospitals directly. It accredits the *accreditors* — it evaluates whether national accreditation bodies meet an international meta-standard. ISQua-recognized accreditation bodies are a quality signal because they have themselves been evaluated.

ISQua-recognized bodies include JCI, ACHSI (Australia), Trent Accreditation Scheme (UK), Accreditation Canada International, NABH (India), CHKS (UK), Det Norske Veritas (Norway), and several others. If a facility carries accreditation from an ISQua-recognized body, that is a meaningful credential. If a facility carries accreditation from a body that is not ISQua-recognized — including some country-level bodies in destinations that lack mature regulatory frameworks — the accreditation may or may not be meaningful, and additional verification is warranted.

### Country-level national accreditation bodies

Each major medical-tourism destination has a national accreditation body. Their stringency varies; the ISQua recognition tier is the most useful single check.

- **India:** NABH (National Accreditation Board for Hospitals and Healthcare Providers). ISQua-recognized. Directory at *nabh.co*.
- **Australia:** ACHSI (Australian Council on Healthcare Standards International). Operates internationally; ISQua-recognized.
- **United Kingdom:** Trent Accreditation Scheme.
- **Canada:** Accreditation Canada International. Operates internationally.
- **Thailand:** Healthcare Accreditation Institute (HAI). ISQua-recognized.
- **Mexico:** Consejo de Salubridad General (CSG) certification.
- **Turkey:** Ministry of Health licensing plus occasional JCI overlay.

For each destination, the book recommends searching the public directory of the relevant body to verify the facility's accreditation status. The Worksheet 14 (Accreditation Verification Checklist) in the back matter has the directory URLs and the verification steps.

### What accreditation does not evaluate

This bears repeating because it is the largest single misunderstanding among medical-tourism patients:

- Individual surgeon outcomes or surgical volume.
- Specific procedure quality.
- Long-term patient outcomes.
- Marketing claims made by the facility.
- The competence of any specific physician on the facility's roster.

What it does evaluate is the *system* the facility operates — the credentialing process for surgeons, the surgical safety protocols, the infection prevention practices, the medication management, the emergency response capability, the documentation and quality improvement.

A JCI-accredited hospital with a poorly-credentialed individual surgeon can still produce a bad outcome for a specific case. A non-accredited facility with an extraordinary individual surgeon may produce good outcomes despite the system gap. But the *base rate* of preventable harm in non-accredited facilities is meaningfully higher than in accredited facilities, in every dataset the book draws from.

### The accreditation theater problem

Some facilities advertise accreditation they do not hold. Some advertise accreditation that has lapsed. Some use language that *implies* accreditation without naming a specific body — "internationally accredited," "European-standard facility," "trained to American standards," and so on.

None of this language has regulatory meaning. The only verification path that works is direct: search the accreditation body's public directory. If the facility is in the directory with current status, the accreditation is real. If it is not, the marketing language is not enough.

::: {.decision-gate}

Do not send a deposit to a facility whose accreditation you have not verified independently in the relevant accreditation body's public directory. The verification takes ten minutes. The cost of skipping it is the difference between buyer protection and marketing-based decision-making.

:::

*A reusable copy is Worksheet 14 (Accreditation Verification Checklist) in the back matter.*

### The verification process

For any facility you are considering:

1. Identify the accreditation the facility claims (JCI, AAAASF, NABH, ISO 9001, ACHSI, HAI, etc.).
2. Identify the accreditation body's public directory URL. Most are easy to find — JCI, NABH, AAAASF, HAI all have public directories.
3. Search the directory for the facility name. Confirm the name matches, the accreditation date is current, and the accreditation scope covers what you need.
4. If the accreditation is from a body that is not ISQua-recognized, check what that body actually evaluates. Some are real; some are window dressing.
5. If the facility advertises accreditation but does not appear in the relevant directory, the accreditation is not verifiable. The next question is whether the facility's other credentials (surgeon credentialing, regulator licensing, peer-reviewed outcome publications) make up for the gap. Often they do not.

This is not a difficult exercise. It is a ten-minute task per facility. It is also one of the strongest single signals the book recommends.

## Chapter 5 — Insurance and the Cross-Border Claim

The default position of US health insurance on elective medical care abroad is no coverage. This is the single most important fact for any reader to understand before making medical tourism financial assumptions.

### The default

US private health insurance: elective care abroad is not covered. The default position across all major US payers (UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, Cigna, Anthem, Humana, Blue Cross Blue Shield plans) is that non-emergency medical care performed outside the United States is not a covered benefit. There are narrow exceptions, listed below, but the default holds for the vast majority of medical-tourism scenarios.

US Medicare: very limited coverage for out-of-country care. Specific narrow exceptions exist for emergency care in border areas, for emergencies on ships within 6 hours of US ports, and for a few rare circumstances involving immediate need. Elective care abroad is not covered. Medicare Advantage plans (Part C) sometimes offer limited international travel benefits but typically only for emergencies, not elective procedures.

US Medicaid: varies by state but is generally not available for elective care abroad. Medicaid is a state-administered program with federal funding; state rules differ but the default is the same.

### The narrow exceptions

There are five exceptions worth knowing about. Most readers will not qualify for any of them.

**Exception 1 — Emergency care abroad.** Most US private insurance covers emergencies that arise while traveling, *as long as* the travel was not specifically for the medical procedure. Travel medical insurance can be purchased as a supplement for additional coverage. This does not cover planned medical tourism — the elective procedure is not an emergency.

**Exception 2 — Reconstructive care after mastectomy.** The Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act (WHCRA, 1998) requires US insurers to cover reconstruction after mastectomy, including reconstruction on the contralateral breast for symmetry. The statute does not specifically require out-of-country coverage; whether a US insurer will reimburse for reconstruction performed abroad depends on the specific plan. Verify with your insurer directly.

**Exception 3 — Complications of a procedure performed abroad.** Most US insurance will cover complications of a procedure performed abroad as a *separate medical event* in the US. This means: if you have surgery in Costa Rica and a complication arises after you return home, the treatment of the complication is typically covered, even though the original surgery was not. The original surgery costs are still your responsibility; the complication is treated as new US medical care.

**Exception 4 — Functional procedures with cosmetic components.** Septoplasty, panniculectomy after massive weight loss, certain blepharoplasty cases with documented functional impairment — these may be covered if the functional indication is documented. The cosmetic component is typically not covered. This is procedure-specific and depends on the specific plan and the documentation.

**Exception 5 — Self-insured employer plans (ERISA) that explicitly include medical tourism.** A small but growing number of US employers self-insure (rather than purchasing a fully-insured plan) and explicitly include cross-border medical tourism options in their plan design. Walmart, Lowe's, and a few others have been cited in trade press. Most self-insured plans still default to no coverage for elective care abroad. Check your specific plan document.

### The UCR / alternate-benefit / out-of-network reimbursement gap

When out-of-network or out-of-country care is reimbursed, payment is typically calculated against US Usual, Customary, and Reasonable (UCR) rates for the procedure, not the foreign invoice amount. This is the most underappreciated reimbursement issue in medical tourism.

A specific example. A US patient has cosmetic-related breast reconstruction performed in Costa Rica for $12,000, and her insurance plan covers reconstruction after mastectomy. The plan's UCR rate for the procedure in her zip code is $6,000. The plan covers 50 percent of the UCR rate for out-of-network or out-of-country care. The reimbursement is $3,000, against a $12,000 invoice.

"Yes, we cover it" rarely means "yes, we will pay a meaningful share." Ask your insurer for the specific schedule, percentage, and out-of-network rules that would apply *before* you assume your insurance contributes to your medical-tourism cost. Get the dollar figure, not just the percentage.

### Travel medical insurance vs. cosmetic-tourism complication insurance

These are two distinct products, and confusing them is a common and expensive mistake.

**Travel medical insurance** covers trip cancellation, interruption, lost baggage, and emergencies that arise during travel and are *unrelated to* the planned medical care. Most standard travel-medical policies explicitly exclude complications related to elective procedures the trip was organized around. Coverage tiers range from $50,000 to $1,000,000 in emergency medical evacuation and emergency medical care; pricing is roughly $50 to $300 per trip depending on age, destination, and coverage tier.

**Cosmetic-tourism complication insurance** is a separate, specialized product. A handful of providers (CosmetAssure historically, now bundled into broader surgical-complication carriers; international hospital-issued products through Bumrungrad and similar) offer policies that cover complication treatment, return travel, and sometimes revision surgery if a covered complication occurs within a defined window after the original procedure. Coverage is procedure-specific and has meaningful exclusions: the original surgery must be at an approved facility, complications from off-label drug use are excluded, BBL is sometimes excluded entirely or carries a separate higher premium, and pre-existing conditions you did not disclose can void coverage. Premium ranges from $200 to $2,000 depending on procedure category and coverage tier.

For most medical tourism, the right combination is standard travel medical insurance (because you are traveling, you need it for non-medical-tourism emergencies and trip disruption) plus, for higher-complexity procedures, the cosmetic-tourism complication insurance product if available. Verify what each covers in writing before you travel.

### Country-of-care complication insurance offered by destination hospitals

Some destination hospitals (Bumrungrad in Thailand, Apollo Hospitals in India, and others) offer their own complication insurance products to international patients. Coverage typically applies for a defined window (commonly 90 days) and is limited to complications treatable at the originating hospital. These products vary widely in scope; read the policy. They can be useful as a supplement, but they are not a substitute for travel medical insurance or for verifying that your US insurance will cover return-home complications.

### What this means operationally

For most readers, the operating assumption should be:

- US insurance will not pay for elective medical tourism. Plan as if the cost is fully out of pocket.
- US insurance may cover return-home complications if they arise. The original procedure cost is yours.
- Travel medical insurance is worth carrying for any international travel, including medical tourism, but understand its scope is non-elective emergencies.
- Cosmetic-tourism complication insurance is worth considering for higher-complexity procedures (cosmetic surgery, BBL, complex bariatric) but read the exclusions carefully.

## Chapter 6 — The Facilitator Industry

The medical-tourism facilitator industry is a large, mostly unregulated business that sits between the patient and the destination hospital. Understanding how facilitators work — their business models, their incentive structures, and the patterns they produce — is one of the more important pieces of buyer-protection knowledge.

### What facilitators do

A medical-tourism facilitator is a coordinator, agency, or concierge service that arranges some or all of the following for the patient: initial clinic introduction, communication between patient and clinic, pre-trip logistics (visa, accommodation, transfers), in-destination support (translation, escort to appointments, ground transportation), aftercare coordination (return-flight logistics, US follow-up referrals), and complication management. Some facilitators do all of this. Some do only the initial coordination and let the patient handle logistics. Some specialize in specific procedure categories (cosmetic surgery facilitators, dental tourism facilitators, fertility tourism facilitators).

The facilitator can be useful, particularly for first-time medical tourists or for patients who do not speak the destination language. A good facilitator handles logistics that would otherwise be a major time sink, smooths communication where language barriers exist, and provides on-the-ground support during the trip.

The facilitator can also be a problem, particularly when the facilitator's economic incentives are misaligned with the patient's clinical interests. The book describes the patterns that produce both outcomes.

### The four facilitator business models

Facilitator economics fall into four general categories. The categories matter because they predict the conflicts.

**1. Commission-based.** The most common model. The facilitator receives a percentage (typically 10 to 25 percent) of the patient's procedure cost from the destination clinic. The patient pays the clinic; the clinic pays the facilitator. The patient may not realize a commission is being paid; the facilitator may be required to disclose it under FTC endorsement guidelines (16 CFR Part 255) but enforcement is inconsistent and many disclosures are buried in the fine print.

The conflict: the facilitator's recommendation correlates with their commission, not with the patient's outcome. Facilitators often have a small number of partner clinics with which they have pre-negotiated commission arrangements; "choice" presented to the patient is often constrained by which clinic produces the highest commission for the facilitator.

**2. Retainer-based.** The patient pays the facilitator a flat fee for services (logistics, coordination). The facilitator is paid by the patient, not the clinic. This is a cleaner conflict structure; the facilitator's incentive is to deliver the services the patient hired them for. It is also less common because patients are typically more willing to pay nothing (commission model) than to write a check for the facilitator's services.

**3. Hospital-employed coordinator.** The "coordinator" is an employee of the destination hospital's international patient department. Their job is patient acquisition for that hospital. Cheapest for the patient (the hospital absorbs the cost as part of its International Patient Department overhead), and maximum conflict — the coordinator's professional incentive is to convince the patient to book at this specific hospital.

**4. Transparent-fee facilitator.** The facilitator charges the patient a stated fee for services, takes no commission from the clinic, and discloses the structure openly. This is the rarest model. It tends to be associated with higher patient cost and higher trust.

### Regulatory landscape

The US FTC's endorsement guidelines (16 CFR Part 255) require disclosure of material commercial relationships in endorsements and testimonials. The guidelines apply to medical-tourism facilitators when they recommend specific clinics; enforcement against facilitators has been inconsistent.

The US Medical Tourism Association is a trade body (not a regulator) that represents facilitators and destination hospitals. It publishes a code of ethics and offers a certification program. Membership in or certification by the MTA is a signal, not a guarantee.

Most destination countries have no regulatory framework specific to medical-tourism facilitators. Facilitators are often operating in regulatory gray zones in both the source country (US) and the destination.

### Useful facilitator cases

A facilitator can be genuinely useful for:

- **Logistics-only services.** Airport transfer, accommodation booking, in-destination ground transportation, translation services. These are commodity services with clear scope and no clinical implications.
- **Cultural and language mediation.** A facilitator who is fluent in the destination's language and culture can smooth communication, particularly for first-time medical tourists or non-native speakers of the destination's language.
- **Aftercare coordination.** A facilitator who manages the return-flight logistics, the US follow-up referral, and the records-packet handoff can save the patient meaningful time and reduce the risk of post-procedure logistical failures.

For each of these, the facilitator's job is operational, not clinical. The conflict-of-interest risk is low.

### Harmful facilitator patterns

A facilitator can be a problem when:

- **The facilitator becomes the single point of contact between the patient and the clinic.** This is the "concierge trap." All information about the surgeon, the procedure, the recovery, the records — everything flows through the facilitator. The patient never speaks directly to the surgeon, never receives written information that did not come through the facilitator's filter. The framework breaks down because verification depends on direct contact.
- **The facilitator refuses to disclose how they are compensated.** A facilitator who cannot or will not tell you whether they are paid by the clinic or by you is hiding a conflict.
- **The facilitator pressures the deposit timing.** The deposit calendar is a sales artifact. If the facilitator is the one creating the pressure, the facilitator's incentives are not the patient's.
- **The facilitator presents only one clinic as the option.** A facilitator who "specializes" in a single destination clinic is essentially a commissioned sales agent for that clinic, not a neutral coordinator.
- **The facilitator discourages independent second opinions.** A facilitator who says "you don't need a second opinion; this clinic's surgeon is the best" is not facilitating; they are selling.

### How to evaluate a facilitator

The Worksheet 8 (Coordinator Log) in the back matter is the operational tool, but the questions to ask before engaging a facilitator are:

1. How are you compensated for arranging my care? (Commission from the clinic, retainer from me, employed by the clinic, transparent fee — which one?)
2. How many clinics do you work with in this destination? If only one, why?
3. Will I have direct contact with the surgeon before deposit?
4. What records will I have direct access to (without going through you)?
5. If I want an independent second opinion, will you arrange or impede it?
6. What is your involvement in aftercare and follow-up?

Answers to these questions sort the useful facilitators from the harmful ones quickly.

# Part II — Knowing Your Own Decision

## Chapter 7 — The Five Rules

The five rules organize the work the book asks of you. They are not procedure-specific. They apply to any healthcare decision that crosses an international border.

### Rule 1 — Slow Down

The most expensive decisions in medical tourism are the ones made fast. Every documented pattern of harm in the published literature on medical-tourism complications involves time compression. The CDC's Dominican Republic cluster of 93 deaths is concentrated in patients who consented to procedures within hours or days of arriving, often after the in-person exam revealed candidacy or anesthesia issues the patient was talked past. The published BBL mortality literature (1 in 3,000 to 1 in 6,241 mortality rate per the South Florida case series) tracks the same pattern of compressed decision timelines.

The discipline is operational. Three forms.

**The 20-Minute Safety Pause.** The eight-question pause before any deposit, contract, or flight. It is in the front matter for a reason; reread it whenever you are about to commit something. The questions take twenty minutes to answer honestly. If a coordinator's calendar cannot accommodate twenty minutes, the coordinator's calendar is not a clinical schedule; it is a sales schedule.

**The 60-Day Preparation Plan.** Chapter 11 is the full version. The core: most medical-tourism decisions become substantially better if you take eight weeks rather than two. Most can be made in less time than you fear and more time than the funnel suggests. The plan is the operational form of slowing down.

**The Overnight Rule.** When a plan changes — at the in-person exam, at the consent table, at any decision point — the answer is to sleep on it. One night minimum. The clinic that cannot accommodate one night is telling you something about how the morning will go.

The phrase to keep in mind, from the prior volumes of this series: *I am not saying no. I am asking for the information I need before I say yes.*

### Rule 2 — Decode the Quote

A complete medical-tourism quote answers, in writing, before deposit, the following questions:

- **What procedures are being quoted?** Named by their clinical names, not their marketing names ("Hollywood Smile," "Complete Body Transformation Package," "Heart Care Package").
- **Who is the named surgeon?** Full legal name, board certification, country of practice, registration number in the destination's official register.
- **Who is the named anesthesia provider?** Same level of detail, for any procedure requiring sedation or general anesthesia.
- **Which facility?** Full legal name, address, accreditation status (which body, current accreditation date).
- **What implants or materials?** Manufacturer, model, size range, source.
- **What is the operative-time estimate?** And the anesthesia-time estimate. These are not the same number for combination cases.
- **What is included and what is excluded?** Pre-op evaluation, post-op follow-up, garments, drains, medications, complication management, revision policy.
- **What is the currency, the payment method, and the refund policy?**
- **What records will I receive before departure?**

A quote that does not contain these elements is not complete. The Worksheet 2 (Cross-Procedure Quote Comparison) in the back matter is the operational format.

The reason this rule exists: the "all-inclusive" framing of medical-tourism quotes is what makes the cost comparison to US care look favorable. The same framing also creates room for cost escalation on arrival. A complete written quote, by being complete, makes escalation harder.

### Rule 3 — Verify the Provider

You can verify the surgeon. You can verify the anesthesia provider. You can verify the facility's accreditation. You can do all of this from your laptop, in less than thirty minutes per provider, before you send a deposit.

The verification path:

1. **Identify the official register.** Each country has a medical regulator that maintains a register of licensed physicians. The names vary — the Medical Council of India, the Cédula Profesional registry in Mexico, the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Surgeons, the General Medical Council in the UK, the Medical Council of Thailand. Chapter 16 names them by country.
2. **Search the register by name.** Confirm the surgeon by full legal name. Note the specialty, the board certifications, and the registration number.
3. **Verify the specialty matches the procedure.** A surgeon registered in general surgery is not a board-certified plastic surgeon. A surgeon registered as a cardiologist is not a cardiothoracic surgeon.
4. **Note the international society memberships.** ISAPS (International Society of Aesthetic Plastic Surgery) for plastic surgeons, AAOS (American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons) for orthopedic surgeons, STS (Society of Thoracic Surgeons) for cardiothoracic surgeons, ASMBS (American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery) for bariatric surgeons, and so on. The international societies are not regulators, but their membership requirements often include the country-equivalent of board certification.
5. **Search for the facility in the accreditation body's directory.** JCI, AAAASF, NABH, HAI, etc. Confirm the facility name, the accreditation date, the scope of accreditation.
6. **Document what you find.** Worksheet 5 (Specialist Credential Worksheet) records this. The documentation matters in any future complaint, claim, or revision conversation.

The verification is not a guarantee. A credentialed surgeon at an accredited facility can still produce a bad outcome. But the absence of verifiable credentials is the single strongest predictor of patterns of harm in the documented data. If the credential cannot be verified, the answer is no.

### Rule 4 — Protect the Deposit

A deposit changes the patient's posture from buyer to invested customer. Before the deposit, you are evaluating. After it, you are defending the choice. The framework changes accordingly.

The discipline is to send a deposit only when:

- You have a written, surgeon-signed plan in hand.
- You have verified the surgeon, the anesthesia provider, and the facility.
- You have an independent second opinion (Chapter 14 or 15).
- You have read the refund policy in writing and understand it.
- You can survive losing the deposit if the in-person exam reveals a problem.

The Worksheet 3 (Deposit Readiness Test) in the back matter is the operational form — 15 questions you must answer yes to before deposit. If you cannot answer yes to all 15, the deposit is premature.

The documented pattern here is unambiguous. Cost-driven medical-tourism failures concentrate at the deposit stage. Once the money is sent, the patient's incentive to defend the choice — to dismiss concerns, to discount second-opinion findings, to override the framework — increases sharply. The discipline of not sending the deposit until verification is complete is the cheapest insurance the book offers.

### Rule 5 — Document Everything

The records you take home determine whether the procedure abroad becomes ongoing care or a closed loop.

The 22-item records inventory (Worksheet 11, Records Packet Inventory) is the complete list. The most important items:

- Operative report, signed by the operating surgeon.
- Anesthesia record.
- Pathology report, if any tissue was sent.
- Implant patient cards (manufacturer, model, size, lot, UDI, site).
- Discharge summary with medications, post-op instructions, and flight-clearance criteria.
- Pre-op and post-op imaging.
- Itemized invoice with all payments and receipts.
- Warranty terms in writing.
- The surgeon's direct contact for at least 30–90 days.

The reason for the discipline: cross-border medical care is not a closed-loop transaction. The patient returns to a healthcare system (the US) that did not perform the procedure, must be brought up to speed on what was done, and must continue care for years or decades after. Documentation is the bridge. Without it, follow-up becomes expensive guesswork; warranty claims become impossible to file; revision surgery becomes a from-scratch evaluation rather than a continuation.

The five rules are the framework. The chapters that follow operationalize each rule for specific decisions.

## Chapter 8 — The Seven Leverage Points

The decision to travel for medical care passes through seven leverage points. At each one, you have power and you have risk. The book is organized around this sequence because the earlier you preserve leverage, the more options you have at the later stages.

The same framework appears in Volumes One and Two of this series. The leverage points are not procedure-specific.

### Leverage Point 1 — Before the search

The widest leverage. You have not yet contacted a clinic. Your contact information is not in any coordinator's CRM. The daily messaging has not begun. You can still ask the most basic question — *should I even be considering this?*

What you can do at LP1:
- Take the self-test (Worksheet 13, Chapter 10).
- Read the framework (Parts I and II of this book).
- Identify the procedure category and the destination set without yet committing to either.
- Talk to people who do not have a financial interest in your decision.

What you give up by moving past LP1 too quickly:
- The chance to discover that medical tourism is not the right answer for your specific situation.
- The discipline of choosing the framework before choosing the destination.

The single most important action at LP1: take eight weeks if you have them. Most medical-tourism decisions improve materially when the patient gives themselves eight weeks of pre-search preparation.

### Leverage Point 2 — The funnel

Once you have made first contact with a clinic, the funnel begins. The funnel has predictable moves — friendly responsiveness, photo requests, discount deadlines, social proof. Volume Two documents the cosmetic surgery funnel chapter by chapter. The patterns generalize.

Your leverage at LP2 is still substantial. You can:
- Refuse to send records to multiple clinics simultaneously without a written plan from any.
- Refuse to make a deposit decision before you have completed the 20-Minute Safety Pause.
- Refuse to engage on a coordinator's calendar (their urgency is theirs, not yours).

What you give up by moving past LP2 too quickly:
- The chance to compare written plans across clinics on equivalent terms.
- The discipline of choosing the clinic on the basis of the written plan, not on the basis of the coordinator's responsiveness.

Marcus in Chapter 1 is at LP2. He has not yet sent a deposit. His leverage is still high. He has the framework available; whether he uses it is the question Chapter 11 answers.

### Leverage Point 3 — The destination decision

You commit to a country, often to a city. From here, the comparison set shrinks. Your leverage is still high but increasingly local.

Your leverage at LP3:
- You can switch destinations if the verification work surfaces concerns.
- You can verify the country-level regulatory environment.
- You can use the diaspora frame (Chapter 17) if it applies.

What you give up:
- The breadth of options. Once you have committed to a country, the comparison is among clinics within that country.

### Leverage Point 4 — The quote

A specific clinic delivers a specific quote. You can compare it against alternatives. You can read it line by line. You can negotiate. Chapter 19 is the general quote decoder.

Your leverage at LP4 is high. The quote is a contract proposal. You have not yet committed money or time. You can:
- Demand a complete written quote (per Rule 2).
- Compare across two or three clinics.
- Negotiate the refund policy.
- Ask follow-up questions in writing.

### Leverage Point 5 — The deposit

This is where leverage begins to fall sharply. Before the deposit, you are a buyer. After it, you are an invested customer who is increasingly motivated to defend the choice.

The discipline at LP5: do not send the deposit until Rule 4 (Protect the Deposit) is satisfied. The book's strongest recommendation is to use Worksheet 3 (Deposit Readiness Test) before sending money. If you cannot answer yes to all 15 questions, the deposit is premature.

What you give up by moving past LP5 too quickly:
- The financial cushion to walk away if the in-person exam surfaces concerns.
- The negotiating position to renegotiate terms if the clinic changes the plan.
- Most of the practical leverage you have built across LP1 through LP4.

### Leverage Point 6 — Travel and the in-person exam

You are at the clinic. The plan may change. Pressure compounds — the slot is reserved, the deposit is paid, the flight is booked, the time-off is taken, the family knows you are there.

The leverage you have at LP6:
- The right to take one night before consent.
- The right to ask for changes in writing.
- The right to refuse non-emergency additions to the procedure plan.

The discipline at LP6 is to slow down even when it feels expensive to do so. The 20-Minute Safety Pause was designed for this moment. The Decision Gate at the end of the pause is the operational form.

### Leverage Point 7 — The procedure room

The lowest leverage of the journey. The only useful leverage left is the right to refuse — to stand up, ask for time, decline sedation, walk out.

The discipline at LP7 is preserved from V1 and V2:
- You cannot consent under sedation. If a non-emergency change is proposed after sedation has been administered, the answer must be no by default.
- The companion (Chapter 34) does not consent on your behalf for non-emergency changes.
- The right to refuse is real even at the OR doors. The cost of using it is your trip; the cost of not using it can be your health.

### Why the framework is sequential

The leverage points are sequential because each one is influenced by the decisions at the previous one. The patient who sends the deposit (LP5) without verifying the provider (LP3 and LP4) cannot easily reverse that decision at LP6. The patient who arrives in destination (LP6) without an independent second opinion (LP1 through LP3) is at a disadvantage when the plan changes.

The framework's discipline is to do the work in order. The book's chapters follow the same sequence.

## Chapter 9 — The Procedure Complexity Ladder

Every medical-tourism procedure can be located on a five-step complexity ladder. The ladder is not a moral judgment. It is an operational classification that tells you what specificity to demand, what verification to perform, and what reserve to budget.

The higher the step, the higher the stakes, the more written specificity you should require, and the more careful the verification.

### Step 1 — Low-surprise visits

Diagnostic consultations, single-area minor procedures, executive physicals, second-opinion visits, scans, biopsies (when done without intervention). Cataract assessments, LASIK consults, single tooth extractions, dermatologic punch biopsies.

**The leverage question:** What records will I receive from this visit, independent of whether I book a procedure?

Step 1 visits are low-stakes from a medical perspective. The framework discipline is light — verify the facility, get the records, do not commit to a subsequent procedure on the basis of a Step 1 visit alone.

### Step 2 — Outpatient single-procedure cases

LASIK, dental crown, cataract surgery, single-area dermatology, single dental implant. The procedure happens in a single setting, recovers in a few days, has standardized techniques and equipment globally.

**The leverage question:** If the plan changes after the in-person exam, will you pause and give me the revised options without losing my deposit?

Step 2 procedures have mature outcome data and standardized equipment. The framework's discipline focuses on the deposit (don't send before verification), the named provider (verify the credential), and the records (operative report, implant card if applicable).

### Step 3 — Outpatient multi-procedure or combined cases

Multiple cosmetic procedures in one session, full-mouth dental rehabilitation, hair restoration, multi-procedure aesthetic combinations. The procedure may be ambulatory but the combination adds complexity in anesthesia time, recovery, and follow-up.

**The leverage question:** Why is this combination right for my anatomy and goals, and would staging across two trips be lower risk?

Step 3 procedures are where the "package" marketing pattern often appears. The framework's discipline is to evaluate the components individually as well as in combination. Many combination procedures are reasonable; some are sales artifacts dressed in clinical language. The independent second opinion (Chapter 14 or 15) helps distinguish.

### Step 4 — Inpatient surgical procedures with implants or general anesthesia

Joint replacement (THR, TKA), breast augmentation, bariatric surgery (sleeve, bypass), abdominoplasty, gluteal fat transfer (BBL — with the V2 recommend-against pattern for intramuscular technique), most reproductive procedures (egg retrieval, embryo transfer), most non-emergency cardiac interventions (TAVR, valve repair with structural surgery).

**The leverage question:** Surgeon name, anesthesia provider name, facility accreditation, implant specification — all in writing before deposit.

Step 4 procedures are inpatient. They involve general anesthesia or significant sedation. They often involve implants that the patient will live with for decades. The framework's full discipline applies: verify the surgeon AND the anesthesia provider AND the facility accreditation AND the implant specification, all in writing, before deposit. Worksheets 5, 6, and 14 are the operational tools.

### Step 5 — Major combination procedures, transplants, complex inpatient

CABG, valve replacement (mechanical or biological prosthesis), valve repair, septal defects, complex oncology surgery, transplant, full-body rehabilitation surgery after massive weight loss, BBL combined with other major procedures.

**The leverage question:** Why is a fully accredited tertiary hospital not the right setting? What is the transfer protocol if a complication requires ICU-level care? What is the response time of the nearest higher-level facility?

Step 5 procedures are where most published medical-tourism deaths concentrate. The CDC MMWR DR series of 93 US-citizen deaths is overwhelmingly Step 5 cases — combination procedures performed in non-accredited ambulatory facilities. The framework's strongest discipline applies. The general recommendation: Step 5 procedures should be performed only at fully accredited tertiary hospitals with documented outcomes data and named transfer protocols. If a clinic cannot deliver those specifications, the procedure should not happen there.

### The 60-second placement exercise

For any procedure you are considering, identify which step it sits on. If you are not sure, default to the higher step. A combination case is at minimum Step 3; if it involves implants and general anesthesia, Step 4; if it involves major systems (cardiac, structural, transplant), Step 5.

The placement determines the verification work. Step 1 and 2 require basic verification. Step 3 requires the framework with additional attention to the combination question. Step 4 requires the full framework. Step 5 requires the full framework plus tertiary-hospital verification and transfer-protocol documentation.

For readers planning more than one procedure across a multi-year window, Worksheet 16 (Multi-Procedure Sequencing) in the back matter helps sequence the procedures by complexity step, recovery requirements, and reserve budget.*

The higher the step, the more written specificity, named providers, and documented safety processes you should require — and the less willing you should be to accept "we will handle it" as an answer.

## Chapter 10 — Is Medical Tourism the Right Answer for Me?

This is the question this book is structured to help you answer. It is the question most readers came to the book to ask. The honest answer for some readers will be yes. The honest answer for others will be no. Both answers are useful.

The work is in Worksheet 13, the Is Medical Tourism Right for Me? Self-Test. The 20 questions in the worksheet sort readers into three categories: proceed with research, pause and reconsider, or seek a different decision pathway entirely. This chapter walks through the self-test framework and discusses what the categories mean.

### When medical tourism is often the right answer

The framework is most likely to support medical tourism when:

- **The procedure has mature international outcome data.** Joint replacement, dental rehabilitation, LASIK, cataract surgery, sleeve gastrectomy, IVF cycles, single-area cosmetic surgery at JCI-accredited facilities — these procedures have well-documented international outcomes that can be compared to US outcomes.
- **The destination has top-tier tertiary centers in the procedure category.** Apollo Hospitals in India for cardiac and orthopedic; Bumrungrad in Thailand for general; Anadolu Medical Center in Turkey; Hospital CIMA in Costa Rica for orthopedic and general; specific named centers across destinations.
- **You have time to apply the framework properly.** Eight weeks of preparation. Three months is better.
- **The financial differential is real and meaningful for your situation.** The True Cost Calculator (Worksheet 1) shows you the all-in differential, not the headline figure.
- **You have a US-based provider relationship who can manage post-return follow-up.** For procedures with multi-year follow-up requirements (bariatric surgery, joint replacement, breast implants), a US-based clinician who has agreed to evaluate the records and provide continuity matters enormously.
- **You have a companion who has been briefed on Chapter 34's responsibilities.** Solo medical tourism is workable for low-step procedures and meaningfully riskier for higher-step procedures.

### When medical tourism is often the wrong answer

The framework is most likely to recommend against medical tourism when:

- **The procedure requires ongoing complex US-based specialty care.** Complex chronic disease management, ongoing oncology surveillance, autoimmune disease management, certain transplant follow-up.
- **You cannot give the decision the time the framework requires.** A procedure decision that has to happen in the next two weeks rarely benefits from medical tourism; the framework cannot be applied properly in that window.
- **You have an active, unstable medical condition that argues for proximity to your US specialist.** Decompensated heart failure considering valve replacement abroad is a different situation than stable valvular disease considering elective replacement.
- **The procedure category is one the book recommends against in low-regulation settings.** Transplant tourism, unapproved alternative cancer therapies, stem-cell tourism, surgery in non-accredited facilities — these are covered in Chapter 30 and are pattern-level recommend-against, not patient-specific.
- **You do not have a financial reserve to walk away from the deposit if the in-person exam surfaces concerns.** Cost-crisis patients in extreme financial stress are at the highest risk of being unable to use the framework's most important discipline (the right to walk).
- **You do not have a companion or substitute systems for higher-step procedures.** Companion absence at Step 4 and Step 5 procedures meaningfully raises risk.

### The "what would your home physician do?" sanity check

A useful exercise before committing to medical tourism: imagine the conversation you would have with your primary care physician if they knew everything about the destination, the surgeon, the procedure, and your situation. Would they support the plan?

If yes — if you can imagine your home physician saying "this looks like a reasonable plan given your situation" — you are likely on solid ground.

If no — if you can imagine your home physician saying "I would want you to consider X before going" — work through X before committing. X is usually a verification step, a second opinion, or a timeline question.

If you find yourself wanting to hide the plan from your home physician, the framework is telling you something. The discomfort is information.

### Telemedicine second opinion as the path forward

For many readers, the right answer at this stage is a formal telemedicine second opinion from a US academic medical center. Chapter 15 covers Cleveland Clinic Online Medical Second Opinion, Mayo Clinic Online Second Opinion, MD Anderson, MSK International, Mass General Brigham International, and Johns Hopkins International. The cost is $900 to $2,500 per case. The turnaround is 7 to 14 days. The opinion is independent of any medical-tourism economic interest.

A telemedicine second opinion is particularly useful for readers who:
- Have a procedure recommendation but want to verify the recommendation is sound.
- Are considering a complex or high-step procedure where the destination has fewer comparable facilities.
- Want a written record of independent specialist agreement (or disagreement) before committing.
- Are not sure whether medical tourism is the right answer at all.

For some readers, the telemedicine opinion will confirm the US recommendation and the medical tourism plan; for others, it will surface considerations that change the plan or that argue for a different pathway entirely. Both outcomes are useful.

### What this chapter does not do

This chapter does not tell you which procedure to have. It does not evaluate whether your specific clinical situation is right for the procedure you are considering — that is a clinical question for a qualified physician.

It does tell you whether *medical tourism* (cross-border care) is likely to be the right answer for your specific situation, given the procedure category, the time available, the financial reserve, the support system, and the destination set you are considering. The 20-question self-test in Worksheet 13 is the operational form.

## Chapter 11 — The 60-Day Preparation Plan

Most medical-tourism decisions improve materially when the patient gives themselves eight weeks of preparation. Some decisions benefit from longer; some can be made in less. This chapter is the operational form of "slow down" — the week-by-week plan that turns the framework into a sequence of actions.

The plan assumes Step 3 or Step 4 procedures (most cosmetic surgery, most orthopedic, most bariatric, most reproductive). For Step 1 and Step 2 procedures, the plan compresses. For Step 5 procedures, the plan extends and the framework's full discipline applies.

### Week 1 — Framework

Read Parts I and II of this book. Take the self-test (Worksheet 13). Identify the procedure category, the complexity-ladder step, and the destination set you are considering. Set the walk-away number — the dollar amount above which the procedure no longer makes financial sense for you.

Identify your support system. Who will be your companion if travel is required? Who is your home physician or specialist that will manage follow-up? If those answers are not yet clear, this is the week to identify them.

End of Week 1 deliverable: a one-page situation summary. Procedure. Complexity step. Walk-away number. Destination set (no more than three countries). Support system. Specific concerns you want a second opinion to address.

### Week 2 — Initial research

Begin clinic-level research within the destination set. Identify three to five candidate facilities per destination. For each, verify accreditation in the relevant directory (JCI, AAAASF, NABH, HAI). Verify the surgeon's credentials in the destination country's official register.

Do not yet make contact with clinics. The funnel begins as soon as you provide contact information; delaying first contact preserves leverage at LP1 and LP2.

Begin the literature search (Chapter 12). What does the published outcome data show for your procedure at international tertiary centers? Is there a registry like AJRR (orthopedic), STS (cardiac), SART (IVF) that the destination facility participates in?

End of Week 2 deliverable: a verified short list of facilities. For each: accreditation status, named surgeon with verified credential, and at least one peer-reviewed source documenting outcomes for your procedure at international centers.

### Week 3 — First contact with shortlist

Make initial contact with three to five candidate facilities through their international patient departments. Request written information at each:
- Surgeon name (full legal name) and verifiable credential.
- Anesthesia provider name and credential, for procedures requiring anesthesia.
- Facility accreditation documentation.
- Sample procedure quote with the structure Chapter 19 specifies.
- Refund policy in writing.

Use Worksheet 8 (Coordinator Log) to track the responses. Note the responsiveness — but also note the substance.

End of Week 3 deliverable: written information from at least three facilities. The information is on equivalent terms (you asked the same questions). Most facilities will respond; some will not. The non-responders self-select out of the comparison.

### Week 4 — Second opinion

Arrange an independent second opinion. Three options:

1. **In-person second opinion at a US specialist.** Best for procedure recommendations that have not yet been finalized. The US specialist evaluates your situation independently and gives a written opinion.
2. **In-person second opinion at a destination facility different from the one you are considering.** Useful if your situation has already been worked up and you want a second perspective from a clinician in the destination market.
3. **Formal telemedicine second opinion** from Cleveland Clinic Online Medical Second Opinion, Mayo Clinic Online Second Opinion, MD Anderson, MSK International, Mass General Brigham International, or Johns Hopkins International. Cost $900–$2,500; turnaround 7–14 days. See Chapter 15.

For most cost-crisis readers, the telemedicine option is the right balance of cost, speed, and credibility. For complex Step 5 procedures (cardiac, oncology), the telemedicine path is especially valuable.

End of Week 4 deliverable: a written independent second opinion. The opinion either confirms the procedure recommendation and the rough plan, or surfaces considerations that change the plan. Both outcomes are useful.

### Week 5 — Quote comparison

You now have written information from three to five facilities and an independent second opinion. Compare on equivalent terms using Worksheet 2 (Cross-Procedure Quote Comparison). Compute the true cost using Worksheet 1 (True Cost Calculator). Identify the facility that scores best across cost, verification, second-opinion compatibility, and follow-up logistics.

End of Week 5 deliverable: a chosen facility with documented rationale. The rationale is written down because writing it down forces clarity and creates a record for future reference if anything changes.

### Week 6 — Deposit decision

If the chosen facility passes all 15 questions in Worksheet 3 (Deposit Readiness Test), this is the week to send the deposit. The deposit secures a surgical date and (for some procedures) implant ordering or operative room time. The deposit should be the smallest amount the facility will accept that secures the date, with the most favorable refund terms negotiated in writing.

If Worksheet 3 returns any "no" answers, this is the week to resolve them — not to send the deposit anyway. The discipline of not sending the deposit prematurely is the cheapest insurance the book offers.

End of Week 6 deliverable: either a deposit sent with all 15 readiness questions answered yes, or a documented reason to delay or change destinations. Both are acceptable.

### Week 7 — Logistics

Book travel. Reserve accommodation. Confirm companion arrangements (or substitute systems for solo travel). Arrange in-destination ground transportation. Notify your home physician of the planned procedure and confirm post-return follow-up arrangement. Begin pre-op compliance — smoking cessation if relevant, GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) medication hold per ASA (American Society of Anesthesiologists) guidance if relevant (V2 Chapter 17 has the full medication framework), nutrition optimization, supplement holds.

End of Week 7 deliverable: travel and logistics confirmed. Pre-op compliance underway.

### Week 8 — Final confirmation

Confirm the surgical plan with the destination clinic. Confirm the anesthesia provider name and credentials a final time. Confirm the records you will receive at discharge. Confirm the post-return follow-up arrangement with your US-based clinician. Prepare the records-packet binder template you will fill in at the destination (Worksheet 10, Records Packet Cover Sheet).

End of Week 8 deliverable: ready to travel. The trip is planned. The framework has been applied. The decisions have been made. The remaining work is in-destination and post-return.

### What "prepared" looks like at end of Week 8

The patient who has completed the 60-day plan has:
- A verified facility, surgeon, and anesthesia provider.
- A written, surgeon-signed plan.
- An independent second opinion.
- A complete quote with refund policy in writing.
- A deposit sent under named conditions.
- Travel and logistics confirmed.
- A companion briefed (Chapter 34) or substitute systems in place.
- A US-based post-return follow-up arranged.
- A records-packet template ready.

Marcus, in our opening scene, was at Week 0 of this plan. The framework's discipline is what gets him from Week 0 to Week 8. Whether the trip works out the way we describe in Chapter 31 depends on whether the framework holds.

### Composite Scenario A — Marcus at Week 2

Marcus is two weeks into the plan. The self-test result said proceed with research, with attention to the complexity-ladder step (his bilateral TKA is Step 4 — inpatient, implants, general anesthesia). He has identified three candidate facilities — a JCI-accredited tertiary hospital in San José in Costa Rica, Apollo Hospitals in Chennai, and Anadolu Medical Center near Istanbul. Two are JCI-accredited; one is in the accreditation directory but the accreditation date is from 2022 and he is checking whether reaccreditation has occurred.

He has not yet contacted any of the three clinics. He has reviewed the surgeon credentialing patterns in each country (Chapter 16 has the patterns). He has scheduled a telemedicine second opinion through Cleveland Clinic Online Medical Second Opinion for Week 4 — the cost is $1,800 and the turnaround is 10 days.

He is having dinner with his brother for the first time since the orthopedic appointment. His brother does not have strong opinions about medical tourism but does have strong opinions about Marcus not telling him for two weeks. The conversation is harder than the research. By the end of the dinner, his brother has agreed to be the home contact during the trip — to know the dates, to have the records, to be the person Marcus's wife calls if she needs a second opinion in the family.

Marcus's wife is now part of the planning. The hidden phase of the decision is over. The framework is operating on a calendar Marcus controls.

The hardest part of the 60-day plan is not the work. It is the discipline of taking eight weeks when the funnel will offer you eight days. The reward is a decision you can defend to yourself at year five.

# Part III — Researching Your Procedure

## Chapter 12 — How to Read Clinical Literature You Didn't Go to Medical School For

A surprising amount of useful clinical evidence about medical-tourism procedures is in published peer-reviewed literature you can read for free. You do not need to go to medical school to read it. You need a structured 30-minute search process and a working understanding of what evidence is reliable.

### Where to look

**PubMed** (*pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov*) is the National Library of Medicine's free database of biomedical literature. Search by procedure name (e.g., "sleeve gastrectomy outcomes"), procedure name plus destination ("knee replacement Costa Rica"), or specific clinical question ("rhinoplasty revision rate"). Most abstracts are free; many full papers are also free.

**Google Scholar** (*scholar.google.com*) indexes a broader set of academic literature. Useful for finding papers Google's regular search would miss.

**Cochrane Library** (*cochranelibrary.com*) publishes systematic reviews — the highest-quality form of clinical evidence summary. If a Cochrane review exists on your procedure, read it first.

**Specialty society websites** (ASPS, AAOS, ASMBS, STS plus ASRM — American Society for Reproductive Medicine, ASCO — American Society of Clinical Oncology, AAO — American Academy of Ophthalmology) publish position statements, clinical practice guidelines, and patient education materials. These are not as rigorous as peer-reviewed primary literature but are useful for clinical context.

**The CDC's Yellow Book** (*wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/yellowbook*) has a chapter on medical tourism that is updated annually and provides federal-government context on the field.

**FDA databases** (drug safety, device safety, expanded access program) are useful for understanding drug and device approval landscape, including products marketed abroad that are not approved in the US.

### The hierarchy of evidence

Not all clinical evidence is equal. The hierarchy, from strongest to weakest:

1. **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** (Cochrane, others) — synthesize multiple studies into a single quality-weighted conclusion.
2. **Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)** — gold standard for individual studies; patients randomized between treatments allow strong causal inference.
3. **Cohort studies** — follow patients over time, useful for outcome data when RCTs are not feasible. Most medical-tourism outcome data is cohort-level.
4. **Case-control studies** — compare patients with an outcome to patients without; useful for rare outcomes but more susceptible to bias.
5. **Case series** — describe outcomes in groups of patients without comparison; weakest study design but often the only available evidence for new procedures or specific destinations.
6. **Expert opinion and editorials** — useful for interpretation but not primary evidence.

When you find a paper, identify which level it represents. A Cochrane review trumps a single RCT; an RCT trumps a cohort study; cohort trumps case series. Most published outcome data on international medical-tourism procedures is cohort-level or case-series; the framework is to be appropriately skeptical of single-center case series that report extraordinary outcomes.

### How to read an abstract

Most readers will read abstracts, not full papers. The abstract structure is standardized: Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions. Read all four sections, not just the conclusion.

The single most common abstract-reading error: reading the conclusion in isolation and missing the qualifications in the methods. A conclusion that says "sleeve gastrectomy is safe and effective at our center" is meaningful only when read against a methods section that describes "consecutive patients at a single center, 2017–2022, retrospective review."

Watch for:
- **Sample size.** Tiny samples produce unreliable conclusions.
- **Patient selection.** Were complex cases excluded? Were the patients similar to you?
- **Follow-up duration.** A "12-month outcomes" study cannot tell you about 10-year survival.
- **Conflict-of-interest disclosure.** Authors funded by manufacturers, or who serve as paid consultants, have a disclosed bias.
- **Single-center vs. multicenter.** Single-center studies often reflect that center's specific practice; multicenter studies generalize better.

### The conflict-of-interest disclosure check

Every peer-reviewed paper has a disclosure section, usually near the end. The authors must disclose financial relationships with industry, paid consultancies, patents, and other material interests. A clean disclosure ("the authors declare no conflicts of interest") is not a guarantee of unbiased work, but a heavy disclosure list (consultancies for the device manufacturer of the implant the paper studies) is a meaningful signal to read the paper more carefully.

For medical-tourism-relevant publications specifically, watch for:
- Authors affiliated with the destination clinic or hospital being studied.
- Funding from the destination's tourism authority or industry association.
- Conflicts that involve the specific device, drug, or technique being discussed.

These do not invalidate the work but they do warrant a more careful read.

### The 30-minute baseline research protocol

For any procedure you are seriously considering:

1. **Five minutes — Wikipedia.** The procedure's Wikipedia article gives you basic terminology, the general clinical context, and links to authoritative sources. Wikipedia is not a citable source but it is an excellent starting point.
2. **Ten minutes — Specialty society patient education.** ASPS, AAOS, ASMBS, ASRM, ASCO, AAO all have patient-facing pages on their procedures. These give you the clinical framing as the relevant specialty society defines it.
3. **Ten minutes — PubMed search.** Search the procedure name plus "outcomes" or "complications." Read three to five abstracts. Note recent publication dates, sample sizes, and patient populations.
4. **Five minutes — CDC Yellow Book.** Check the medical tourism chapter for any specific advisories relevant to your procedure or destination.

This is not a comprehensive literature review. It is a 30-minute baseline that prevents you from being blindsided by basic clinical facts. Most readers do not even do this much. The difference between a 30-minute baseline and no baseline is meaningful.

### What this chapter does not do

It does not turn you into a clinician. It does not give you the judgment to evaluate complex clinical evidence. Those things require training and experience the book cannot provide.

What it does: equip you to read enough literature to ask informed questions, recognize when a clinic's claims are inconsistent with published evidence, and identify when your situation is unusual enough to warrant a specialist second opinion. The 30-minute baseline is the foundation; Chapter 14 (Independent Second Opinions) and Chapter 15 (Telemedicine Second Opinions) build on it.

## Chapter 13 — Outcome Data: Where It Exists, Where It Doesn't

For some procedures, US patients have access to detailed outcome registries that report center-level and surgeon-level results. For other procedures, the data does not exist at that level of granularity. For international procedures, the outcome data is generally weaker, often single-center case series, sometimes nothing at all.

The framework's discipline is to know what data is available, use it where it exists, and recognize when it does not.

### The US outcome registries

Several US-based outcome registries publish patient-relevant data:

**AJRR (American Joint Replacement Registry)** is the US joint replacement registry, operated by AAOS. It captures more than 2.5 million procedures across more than 1,300 institutions. The annual report publishes 10-year implant survivorship, complication rates, and revision rates by implant brand, surgical technique, and patient demographic. For joint replacement decisions, AJRR is the strongest US data source.

**STS National Database (Society of Thoracic Surgeons)** is the cardiac surgery registry. STS publishes risk-adjusted mortality data by center and procedure. For CABG, valve replacement, and structural cardiac intervention decisions, STS data is the US benchmark.

**NCDR (National Cardiovascular Data Registry)** is the broader cardiovascular registry, with specific modules for cath lab procedures, ICD implants, and structural intervention.

**NSQIP (National Surgical Quality Improvement Program)** is the American College of Surgeons' general surgery quality registry. Less procedure-specific than the others but useful for overall surgical safety context.

**SART (Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology)** publishes IVF cycle success rates by clinic, by patient age band, by procedure type. SART data is uniquely transparent in US healthcare — clinic-level outcomes are publicly available.

**FDA MAUDE (Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience)** is the medical device adverse event database. Searchable by device, brand, and reporting facility. Useful for understanding device-specific complication patterns.

### International outcome registries

Several international registries publish at registry quality comparable to US data:

- **NJR (National Joint Registry, England and Wales)** for joint replacement.
- **AOANJRR (Australian Orthopaedic Association National Joint Replacement Registry).**
- **SHAR (Swedish Hip Arthroplasty Register).**
- **ESHRE (European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology)** for IVF.
- **Country-specific cardiac surgery registries** in the UK, Germany, India, and several other jurisdictions.

For procedures done in countries with these registries, the data is comparable to US data quality. For procedures in countries without registry coverage, the data is weaker.

### Clinic-reported outcomes — what to take and what to leave

Many international clinics report their own outcomes on websites, in marketing materials, and in patient information packets. These reports vary in reliability.

What to take seriously:
- Outcomes reported in peer-reviewed publications. If the clinic has published in *JBJS*, *Annals of Thoracic Surgery*, *Obesity Surgery*, or another peer-reviewed journal, the published outcomes are subject to editorial review and may be cited.
- Outcomes that are part of a recognized international registry submission.
- Outcomes reported with full methodology — sample size, time period, complication definitions, exclusions.

What to be skeptical of:
- Outcomes reported without methodology. "99% success rate" with no definition of "success" is marketing.
- Outcomes that omit complication rates.
- Outcomes that report only the most flattering metric (e.g., "30-day mortality 0%" for a procedure where 30-day mortality is rare anyway, while omitting longer-term complication data).

### The best-available-evidence framing

For many medical-tourism procedures, you will not have clinic-specific outcome data. The framework's response: use peer-reviewed outcome data for the procedure category at the destination tier (e.g., "outcomes at JCI-accredited Indian cardiac surgery centers" rather than "outcomes at Apollo Hospitals Chennai specifically"), combined with the verification of accreditation, surgeon credentials, and procedure volume.

This is not as good as clinic-specific data. It is the best available data in many cases. The framework asks you to be appropriately skeptical, not paralyzed.

### When the data does not exist

Some procedure-destination combinations have essentially no public outcome data. Some destinations have no registry participation. Some procedures are too low-volume to support published outcomes. Some clinics simply do not publish.

When this is the case, the verification framework rests more heavily on:
- Accreditation status (Chapter 4).
- Surgeon credentialing (Chapter 16).
- Independent second opinion (Chapters 14 and 15).
- The procedure complexity ladder (Chapter 9).

The absence of data is itself information. A Step 5 procedure (cardiac, oncology, transplant) at a destination with no public outcome data warrants substantially more caution than the same procedure at a center with documented outcomes.

## Chapter 14 — Independent Second Opinions

An independent second opinion is the single most useful piece of verification work the patient can do. The book has put it at the center of the 60-day plan (Week 4) for that reason.

A second opinion is an assessment by a clinician who has no financial or referral interest in your decision. It can be in-person at home, in-person at a destination second clinic, or via formal telemedicine (Chapter 15). The form varies; the function is the same.

### Why a second opinion matters

The first clinical opinion you receive is from the clinic that will perform the procedure. This is not inherently biased — the clinic has accurate information about the procedure they offer — but it is *interested.* The clinic has a commercial relationship to the recommendation. An independent second opinion has no such relationship.

In medical tourism specifically, the second opinion serves additional functions:
- **Verifies the procedure recommendation.** Is the proposed procedure right for your situation, or is it the procedure the destination clinic happens to specialize in?
- **Verifies the scope.** Is the proposed combination right, or is the package the destination clinic typically sells?
- **Verifies the timeline.** Is the urgency the clinic is asserting clinically warranted?
- **Generates a written record.** A second opinion in writing is evidence in any future complaint or claim.

### Three forms

**In-person at home.** Schedule an appointment with a US specialist who has no commercial relationship to your destination clinic. The US specialist evaluates your situation independently. Cost: typically $300–$500 for a consult (insurance may cover; verify). Best for: procedure recommendations that have not yet been finalized.

**In-person at a destination second clinic.** If you are already in the destination or planning a pre-procedure scouting trip, an in-person second opinion at a different clinic in the same destination market is useful. The second clinic evaluates your situation; you have a comparison from two providers in the same regulatory environment. Cost: variable; often $50–$200 for an outpatient consult. Best for: situations where you have already done home-based research and want a destination-clinic perspective.

**Formal telemedicine** from Cleveland Clinic, Mayo Clinic, MD Anderson, MSK International, Mass General Brigham International, or Johns Hopkins International. Chapter 15 covers these in detail. Cost: $900–$2,500. Best for: cost-driven medical tourism where the question is whether the recommended procedure and destination are right; for higher-stakes procedures; for situations where written specialist agreement (or disagreement) matters.

### The second-opinion question list

When you arrange a second opinion, the questions to ask are not "is the destination clinic good." The questions are:

1. **Is the proposed procedure appropriate for my clinical situation?** Independent of where it is performed.
2. **Are there alternatives I should consider?** A different procedure, a different timing, a different sequence.
3. **What is the realistic outcome range for this procedure on someone with my profile?** Best case, typical case, worst case.
4. **What are the specific factors that would make this procedure higher-risk in my case?** Comorbidities, medications, prior surgeries, demographics.
5. **What records should I bring back from the destination procedure?** For ongoing care at home.
6. **If I have a complication, what should be my home-care pathway?**

Notice that none of these questions ask the second-opinion provider to evaluate the destination clinic. The second opinion is about your clinical situation, not the destination. The destination evaluation is what Chapter 17 covers.

*A reusable copy of the Second Opinion Request template is Worksheet 7 in the back matter.*

### When a second opinion changes the plan

A meaningful percentage of second opinions surface considerations that change the plan. Sometimes the procedure recommendation changes; more often the scope or timing changes. Sometimes the second opinion confirms the original recommendation but adds clinical context that affects the destination selection (e.g., "this is right for you, but it should be done at a facility with X capability, which limits your destination set").

The patient who receives a second opinion that contradicts the destination clinic's recommendation faces a hard moment. The temptation is to discount the second opinion ("they don't know my situation"). The discipline is to take it seriously. Two opinions are more informative than one. If they disagree, you have learned that your decision is harder than you thought; if they agree, you have higher confidence.

### What a second opinion is not

A second opinion is not:
- A guarantee of outcome.
- A substitute for the in-person clinical evaluation by the destination clinic.
- A regulatory authority on whether the destination clinic is qualified.
- A blessing of medical tourism as a category for your situation.

It is one well-defined piece of information — an independent assessment of your clinical situation by a clinician with no economic stake in your decision. It is one of the highest-leverage pieces of work in the 60-day plan.

## Chapter 15 — Telemedicine Second Opinions from US Academic Centers

This chapter is new in Volume Three. The previous volumes did not cover formal international telemedicine second-opinion programs because the procedure-specific framing was different. For the foundation volume — for the reader who has not yet picked a procedure or a destination — the telemedicine second-opinion option is one of the most important pieces of information in the book.

Several US academic medical centers operate formal international second-opinion programs. They are not promotional; they are paid services that deliver a structured written specialist opinion within a defined timeline. For US patients considering medical tourism, they are often the right answer to the question "should I really do this?"

### The major programs

**Cleveland Clinic Online Medical Second Opinion** (*my.clevelandclinic.org/online-services/online-medical-second-opinion*). One of the longest-established programs. Covers nearly all specialties. The process: the patient submits medical records, the Cleveland Clinic specialist reviews them, a written opinion is delivered in 7–14 days, with optional video consultation. Cost: $1,800–$2,500 per case depending on specialty.

**Mayo Clinic Online Second Opinion** (*mayoclinic.org/online-services/online-second-opinion*). Similar to Cleveland Clinic; covers most specialties. Cost: $1,500–$2,500.

**MD Anderson Online Second Opinion** (*mdanderson.org/online-second-opinion*). Specifically oncology. Comprehensive cancer-specific review with treatment plan recommendation. Cost: $900–$1,800.

**Memorial Sloan Kettering International (MSK Direct)** (*mskcc.org/international*). Oncology second opinion at one of the world's top cancer centers. Cost: $1,500–$3,000.

**Massachusetts General Hospital International / Mass General Brigham International** (*massgeneral.org/international*). Broad specialty coverage. Cost varies.

**Johns Hopkins International** (*hopkinsmedicine.org/international*). Broad specialty coverage. Established program.

### What they will do

The structured telemedicine second opinion includes:
- Records review by a board-certified specialist at the academic medical center.
- A written opinion document, generally 1–3 pages, that addresses the patient's clinical situation, the proposed treatment plan (if any has been finalized), alternative considerations, and the specialist's recommendation.
- Optional video consultation (15–30 minutes) with the reviewing specialist to discuss the opinion and answer questions.
- For oncology programs specifically, often a treatment plan recommendation that includes second-opinion-derived adjustments to the original plan.

### What they will not do

- Take over the patient's care.
- Prescribe medications.
- Perform procedures.
- Manage ongoing treatment.
- Serve as the patient's primary clinician for the procedure.
- Provide referrals to specific international clinics.

The second-opinion programs are structured to provide opinion, not care. They are exactly the form of independent assessment medical-tourism patients need.

### When telemedicine is the right answer

The formal telemedicine second opinion is particularly useful when:

- **The reader is uncertain whether medical tourism is the right answer.** A telemedicine specialist can evaluate the clinical situation independently of medical tourism economics and provide a US-academic-center perspective.
- **The procedure is high-stakes or high-complexity.** Cardiac, oncology, complex spine — situations where the procedure recommendation itself benefits from independent specialist review.
- **The patient wants a written record of independent specialist agreement.** Particularly useful for cost-crisis readers who want to defend the decision to themselves at year five.
- **The destination set is narrow and a specialist opinion can help refine it.** The reviewing specialist may not know specific international clinics but may know procedure-specific considerations that affect destination evaluation.

### When telemedicine is not enough

The formal telemedicine second opinion has limitations. It is not the right answer when:

- **Hands-on examination is required.** Some procedures (specific orthopedic situations, certain dermatologic evaluations) require in-person physical exam. The reviewing specialist will note this and recommend in-person follow-up.
- **The decision is genuinely time-sensitive.** A telemedicine opinion takes 7–14 days. If the procedure is acutely urgent, this timeline may not work — though acutely urgent procedures are rarely candidates for medical tourism anyway.
- **The procedure is one the reviewing specialist's institution does not offer.** A Cleveland Clinic specialist will offer a second opinion on any procedure, but may have less procedure-specific context for procedures that are concentrated outside US academic centers (some cosmetic surgery techniques, certain IVF configurations, some refractive procedures).

### How to use a telemedicine second opinion in the 60-day plan

The plan's Week 4 deliverable is an independent second opinion. For most cost-driven medical-tourism readers, the formal telemedicine option is the right balance of cost, speed, and credibility.

The process:
1. **Week 3:** Identify which program is the right fit. For oncology, MD Anderson or MSK. For most other specialties, Cleveland Clinic or Mayo. Submit the application.
2. **Week 3, days 4–7:** Submit medical records as requested. The programs have specific record-submission protocols.
3. **Week 4:** Receive the written opinion. Schedule the optional video consultation if available.
4. **Week 4, end:** Integrate the opinion into the destination/procedure decision. If the opinion changes the plan, work through what changes.

### How the telemedicine path resolves an oncology decision — forward to Chapter 29

The clearest illustration of how the formal telemedicine second opinion operates for a high-stakes oncology decision is Composite Scenario E (Karen and her mother), developed in full in Chapter 29. In brief: Karen's mother is recently diagnosed with stage III breast cancer; the US oncologist's plan is standard; Karen submits the records to MSK International Second Opinion ($1,800, twelve-day turnaround); the MSK breast oncology specialist confirms the US plan and refines one element (a genomic test informing endocrine therapy). The family stays home for the treatment. The full scene — with the regimen specifics, the genomic-test detail, and the year-2 follow-up — is in Chapter 29.

Two general points the scene illustrates:

Not every second opinion confirms the home plan. Sometimes the second opinion surfaces considerations that change the plan. Sometimes it identifies a destination option the family had not considered. Both outcomes are useful. The point is the independent assessment.

The book recommends a formal telemedicine second opinion for nearly every reader considering medical tourism for a Step 3 or higher procedure. The cost is meaningful but not large relative to the procedure cost. The value — independent specialist agreement or disagreement, in writing, with no medical-tourism economic interest — is high.

## Chapter 16 — Specialist Credentials by Country

The book's Rule 3 is verify the provider. This chapter is the operational form: how specialist credentialing works in the major medical-tourism destinations, and how to verify a specific surgeon in the destination's official register.

The verification is procedure-agnostic and country-specific. Each country has its own regulatory structure. The patterns recur but the specific bodies and procedures vary.

### Mexico

The Mexican medical regulator is the Comisión Nacional de Arbitraje Médico (CONAMED), though specialty certifications are issued through specialty councils (Consejos de Especialidad Médica). The cédula profesional registry, maintained by the Secretaría de Educación Pública, contains licensed physicians and their professional registration numbers. Specialty council certifications layer on top — for plastic surgery, the Consejo Mexicano de Cirugía Plástica, Estética y Reconstructiva; for orthopedic surgery, the Consejo Mexicano de Cirugía Ortopédica y Traumatología.

To verify a Mexican surgeon: search the cédula profesional registry at *cedulaprofesional.sep.gob.mx* by name. The result confirms licensure. Then verify specialty certification at the relevant Consejo's website.

International society memberships that matter for Mexico: ISAPS for plastic surgeons (international plastic surgery membership requires national board certification), AMCG for general surgeons, AMOSEP for spine surgeons.

### India

The Medical Council of India (MCI, now subsumed into the National Medical Commission) maintains the registry of licensed physicians. State medical councils (Maharashtra Medical Council, Delhi Medical Council, etc.) maintain state-level registries that include the same data plus state-specific licensure.

NABH (National Accreditation Board for Hospitals and Healthcare Providers) accredits hospitals and is ISQua-recognized. The NABH directory at *nabh.co* lists accredited facilities.

Specialty boards: the National Board of Examinations grants the DNB (Diplomate of National Board) certification across specialties; the MCI/state council registries note this. Many Indian specialists also hold Royal College fellowships (FRCS, FRCP) from UK colleges, particularly common in cardiac and orthopedic surgery.

To verify an Indian surgeon: search the Medical Council of India registry at *nmc.org.in*. Verify the DNB or equivalent specialty certification. For cardiac, orthopedic, or oncology specialists, check Royal College fellowship if claimed.

### Thailand

The Thai Medical Council maintains the registry. Specialty boards are organized through the Royal Colleges (e.g., Royal College of Surgeons of Thailand). Healthcare Accreditation Institute (HAI) is the country's accreditation body, ISQua-recognized.

International society memberships and US/European training are common among Thai specialists at top international hospitals (Bumrungrad International, Bangkok Hospital). Verification often involves both Thai registration and the international credential.

### Turkey

The Turkish Ministry of Health licenses physicians; specialty certification is through the Turkish Medical Association's specialty boards. Anadolu Medical Center and several other top hospitals operate with JCI accreditation and Western-style credentialing visible to international patients.

Verification: search the Turkish Medical Association registry; verify specialty board certification; cross-check JCI accreditation status of the affiliated facility.

### Costa Rica

The Colegio de Médicos y Cirujanos de Costa Rica maintains the physician registry. CINME (Centro de Investigación en Mejoramiento Sanitario) and Acreditación Costa Rica handle hospital accreditation; JCI is also common for top hospitals (a JCI-accredited tertiary hospital in San José, Hospital Clínica Bíblica).

Verification: search the Colegio de Médicos registry at *medicos.cr*; verify specialty certification through the relevant specialty association.

### Colombia

The Colombian Medical Council (Tribunal Nacional de Ética Médica) handles ethics; the Ministry of Health maintains licensure registries. Specialty certifications are through specialty colleges (Sociedad Colombiana de Cirugía Plástica, etc.). Several Colombian hospitals carry JCI accreditation.

### Dominican Republic

The Colegio Médico Dominicano maintains the registry. The DR's accreditation framework is less mature; JCI accreditation at specific facilities is the strongest signal. The CDC MMWR series on US-citizen deaths in DR concentrates in non-JCI-accredited ambulatory facilities; the framework's recommendation to verify both surgeon and facility applies particularly strongly here.

### South Korea

The Korean Medical Association maintains the registry. Specialty board certifications are through the Korean Academy of Medical Sciences. Korean medical tourism is concentrated in specific specialties (cosmetic surgery, dermatology, ophthalmology) at facilities with mature international patient programs.

### What to do with credentialing information

For any procedure you are considering, the framework's discipline is:

1. **Identify the named surgeon and the named anesthesia provider** in the written quote.
2. **Look up each in the destination country's official register.** Confirm licensure, specialty certification, registration number.
3. **Look up the international society memberships** if claimed (ISAPS, AAOS, STS, ASCO, etc.).
4. **Verify the hospital accreditation** in the relevant directory (JCI, NABH, HAI, AAAASF).
5. **Document the verification** in Worksheet 5 (Specialist Credential Worksheet). Save screenshots of the registry searches. For procedures requiring sedation, regional, or general anesthesia, Worksheet 6 (Anesthesia Provider Verification) in the back matter captures the parallel verification for the anesthesia provider.

The verification is not a guarantee of a good outcome. The verification is the baseline; the absence of verifiable credentials is the strongest predictor of failure modes documented in the published evidence.

Priya knows him; her mother trusts him. He is in his office, in white coat, recommending a procedure at his own hospital.

**A note from the editor — Credentialing is necessary, not sufficient**

The single most common mistake I see in medical-tourism patient correspondence is the assumption that a credentialed surgeon at an accredited facility will produce a good outcome. The framework's verification work is necessary; it is also not sufficient.

What it does is establish a baseline. A surgeon who is registered in the destination's official register, board-certified in the relevant specialty, and operating at an accredited facility has met the threshold below which the documented patterns of harm concentrate. The CDC's Dominican Republic series — 93 US-citizen deaths between 2009 and 2022, 92 percent involving gluteal fat transfer — concentrates in non-accredited facilities with surgeons whose credentials were not verified before deposit. The book's framework would have prevented most of those deaths from being the patients they were.

The verification is what makes the rest of the framework possible. The independent second opinion (Chapters 14 and 15) presupposes a verified surgeon. The records discipline (Chapter 33) presupposes a credentialed operating physician. The complaint and recourse framework (Chapter 37) presupposes a clinic operating within a regulatory system. Without verification, the rest of the framework rests on assumption.

A verified surgeon can still produce a bad outcome. The framework's discipline is to do everything else that protects you in addition to the verification. The verification by itself is not enough; the verification missing is reliably catastrophic.

## Chapter 17 — Evaluating Destinations Without Rankings

This book does not rank countries. Country rankings, when they exist in medical tourism, are based on indicators that are either marketed (testimonials, before-and-after photographs, social media volume) or weakly correlated with patient outcomes (accreditation density alone, hospital size, technology brand presence). The data is too thin and too volatile to rank fairly.

What the chapter does instead: identifies the signals that actually matter at the destination level, and frames when destination matters more or less to the decision.

### The signals that matter

**Regulatory environment.** Is the destination's medical regulator functional? Are physicians and facilities subject to meaningful oversight? Are complaints addressed? Do regulators publish data? The strength of the regulatory environment correlates with the probability that things go wrong systemically.

**Accreditation density.** How many JCI-accredited (or ISQua-recognized accredited) facilities exist in the destination? More accredited facilities mean more competition on quality, more diffusion of best practices, and a stronger overall ecosystem. A destination with one accredited facility has a fragile ecosystem; a destination with twenty has a robust one.

**Outbreak history.** Has the CDC, WHO, or destination public health authority documented outbreaks of post-surgical infections, antimicrobial resistance, or other safety-relevant patterns? The DR pattern (CDC MMWR series on US-citizen deaths from cosmetic surgery; *M. abscessus* outbreaks) is the most-documented example, but other destinations have outbreak histories worth checking.

**Diplomatic friction and embassy support.** If a US patient has a complication or a dispute, can the US embassy provide useful assistance? Are diplomatic relations such that US-citizen healthcare incidents receive serious attention? This matters more than most patients realize for higher-stakes procedures.

**Civil-court enforcement.** If you need to file a malpractice claim or breach-of-contract claim, is there a functioning civil court system that will hear it? Most cross-border malpractice claims fail not on the merits but on jurisdictional and enforcement issues.

**Procedure-specific volume.** Does the destination have meaningful volume in the procedure category you are considering? Volume correlates with quality at the destination level (more cases means more standardization, more sub-specialty depth, more peer review). A destination with high cosmetic-surgery volume may not be a good destination for cardiac surgery, and vice versa.

### When destination matters more

Destination matters most when:

- **The procedure is high-stakes (Step 4 or 5).** The ecosystem of accredited facilities, regulatory oversight, and outcome data matters more for high-stakes procedures because the consequences of system failure are higher.
- **The procedure requires post-operative escalation capability.** Cardiac, complex orthopedic, complex bariatric — procedures where complications may require ICU, cath lab, or higher-level facility transfer.
- **The continuity of care matters.** Some procedures (oncology, complex chronic disease) have multi-year follow-up requirements that interact with the destination's healthcare system.

### When destination matters less

Destination matters less when:

- **The procedure is low-stakes (Step 1 or 2).** LASIK, cataract surgery, single dental implant — procedures with mature globalized standards and equipment.
- **The procedure is short and self-contained.** Single-area procedures with discrete recovery windows and limited follow-up requirements.
- **The patient has independent verification capability.** A diaspora patient with deep local knowledge, or a patient with a US-based specialist who knows the destination's market, can compensate for some destination-level data gaps.

### The diaspora-specific section

A meaningful share of US medical tourism is diaspora — patients seeking care in their country of origin. The framing is different from the cost-savings framing the rest of the book emphasizes.

**Why the diaspora frame is different.** The diaspora patient is not navigating an unfamiliar destination from a position of weakness. They have language, cultural fluency, family hosting, often professional relationships with local clinicians. The advantages of medical tourism (cost savings, access, expertise) are amplified for them; some of the failure modes (language barriers, cultural mismatches, lack of in-destination support) are mitigated.

**Where the diaspora frame can mislead.** "The doctor we always go to" is a real failure mode for diaspora patients. The cardiologist your family has trusted for decades may not be the cardiologist you would verify if you were making the decision from scratch. The hospital your family has used may not be the hospital with the best outcomes for your specific procedure. Family pressure to use a specific provider, on the basis of relationship rather than verification, is a documented pattern.

**What the framework requires.** The same as any other medical-tourism decision. Verify the surgeon, verify the anesthesia provider, verify the facility, get an independent second opinion. The diaspora frame does not exempt the patient from the framework; it changes the social dynamics around applying it.

**The Worksheet 19 (Diaspora-Specific Worksheet)** in the back matter is the operational form. It includes the specific questions diaspora patients should ask themselves and their families before committing.

Priya is at her sister-in-law's house in Mumbai. Her mother is in the next room with the family. Priya has the laptop open. The NABH directory is on the screen. The Apollo Mumbai facility is in the directory with current accreditation status; the date is current; the scope of accreditation covers cardiac procedures.

# Part IV — Quotes, Contracts, Money

## Chapter 18 — The True Cost of Care Abroad

The advertised procedure price is not the trip cost. This chapter explains the components, the worksheet that adds them up honestly, and the comparison framework that prevents the most common medical-tourism budgeting errors.

### The cost components

A complete cost picture for any medical-tourism procedure has four categories of cost:

**Direct procedure costs:**
- Surgeon fee.
- Facility fee (operating room, recovery, inpatient stay if applicable).
- Anesthesia fee.
- Implant or device cost (separate line item for many procedures).
- Lab work, imaging, pathology.
- Medications dispensed in destination.
- Consumables (garments, drains, surgical supplies).
- Pre-operative consultations and in-destination second opinions if relevant.
- Post-operative follow-up visits in destination.

**Travel and lodging costs:**
- International airfare for patient and companion.
- Local ground transportation (airport transfer, hospital-to-hotel transfers).
- Accommodation for the duration of the trip (often 7–21 days depending on procedure).
- Meals and incidentals.
- Visa, passport, and entry documentation costs if applicable.

**Indirect costs:**
- Lost income during travel and recovery period.
- Companion lost income if relevant.
- Child or elder care during absence.
- Pet boarding.
- Pre-procedure lost time for telemedicine second opinion, US-based pre-op work, records preparation.

**Risk and reserve costs:**
- Travel medical insurance premium.
- Cosmetic-tourism complication insurance premium if applicable.
- Complication reserve (some patients budget 10–20% of the procedure cost as a complication-treatment reserve).
- Revision reserve for procedure categories with meaningful revision rates (rhinoplasty, breast augmentation, joint replacement).
- US-based post-return follow-up costs.

### The True Cost Calculator

Worksheet 1 in the back matter is the complete tool. The structure:

Take the destination clinic's quoted procedure cost. Add each of the cost components above for your specific situation. The result is the true all-in cost.

A specific example to illustrate. Marcus's case from Chapter 1:

- **Direct procedure costs (Costa Rica TKA, bilateral, all-inclusive package):** $14,000.
- **Travel costs:** round-trip airfare for Marcus and his wife as companion ($1,200 each, total $2,400); ground transportation in Costa Rica ($300); accommodation for 14 days in San José ($1,800); meals and incidentals ($600). Travel subtotal: $5,100.
- **Indirect costs:** Marcus's lost income during 14 days of travel plus 4 weeks of post-return recovery before return to desk work — net of paid leave benefits, approximately $4,000.
- **Risk and reserve costs:** travel medical insurance ($400), complication reserve ($1,500), planned US-based post-return PT and follow-up imaging ($2,000). Reserve subtotal: $3,900.

**True all-in cost: $27,000.**

The destination clinic's quoted "$14,000 all-inclusive" was real but the true cost is $27,000. That is still less than the US out-of-pocket ($22,000 in Marcus's case for one knee — the bilateral procedure would have totaled $40,000+ at US prices). The savings are real but smaller than the headline figure.

### The comparison framework

The fair comparison is the true all-in international cost versus the true all-in US cost. Both need to be calculated on equivalent terms.

For US care: insurance-covered out-of-pocket plus all the indirect costs (lost income, follow-up, etc.). Most US patients have a clearer view of the insurance-covered out-of-pocket because the insurance company has provided estimates; the indirect costs are often missed.

For international care: the true all-in cost from the True Cost Calculator. This is often higher than the headline number but lower than the US all-in cost.

If the true differential is meaningful — at least 30–50% savings, often more — medical tourism may be the right financial answer. If the differential is small (under 20%), medical tourism may not justify the operational complexity. The framework asks the reader to do the math honestly.

### Cross-procedure cost comparisons

For readers considering multiple potential procedures (the chronic-condition portfolio reader like Eleanor), the cost calculator applies per procedure. The overall financial pathway is the sum, often staged across destinations and time.

Eleanor's case from Chapter 21 illustrates: four procedures (bilateral knee replacement, cataract surgery, full-mouth dental rehabilitation, hernia repair) across 18 months. Two procedures abroad (Costa Rica orthopedic, Mexico cataract), one abroad in a different destination (Los Algodones dental), one at home (hernia repair through US insurance). The sequencing is a financial optimization as much as a clinical one.

## Chapter 19 — The Quote Decoder, General

A complete medical-tourism quote is a document that allows you to evaluate the procedure offer against alternatives. Most quotes are not complete on first issue. The framework requires asking for what is missing, in writing, before deposit.

### What a complete quote contains

**Procedure specification:**
- The specific procedures by clinical name, not marketing name.
- The CPT or country-equivalent procedure codes where applicable.
- The operative-time estimate.
- The anesthesia-time estimate (for procedures requiring sedation or general anesthesia, this is often different from operative time).

**Provider specification:**
- The named surgeon, with full legal name, board certification, and registration number in the destination country's official register.
- The named anesthesia provider with credential.
- Any other named clinician involved in the case (the cardiologist, the radiologist, the pathologist if relevant).

**Facility specification:**
- The legal facility name (often different from the marketing name).
- The facility address.
- The accreditation status (which body, current accreditation date).
- The hospital tier (ambulatory surgery center, secondary hospital, tertiary hospital).

**Materials and devices:**
- For implant procedures: manufacturer, model, size range, source of supply.
- For procedures with consumables (drains, mesh, sutures, biological products): specifications where relevant.

**Inclusions and exclusions:**
- What is included in the quoted price (procedure, facility, anesthesia, implant, hospital stay, in-destination follow-up, accommodation, transfers — varies by clinic).
- What is excluded (often: pre-op work-up, complication management, revision surgery, take-home medications, accompanying-person costs, currency conversion fees).

**Financial terms:**
- The currency of the quote.
- The deposit amount and the deposit refund policy.
- The payment method accepted (wire, card, cash, financing through a partner lender).
- The payment schedule (deposit, balance, final settlement at discharge).
- The exchange-rate handling and any currency-related risks.

**Records and follow-up:**
- The records the patient will receive before departure.
- The in-destination follow-up schedule.
- The post-return communication arrangement (who is the patient's contact for at least 30–90 days post-procedure).
- The revision and complication policy.

A quote with all of these elements is a quote you can compare against alternatives. A quote missing any of these elements is a quote that requires follow-up before deposit.

### The "all-inclusive" framing

Many medical-tourism quotes use "all-inclusive" framing. This is fine when it is true — when the quote actually includes everything the patient will encounter. It is misleading when the quote includes the procedure but excludes complications, revisions, pre-op work, or items the patient will only learn about on arrival.

The framework's discipline: when a quote says "all-inclusive," ask in writing what specific items are *not* included. Get the exclusions list explicitly. The exclusions list is often where the cost escalation lives.

### Currency, exchange, and payment-fee specifics

For international medical tourism, currency matters operationally:

- **Quote currency vs. payment currency.** Some quotes are in USD; some are in the destination currency. Pay attention to which one. Currency exchange volatility within the planning window can shift the math.
- **Payment-method fees.** Wire fees (typically $30–$50 per outbound wire). Credit card foreign-transaction fees (typically 1–3% of transaction). Currency exchange spreads (the difference between mid-market and the rate your payment processor uses, typically 1–4%).
- **The "dynamic currency conversion" trap.** When paying at a point-of-sale or hotel, the system may offer to charge you in USD rather than the local currency. This conversion almost always has a worse exchange rate than your card's natural rate. Decline the USD option; pay in local currency.

For larger amounts ($10,000+), the right answer is often a single international wire from a US bank account, with the wire fee and the exchange rate negotiated in advance. Some patients use Wise or similar services for better exchange rates.

### Sample question list

For any quote you receive, ask the clinic in writing (not in chat — in email):

1. What is the named surgeon's full legal name and registration number in the destination's official register?
2. What is the named anesthesia provider's credential?
3. What is the facility's accreditation status and accreditation date?
4. What is the operative-time estimate? The anesthesia-time estimate?
5. For implant procedures: what manufacturer, model, and size range?
6. What specifically is included in the quoted price?
7. What specifically is excluded?
8. What is the deposit amount and refund policy?
9. What is the currency of the quote?
10. What records will I receive before departure?
11. What is the post-return follow-up arrangement?
12. What is the revision and complication policy?

A clinic that responds completely to these questions in writing has earned a place in your comparison set. A clinic that responds incompletely, evasively, or with marketing rather than specifications has told you something about how it operates.

## Chapter 20 — Deposits and Pre-Payment

A deposit is the moment your evaluation ends and your defense of your choice begins. The chapter is the operational discipline for that moment.

### Typical deposit ranges

Deposit sizes vary by procedure type and destination:

- **Dental tourism:** typically 10–25% of total. Often $500–$1,500.
- **Cosmetic surgery:** typically 20–40% of total. Sometimes higher (40–50%) at high-demand clinics.
- **Orthopedic and bariatric:** typically 25–40%. Often $2,000–$5,000.
- **Cardiac surgery:** typically 30–50%. Often $10,000+ given the procedure cost.
- **IVF:** often the full cycle cost is required up front.
- **Ophthalmology:** typically 10–25%.

The deposit secures: the surgical date, the implant order if relevant, the operating room time, the anesthesia provider's reservation, and (for some clinics) the international patient department's planning resources.

### The 15-question deposit readiness test

Worksheet 3 in the back matter is the operational form. The 15 questions:

1. Have I read and understood the complete written quote? (Per Chapter 19.)
2. Have I verified the named surgeon in the destination's official register? (Per Chapter 16.)
3. Have I verified the named anesthesia provider's credential? (For procedures requiring anesthesia.)
4. Have I verified the facility's accreditation independently? (Per Chapter 4.)
5. Have I obtained an independent second opinion? (Per Chapter 14 or 15.)
6. Have I read the deposit refund policy in writing?
7. Do I understand the refund policy's specific conditions and exclusions?
8. Have I read the revision and complication policy?
9. Do I have enough financial reserve to walk away from the deposit if the in-person exam surfaces problems?
10. Have I arranged a US-based post-return follow-up provider?
11. Have I arranged my companion (or substitute systems for solo travel)?
12. Have I notified my home physician of the planned procedure?
13. For implant procedures: have I confirmed the implant brand, model, and size range?
14. For high-step procedures: have I confirmed the transfer protocol if a complication requires higher-level care?
15. Am I making this decision on my own calendar, not the clinic's deposit deadline?

If you cannot answer yes to all 15 questions, the deposit is premature. The discipline is to delay the deposit until the questions are answered.

### Refund policies in plain English

Translate every clause before paying:

- *"Nonrefundable"* — you should be comfortable losing it.
- *"Refundable with 30 days' notice"* — travel disruptions or candidacy findings inside that window may cost you.
- *"Transferable"* — may mean only once, only within a year, only to the same procedure, only with the same surgeon.
- *"Applied to treatment"* — you may lose it if you decline to proceed after the in-person consultation.
- *"Subject to surgeon approval"* — no predictable rule; the clinic has discretion.
- *"Credit toward future surgery"* — not a refund. A coupon. Many clinics know patients who walked away once are unlikely to redeem.

The framework's discipline: ask the clinic, in writing, what happens in specific named scenarios:

- I cancel 30 days before travel.
- My flight is canceled two days before travel.
- The surgeon recommends a different procedure after the in-person exam and I decline.
- I am not medically cleared for surgery on arrival.
- The clinic reschedules me.

The answers to these specific questions tell you what the refund policy actually means.

### Payment method risk

How you pay affects your leverage if anything goes wrong:

- **Credit card:** provides dispute rights under the Fair Credit Billing Act (US patients), fraud protection, documentation. Some clinics charge surcharges (2–4%) for card payments; some limit card use to a portion of the total. For US patients, credit card payment offers the strongest dispute leverage if the clinic fails to deliver.
- **Bank wire:** cheaper for the clinic but harder to recover. Once funds clear the destination bank, your home bank generally cannot reverse the transfer. Treat any wire as effectively final.
- **Cash:** may earn a discount but creates record-keeping, safety, and customs-declaration issues. Avoid for amounts over a few hundred dollars.
- **Third-party payment apps (Wise, PayPal, Zelle):** vary in protections. Many offer no dispute rights for "goods and services" sent as personal transfers. A clinic that asks for payment to a personal account rather than a clinic merchant account is asking you to give up most of your leverage.
- **Financing plans (CareCredit, partner lenders):** may carry promotional interest, deferred interest, origination fees. The loan stays even if the surgery does not. Read terms carefully.

For deposits, the framework recommends: use credit card if the clinic accepts it (highest dispute leverage); otherwise use a single international wire from your home bank with the wire fee disclosed in writing. Never send funds to a personal account rather than the clinic's merchant account.

### Negotiating refund terms

The deposit terms are negotiable. The framework's discipline: do not accept the first written terms uncritically. Ask:

- Can the deposit be 25% rather than 40%?
- Can the deposit be refundable, less a reasonable administrative fee, if I cancel more than 30 days before travel?
- Can the deposit be transferable to a different surgical date or to a different family member at the same clinic?
- Can the refund be processed in USD even if the original payment was in destination currency?

Not every clinic will accept negotiations. Their refusal is information. Your willingness to walk away is leverage.

### What to do if the deposit is already sent

Some readers will pick up this book after a deposit is already in. The framework's discipline still applies — the deposit is not the procedure; the framework can still preserve some leverage.

If a deposit is already sent and concerns have surfaced:
- Document the concerns specifically in writing.
- Request the clinic respond to the concerns in writing.
- If the response is unsatisfactory, request the refund per the refund policy.
- If the clinic refuses a refund the policy supports, escalate to the credit card issuer (for chargeback if applicable), the destination's medical regulator, or your country's consumer protection authority.

The leverage post-deposit is lower than pre-deposit but is not zero. The framework's discipline applies at every stage.

## Chapter 21 — Insurance Reimbursement Realities

Chapter 5 covered the structural reality: US insurance default is no coverage for elective medical care abroad. This chapter is the operational chapter — what to do, what to ask, what to expect.

### The "verify with your insurer, not the clinic" rule

The single most useful insurance discipline: ask your insurer, not your destination clinic, what your specific plan covers.

Destination clinics often make general claims about insurance coverage that turn out to be wrong for your specific plan. Some clinics state that "reconstructive procedures are covered by US insurance," which is sometimes true and sometimes false depending on the plan, the procedure, and the documentation. Some clinics partner with "insurance facilitators" who promise to help with claims — these facilitators often have economic interests of their own.

The framework's discipline: call your insurer directly. Reference your specific plan ID and the specific procedure (with CPT code if you can find it). Ask:

- Is this procedure covered out-of-network?
- Is this procedure covered out-of-country?
- If yes to either: what is the reimbursement rate? Per the plan's UCR for the procedure, or at a percentage of the foreign invoice?
- What documentation will the insurer require for reimbursement?
- Is pre-authorization required? If yes, can it be obtained before the procedure?

Document the answers in writing — note the date, the representative's name, the reference number, and the specific quotes. Insurance representatives sometimes give different answers to the same question at different times. The documentation is your record if disputes arise later.

### Predetermination is not payment

A predetermination or preauthorization is an estimate of what insurance would pay if the treatment occurs as described. It is not a guarantee of payment. The final claim can still be denied or reduced because of missing documentation, plan limitations, alternate-benefit clauses, annual maximums, exclusions, or differences between proposed and completed treatment.

Use predetermination as a planning tool, not money in the bank. Do not spend reimbursement before you receive it.

### The UCR / alternate-benefit / out-of-network reimbursement gap

Repeated from Chapter 5 because it is the most underappreciated insurance issue in medical tourism:

When out-of-network or out-of-country care is reimbursed, payment is typically calculated against US Usual, Customary, and Reasonable (UCR) rates for the procedure, not the foreign invoice amount. A specific example:

- US patient has rhinoplasty in Mexico for $4,500.
- The plan covers rhinoplasty when functional (septoplasty + cosmetic) at a percentage of UCR.
- The plan's UCR for rhinoplasty in her zip code is $5,500.
- The plan covers 60% of UCR for out-of-network care: $3,300 reimbursement against a $4,500 invoice.
- Net out-of-pocket: $1,200 (plus any deductible/coinsurance).

This calculation may be favorable or unfavorable depending on the procedure and the destination. The framework's discipline: do the calculation explicitly, with the specific numbers from your plan, before assuming insurance contributes to your cost.

### Procedure codes (CPT, ICD-10) and documentation

For any insurance claim related to medical tourism, you will need procedure codes. The US-standard codes are CPT (Current Procedural Terminology) for procedures and ICD-10 for diagnoses.

International clinics often use country-specific coding rather than CPT. For US insurance claims, the codes need to be mapped to CPT equivalents. Some destination clinics will provide CPT codes; many will not.

The work-around: when you have the procedure name, look up the CPT code yourself (the American Medical Association maintains the CPT code set; many websites provide CPT lookup). Document the code on the claim submission. Your US insurer can clarify whether the code matches their understanding of the procedure.

### Travel medical vs. complication insurance — again

These two products are different. Travel medical covers non-elective emergencies during travel; cosmetic-tourism complication insurance covers complications of the elective procedure. Most patients need both.

The complication insurance product category is procedure-specific. For cosmetic surgery, products exist (CosmetAssure was the historical brand; the category has consolidated). For orthopedic, bariatric, cardiac, and other procedure categories, complication insurance is harder to find — some destination hospitals offer their own complication products, but third-party complication insurance for non-cosmetic procedures is limited.

For procedures with limited complication insurance availability, the framework's discipline is to budget the complication reserve into the True Cost Calculator. Self-insurance for complications is the default for many medical-tourism procedures.

### Composite Scenario C — Eleanor on hold

Eleanor is 65, recently retired, with four procedures in front of her — bilateral knee replacement, cataract surgery, full-mouth dental rehabilitation, and an inguinal hernia repair. She has a Medicare Advantage plan and an HSA built up from her pre-Medicare working years. She is on hold with her insurer.

Forty-eight minutes in, she has a notebook open. The notes so far:

The representative confirms one more thing: there is no situation in which the Medicare Advantage plan pays anything toward her destination procedures. The full cost is on her.

## Chapter 22 — Financing, Monthly Payments, Deferred Interest

A monthly payment can make a large procedure cost look like a small monthly cost. It can also hide the total cost. The chapter is the discipline that protects against the financing-trap pattern in medical tourism.

### The monthly-payment trap

A $20,000 procedure financed at 12% over 48 months is $527 per month. That sounds manageable. The total paid over 48 months is $25,300 — a $5,300 premium over the original procedure cost. That premium is the trap.

The trap compounds when:

- The procedure does not produce the expected result, but the loan continues.
- The procedure requires revision, which the loan does not cover.
- The patient assumes the monthly payment is "free" credit and does not budget the total cost.
- The procedure is one the patient would not have chosen at the all-in cost, but feels they can afford at the monthly payment.

The framework's discipline: when evaluating a financed medical-tourism procedure, calculate the total cost, not the monthly cost. The True Cost Calculator (Worksheet 1) includes a financing line for exactly this reason.

### Promotional interest and deferred interest

Many medical-financing products advertise "0% interest for 12 months" or similar promotional terms. Read the fine print:

- **Promotional 0% rate:** interest is genuinely 0% during the promotional period. After the period ends, the rate jumps to the standard rate (often 25%+).
- **Deferred interest:** interest accrues from day one of the loan. If the balance is paid in full by the end of the promotional period, the accrued interest is forgiven. If the balance is not paid in full — even if there is $1 remaining — the full accrued interest from day one becomes payable immediately.

The deferred-interest structure is the trap. Many patients assume promotional 0% and deferred-interest products are the same; they are not. A patient who assumes the 0% rate and misses the promotional deadline by a small margin can owe years of back interest.

Verify before signing: is this promotional 0% (interest does not accrue) or deferred interest (interest accrues but is forgiven if balance is paid by deadline)? Get the answer in writing.

### Origination fees, late fees, collateral risk

Other financing-product elements to verify:

- **Origination fees:** some lenders charge 2–5% of the loan amount up front. The fee is added to the principal and accrues interest.
- **Late fees:** typically $25–$50 per missed payment.
- **Prepayment penalties:** rare in US medical financing but worth checking.
- **Collateral:** unsecured for most medical financing (no collateral); secured products (home equity, retirement account loans) carry higher risk.

For US patients: CareCredit, Ally Lending, Prosper Healthcare, and similar are common medical-financing brands. Read the specific product terms before signing.

### What happens if the procedure does not go as planned

The most important financing question: does the loan survive the procedure not going as planned?

The answer is almost always yes. The loan is between the patient and the lender. The procedure is between the patient and the clinic. The two are not contractually linked.

If the procedure fails, requires revision, or produces a complication that disrupts your earning capacity, the loan continues. The framework's discipline: only finance a procedure if you can sustain the financing if the procedure does not go as planned.

This is especially relevant for procedure categories with meaningful revision rates (rhinoplasty 10–15%, breast augmentation 10–20% within ten years) and for high-stakes procedures where complications can affect earnings.

### Credit cards and chargebacks

For US patients, credit cards offer the strongest dispute leverage for medical-tourism payments. The Fair Credit Billing Act provides specific protections:

- The right to dispute charges for "services not rendered."
- The right to dispute charges for "services materially different from what was agreed."
- The right to dispute fraudulent charges.

For medical tourism specifically, the chargeback path is most useful when:

- The clinic fails to perform the procedure as described.
- The clinic performs a different procedure than agreed without informed consent.
- The clinic substitutes implant brands or models without consent.
- The clinic charges for services that were not delivered.

The chargeback is less useful for:

- Cosmetic dissatisfaction with the result of an as-agreed procedure.
- Complications of an as-agreed procedure.
- Disagreements about clinical interpretation.

For the chargeback to succeed, documentation matters. The written quote, the consent forms, the operative report, the photos before and after — all of these are evidence. The framework's records discipline (Rule 5) feeds directly into the chargeback discipline.

### The framework's financing discipline

Three rules for medical-tourism financing:

1. **Calculate the total cost, not the monthly cost.** The True Cost Calculator includes financing.
2. **Only finance a procedure if you can sustain the financing if the procedure does not go as planned.**
3. **Prefer credit cards over financing products for procedures under $15,000.** The dispute leverage is higher; the total cost is often lower if the balance is paid quickly.

For procedures over $15,000, the comparison is more nuanced. Financing products may offer better total-cost terms if you have the discipline to pay before promotional periods expire. Credit cards offer better dispute leverage but worse interest if balances carry. The choice depends on your situation.

## Chapter 23 — Step 1–2 Procedures: Ophthalmology, Diagnostic, and Dental

This chapter combines three procedure categories that share a complexity-ladder profile: low-surprise procedures with standardized technique, mature outcome data, and limited follow-up requirements. Most medical-tourism patients in these categories have straightforward, manageable experiences when the framework is applied.

### Ophthalmology

US LASIK volume: approximately 600,000 procedures per year. US cataract surgery volume: approximately 4 million procedures per year — the most common surgical procedure in the world. International medical tourism in ophthalmology: 100,000–200,000 US-outbound annually.

The procedures: LASIK, PRK, SMILE (refractive surgery); cataract surgery with intraocular lens (IOL) implantation; refractive lens exchange (RLE); some retinal procedures (vitrectomy, retinal detachment repair) at lower volume.

**Equipment platforms are globally standardized.** LASIK is performed with excimer laser systems — Allegretto Wave (now WaveLight EX500), Visx Star S4 IR, Schwind Amaris. The same platforms are in US clinics, Mexican clinics, Turkish clinics, South Korean clinics. The platform itself is not a differentiator between US and international care.

**IOLs for cataract surgery are globally standardized.** Alcon AcrySof, Johnson & Johnson Tecnis, Bausch + Lomb Crystalens. Premium IOL options (multifocal, accommodating, toric) are available everywhere.

**Cost differential:** US LASIK $2,000–$4,000 per eye → Mexico $700–$1,500, Turkey $1,000–$2,000, South Korea $1,500–$2,500. US cataract $3,000–$6,000 per eye → India $500–$1,500, Mexico $1,000–$3,000.

**The "premium IOL" upsell pattern.** Standard monofocal IOLs are typically covered by US insurance for cataract surgery; premium IOLs are out-of-pocket. International marketing emphasizes premium IOL options because the cost-savings story is most compelling there. Read the specifications: which IOL brand, which model, monofocal or premium, the specific optical performance trade-offs.

**Verification framework:** AAO (American Academy of Ophthalmology) credentialing standards; ASCRS (American Society of Cataract and Refractive Surgery) membership; for international destinations, the local ophthalmology board certification. Equipment platform verification (which Excimer laser, which IOL brand) matters as a marker of clinic investment in standardized international equipment.

**The framework's discipline for Step 1–2 ophthalmology:** verify the surgeon, verify the equipment platform, verify the IOL brand for cataract surgery. The verification is lighter than for higher-step procedures because the procedure itself is more standardized.

### Diagnostic and preventive

Executive physicals (multi-day comprehensive workups), whole-body MRI (Prenuvo-style preventive imaging), advanced biomarker panels, genomic testing, specialty imaging (PET-CT, advanced cardiac imaging). The category is growing fast — 50,000–100,000 US-outbound annually with younger demographic skew (40s–60s).

**Cost differential:** US executive physical (top-tier programs) $3,000–$10,000 → Thailand (Bumrungrad executive program) $500–$2,000, India $300–$1,500. US whole-body MRI (Prenuvo) $1,000–$2,500 → India $300–$600, Mexico $600–$1,200.

**Known concerns:** Whole-body MRI has documented false-positive overdiagnosis risk. The American College of Radiology (ACR) cautions about routine use in asymptomatic populations. Executive-physical content varies widely; some include comprehensive workups, others are essentially marketing.

**Framework discipline:** check the imaging center accreditation (ACR international equivalent), verify the radiologist credentials, understand the screening rationale before scheduling. For whole-body MRI specifically, ask: what is the plan if an incidental finding requires additional workup? The destination clinic is unlikely to manage that workup at home.

### Dental tourism

For dental tourism, Volume One of this series is the specialist volume. This survey is brief by design — the framework's discipline applies; the chapter's job is to point readers toward Volume One for depth.

**Volume:** 600,000–900,000 US-outbound dental tourists annually — the single largest medical-tourism category by volume.

**Cost differential:** US average implant + crown $4,500–$6,500 per tooth → Mexico $1,200–$2,500; Hungary/Turkey $700–$2,000. A full-mouth rehabilitation savings can be $30,000–$60,000.

**Top destinations:** Mexico (Los Algodones, Tijuana, Cancún), Costa Rica, Hungary, Turkey, Thailand, Croatia.

**Framework discipline (carries from Volume One):** the dental tourism survey-level reminders — verify the dentist's credentials (DDS/DMD US-equivalent in major destinations), verify specialty certifications where claimed (prosthodontics, oral surgery, periodontics), verify the lab's location and process for prosthetics, get the implant brand and model documented, understand the multi-trip pattern for All-on-X and similar procedures.

The dental survey is the lowest-stakes single-procedure category in medical tourism. The framework applies fully but the consequences of system failure are lower than for higher-stakes categories. Volume One covers the details.

### Composite Scenario D — Daniel scrolling at midnight

Daniel is 42. His father is 78, recently diagnosed with mild dementia, lives alone in the same suburban Phoenix neighborhood Daniel grew up in. The father's ophthalmologist confirmed cataract surgery is needed in the left eye, recommended within the next year. The US estimate, with the father's Medicare Advantage plan, is $4,500 out of pocket — the cataract surgery itself is covered, but the father wants a premium toric IOL that the plan does not cover.

## Chapter 24 — Bariatric Surgery

Bariatric surgery — the surgical treatment of severe obesity and its complications — is one of the most consequential medical-tourism categories. The procedures are inpatient (Step 4 on the complexity ladder). The follow-up requirements are multi-year. The outcomes correlate strongly with patient selection, surgical technique, and post-operative support. The framework's full discipline applies.

### Procedure types

**Laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy** (most common globally; approximately 70% of bariatric volume). The surgeon removes the lateral two-thirds of the stomach, leaving a banana-shaped narrow gastric sleeve. Standardized technique; mature outcome data; meaningful long-term revision rate (15–20% within 10 years).

**Roux-en-Y gastric bypass** (older, more complex, declining share). The surgeon creates a small gastric pouch and bypasses much of the stomach and the proximal small intestine. Higher early-complication risk than sleeve; better weight-loss outcomes for some patient profiles; revision rate 10–15% within 10 years.

**Duodenal switch (BPD-DS).** Highest weight-loss outcomes; highest complication rate; most demanding surgical technique. Lower volume; concentrated at high-volume specialty centers.

**Adjustable gastric banding** (LAP-BAND). Largely abandoned in US practice due to poor long-term outcomes and high removal rates. Some Mexico clinics still offer; the framework recommends against in most cases.

**Revision bariatric.** Conversion procedures (sleeve to bypass, bypass to BPD-DS) or revision of failed previous procedures. Higher complexity than primary; specialty surgical expertise required.

### The US context

US bariatric surgery volume: approximately 280,000 procedures annually (ASMBS — American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery). Insurance coverage varies dramatically by employer, state, and plan. Many patients qualify clinically but cannot obtain covered procedures within reasonable timelines. This is the primary driver of bariatric medical tourism.

US bariatric COE (Center of Excellence) designation through ASMBS requires specific volume, multidisciplinary support, and outcome reporting. COE centers have published 30-day mortality of approximately 0.1–0.3% for sleeve gastrectomy, complication rates of 5–10%.

### The Mexico bariatric corridor

Approximately 25,000–40,000 US patients travel abroad annually for bariatric surgery; the Mexico bariatric corridor (Tijuana, Mexicali, less commonly Cancún) is the dominant destination for US patients. The corridor has matured over fifteen years. Several Tijuana centers carry ASMBS-COE-equivalent recognition and publish outcomes in peer-reviewed bariatric journals.

**Cost differential:** US sleeve gastrectomy cash price $15,000–$25,000 → Mexico $4,000–$8,000; Turkey $3,000–$6,000.

### The pre-operative evaluation problem

US bariatric programs require, before surgery: psychological assessment, nutrition consultation, smoking cessation documentation, sometimes a 6-month medically supervised weight-loss program, occasionally cardiac clearance, blood work and imaging. This pre-operative pathway is required by US insurance for coverage and by US bariatric surgeons as a standard of practice.

Mexico bariatric clinics often do not require this full pre-operative pathway. Some require minimal pre-op evaluation; some accept patients on the basis of self-reported history.

The framework's discipline: complete the US-standard pre-operative evaluation regardless of where the procedure is performed. The cost of US-based pre-op psychology, nutrition, smoking cessation, and clearance is modest ($500–$2,000); the value in terms of procedure outcome and revision risk is substantial.

Pre-op psychological evaluation in particular matters. Bariatric outcomes correlate with pre-existing mental health (binge eating disorder, depression, anxiety, body dysmorphic disorder). A patient who skips the psych eval because the destination clinic does not require it is foregoing one of the strongest predictors of long-term success.

### The follow-up problem

ASMBS protocol requires multi-year follow-up after bariatric surgery — 1-year, 2-year, then annual indefinitely. The follow-up monitors nutritional status (deficiencies are common), weight trajectory, comorbidity resolution, and revision indicators.

Mexico destination clinics, by geography, cannot deliver multi-year follow-up. The framework's discipline: identify a US-based bariatric surgeon willing to manage long-term follow-up before booking. Many US bariatric surgeons will manage the follow-up of a patient operated abroad if the records are complete, the operation was standard, and the patient is compliant. Some will not. Identify which category your local options are in before traveling.

The framework recommendation: a US-based bariatric surgeon agreement in writing, prior to traveling, that they will provide the post-operative follow-up. The agreement may include a per-visit fee schedule; budget it into the True Cost Calculator.

### The long-term revision rate

Revision rates at 10 years post-bariatric: sleeve gastrectomy 15–20%, gastric bypass 10–15%, BPD-DS 10–15%. The revision is often a conversion procedure to address weight regain, complications, or refractory comorbidities.

The framework's discipline: budget the revision possibility into the financial decision. A revision procedure 5–10 years post-primary may cost $15,000–$25,000 abroad or $25,000–$45,000 at home. The patient who could not survive losing the primary deposit is unlikely to absorb a revision cost five years later.

### Sources

ASMBS guidelines and Center of Excellence framework; IFSO (International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity); *Obesity Surgery*; *Surgery for Obesity and Related Diseases* (SOARD); peer-reviewed long-term bariatric outcome literature.

### Framework summary for bariatric medical tourism

- Complete US-standard pre-op evaluation (psychological, nutritional, medical clearance) regardless of destination.
- Verify the surgeon, the anesthesia provider, and the facility (Chs 16, 4).
- Establish US-based post-operative follow-up arrangement before booking.
- Verify the destination clinic's ASMBS-COE-equivalent recognition and published outcomes.
- Budget for revision possibility (10–20% within 10 years).
- For Mexico corridor specifically: the framework's discipline plus an honest assessment of the destination clinic against the published outcome data.

## Chapter 25 — Cosmetic Surgery and Hair Restoration

For cosmetic surgery and hair restoration, Volume Two of this series is the specialist volume. This survey is brief — its job is to convey the framework's positions at survey depth and point readers to Volume Two for the full treatment.

### The volume and the cost differential

US-outbound cosmetic surgery and hair restoration: 300,000–500,000 patients annually — the second-largest category after dental. Cost differentials are large: US BBL $8,000–$15,000 → Dominican Republic/Colombia $3,000–$6,000; US rhinoplasty $8,000–$15,000 → Mexico $3,000–$5,000; US hair restoration $8,000–$15,000 → Turkey $2,000–$4,000.

### The carried positions from Volume Two

Volume Two of this series, *The Cosmetic Surgery Field Guide*, established several editorial positions that this volume carries forward and applies more broadly. The most consequential:

**The CDC MMWR DR data.** The CDC's *Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report* volume 73, number 3 documented 93 US-citizen deaths in the Dominican Republic from cosmetic surgery, 2009–2022, with 92 percent involving gluteal fat transfer (BBL). The pattern is concentrated in non-accredited facilities performing combination procedures on patients with comorbidities. This dataset informs Chapter 30's recommend-against editorial position on BBL with intramuscular fat injection in non-accredited facilities.

**The BBL mortality rate.** Peer-reviewed estimates: 1 in 3,000 to 1 in 6,241 — the highest of any cosmetic procedure. The mortality concentrates in patients with the documented risk-factor combination: BMI above the ASPS-recommended caution threshold, combination procedures (BBL plus another procedure in the same session), intramuscular fat injection technique, non-accredited ambulatory facility.

**The demonstration-case waiver pattern.** Some clinics offer reduced procedure costs in exchange for the patient signing a waiver allowing the clinic to use the patient's pre- and post-procedure photographs in marketing. The waiver is often a single-page document with significant rights given up. Volume Two covers the pattern in detail.

**BIA-ALCL surveillance.** For patients with textured breast implants, the FDA recommends symptom-based surveillance for breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma. Volume Two covers the surveillance framework.

### Hair restoration techniques

Two dominant techniques in the international cosmetic-tourism market:

**FUE (Follicular Unit Extraction).** Individual follicular units are extracted from the donor area (typically the back of the scalp) and transplanted to the recipient area. Minimal scarring; widely available; the standard technique in the Turkey-dominated international hair-restoration market.

**DHI (Direct Hair Implantation).** Similar to FUE but with a direct implanter pen that places follicles into the recipient site without first creating recipient sites separately. Slightly faster; equivalent outcomes; widely available.

**FUT (Follicular Unit Transplantation).** Older strip-based technique. A strip of donor scalp is removed, follicular units harvested under microscope, and transplanted. More scarring; less common abroad; still performed at some US clinics.

For Turkey-corridor hair restoration specifically: FUE and DHI dominate. Volume of procedures is high; outcomes are generally good when the framework is applied; cost differential is large.

### Framework summary for cosmetic medical tourism

- The CDC MMWR DR data informs the recommend-against position (Chapter 30). For BBL specifically: intramuscular fat injection in non-accredited facilities should be avoided.
- BIA-ALCL surveillance for textured breast implants is a long-term issue; Volume Two covers it.
- The demonstration-case waiver pattern is widespread; read it carefully.
- For hair restoration: FUE and DHI techniques are widely standardized; the framework's discipline of verifying the surgeon, the technique, and the clinic applies.
- Volume Two's specialist coverage is the right next read for any reader seriously considering cosmetic surgery or hair restoration abroad.

## Chapter 26 — Reproductive Medicine (IVF)

In vitro fertilization and related reproductive procedures (IVF, ICSI, egg/sperm freezing, fertility preservation, preimplantation genetic testing, donor cycles) are a substantial and growing medical-tourism category. US IVF cycle costs ($15,000–$25,000 including medications) and patchy insurance coverage drive the outbound.

### The procedures

**IVF (in vitro fertilization).** Eggs retrieved, fertilized in vitro, embryos transferred. The foundational procedure.

**ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection).** A single sperm is injected directly into a single egg. Used for male-factor infertility or when conventional IVF has failed.

**Egg/sperm freezing (cryopreservation).** Eggs or sperm preserved for later use. Egg freezing has become substantially more common in the past decade.

**Preimplantation genetic testing (PGT).** PGT-A screens for aneuploidy (chromosomal abnormalities); PGT-M tests for specific gene mutations; PGT-SR tests for structural rearrangements. Different tests have different evidence bases for clinical benefit.

**Donor cycles.** Donor eggs or donor sperm. Legal framework varies dramatically by destination.

### Volume and demographics

US IVF cycle volume: approximately 330,000 cycles annually (SART). Estimated 30,000–60,000 US patients travel abroad annually for fertility treatment. Demographics: predominantly women 35–44; some male-factor cases; donor cycles for same-sex male couples or single intended parents.

### Cost differential

- US IVF cycle (including medications): $15,000–$25,000.
- Czech Republic IVF: $4,000–$7,000.
- Spain IVF: $5,000–$8,000.
- Greece IVF: $4,000–$6,000.
- Mexico IVF: $5,000–$10,000.
- India IVF: $3,000–$6,000.

### Cycle success rates — the publishable advantage

Reproductive medicine has unusually transparent outcome data. SART (Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology) publishes US clinic-level success rates by maternal age band. ESHRE publishes European data. Top international centers typically publish equivalent data in peer-reviewed literature.

US average live birth rate per cycle:
- Under 35: 30–35%.
- 35–37: 25–30%.
- 38–40: 15–20%.
- 41+: 5–10%.

Top international centers report comparable bands. The verification framework includes checking the clinic's published success rates against the SART/ESHRE baseline for your age and indication.

### The legal landscape (must address carefully)

Reproductive medicine has dramatic legal variation by destination:

**Donor anonymity.** UK, Sweden, Netherlands, Germany have removed donor anonymity in recent years. Spain, Greece, Czech Republic still allow anonymous donors. The choice affects long-term family considerations.

**Sex selection.** Legal in US, Mexico, parts of Thailand, India. Banned in UK, most of EU.

**Commercial surrogacy.** Legal in some US states (varies state by state); Ukraine had been a major destination pre-2022; Mexico's legal status varies by state; India banned commercial surrogacy for foreigners in 2015; many European countries ban commercial surrogacy. The legal complexity is substantial.

**Preimplantation genetic testing.** PGT-M for specific mutations widely available globally; PGT-A for aneuploidy screening has variable acceptance. UK regulations restrict embryo selection more than US.

For any IVF medical tourism, understand the legal framework at the destination before committing. The legal framework affects donor selection, surrogacy options, parental rights, and citizenship of children. Consult with both a reproductive endocrinologist and a family-law attorney with international experience for any procedure involving donor gametes or surrogacy.

### Specific destinations

- **Czech Republic (Prague, Brno):** mature IVF tourism corridor; high volume; well-regarded outcomes; affordable; some donor anonymity considerations.
- **Spain (Barcelona, Madrid, Valencia):** another mature European corridor; legal framework favorable for donor anonymity.
- **Greece (Athens, Crete):** lower volume than CZ/ES but growing; cost-competitive.
- **Mexico (Guadalajara, Tijuana):** strong for US patients; legal landscape varies by state.
- **India (Mumbai, Delhi):** lowest cost; some legal complexity for foreign patients with donor cycles.
- **Cyprus, Greece, and several other Mediterranean destinations:** various specializations.

### Framework summary for reproductive medical tourism

- Verify the reproductive endocrinologist's credentials (REI fellowship-equivalent) and the clinic's published success rates against SART/ESHRE for your age band.
- Understand the legal framework at the destination — donor anonymity, sex selection, surrogacy, PGT regulations.
- For donor cycles or surrogacy: consult with family-law attorneys with international experience before booking.
- Budget realistically — IVF requires multiple cycles for some patients; the cost differential expands with cycle count.
- Logistics: IVF requires the patient to be in destination for approximately 2–3 weeks per cycle (stimulation, retrieval, embryo development, transfer).
- US-based follow-up arrangement is straightforward for IVF (any US OB-GYN can manage pregnancy follow-up); for donor cycles, a US REI is often involved in the follow-up.

### Sources

SART data; ESHRE Annual Reports; ASRM (American Society for Reproductive Medicine) guidelines; *Human Reproduction*; *Fertility and Sterility*; reproductive endocrinology peer-reviewed literature.

## Chapter 27 — Orthopedic Surgery (Joint Replacement and Spine)

Orthopedic surgery — joint replacement and spinal procedures — is one of the highest-volume medical-tourism categories for inpatient surgical procedures. The combination of large cost differentials, mature outcome data, globally standardized implants, and recoverable post-operative requirements makes it a procedure category where the framework applies particularly well.

### Procedures covered

**Joint replacement:**
- Total hip arthroplasty (THA).
- Total knee arthroplasty (TKA).
- Partial knee arthroplasty (UKA).
- Hip resurfacing (less common).
- Shoulder arthroplasty (lower volume in medical tourism).

**Spine:**
- Lumbar fusion (open or minimally invasive).
- Cervical fusion.
- Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF).
- Lumbar disc replacement.
- Cervical disc replacement.

### Volume and demographics

US joint replacement volume: approximately 700,000 TKAs and 350,000 THAs annually (AAOS). US lumbar fusion: approximately 500,000 annually. Estimated 50,000–80,000 US patients travel abroad annually for orthopedic procedures.

### Cost differential

- US THA: $30,000–$50,000 cash; $15,000–$25,000 out-of-pocket on PPO insurance after deductible.
- India THA at top tertiary centers: $7,000–$12,000.
- Mexico THA: $13,000–$20,000.
- Costa Rica THA: $14,000–$20,000.
- Thailand THA: $12,000–$18,000.
- Turkey THA: $11,000–$16,000.
- US lumbar fusion: $80,000–$150,000.
- India lumbar fusion: $10,000–$15,000.

### Outcomes data — the registry advantage

Orthopedic surgery has the best outcome registry data of any medical-tourism procedure category. AJRR (American Joint Replacement Registry) covers more than 2.5 million procedures across more than 1,300 US institutions. NJR (UK), AOANJRR (Australia), SHAR (Sweden) provide international registry data.

10-year implant survivorship for THA/TKA at top US and international centers: 90–95%. The variance between centers is meaningful — top centers in the 95% range, lower-volume centers in the 85–90% range. Volume and surgeon experience correlate with outcome.

### Implant manufacturer dominance

The orthopedic implant market is dominated by four manufacturers: Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, Johnson & Johnson DePuy Synthes, Smith + Nephew. The same implant brands (and often the same specific models — Stryker Triathlon knee, Zimmer Persona, DePuy Attune) are available in US tertiary centers, Costa Rican JCI-accredited hospitals, Indian Apollo facilities, Turkish Anadolu hospitals.

This is one of the operational advantages of orthopedic medical tourism: the implant the patient receives abroad is often identical to the implant the patient would have received at home. The verification: ask for the implant manufacturer, model, size range, and warranty terms in writing before deposit. Worksheet 5 captures this.

### The "robotic-assisted" marketing pattern

Several robotic surgical platforms (Stryker Mako, Zimmer ROSA, Johnson & Johnson Velys) are now widely available globally. International clinics often market robotic-assisted procedures heavily.

The peer-reviewed outcome data on robotic-assisted joint replacement, compared to non-robotic at experienced surgeon hands, does not show statistically significant differences in long-term outcomes. The robotic platform may improve component positioning precision in some hands; the impact on patient-reported outcomes and long-term survivorship is not differentiated in published studies.

The framework's discipline: do not pay a premium for robotic-assisted technology assuming it produces better outcomes. The peer-reviewed data does not support that assumption. If a destination clinic uses a robotic platform and the cost is comparable, that is fine. If the robotic platform comes with a substantial price premium, the premium is for marketing, not for differentiated outcomes.

### Rehabilitation requirements

The dominant logistical issue for orthopedic medical tourism is post-operative physical therapy. Standard protocol for TKA: 6 weeks of formal PT, often 3 sessions per week, in the recovery and early rehabilitation phase. Lumbar fusion: similar PT requirements over 8–12 weeks.

The patient who travels abroad for joint replacement returns home with a long PT trajectory ahead. The framework's discipline: identify a US-based PT provider before traveling. Confirm the PT provider will accept records from the destination procedure and develop a rehabilitation plan. Budget the PT cost (typically $80–$150 per session in the US; insurance may cover) in the True Cost Calculator.

### DVT/PE prophylaxis and return flight

Major joint replacement carries elevated risk of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism in the post-operative period. Standard prophylaxis: anticoagulation (often a low-molecular-weight heparin or direct oral anticoagulant) for 14–28 days post-procedure; compression stockings; early ambulation.

The return flight from the destination requires specific timing consideration. Most orthopedic surgeons recommend minimum 10–14 days post-procedure before flying for major joint cases. The risk of DVT/PE on long-haul flights is elevated during the post-operative window.

The framework's discipline: book the return flight conservatively. A 14-day post-procedure return is the minimum; 21 days is often better. Travel medical insurance should cover any in-destination extension if complications arise.

### Composite Scenario D continued — Daniel's father, the hip replacement

Daniel's father (78, mild dementia) has had successful cataract surgery in Mexicali three months earlier (Chapter 23). The father's left hip has progressed; the US orthopedic surgeon now recommends total hip replacement.

The framework's discipline applies again, with the additional consideration of the father's mild dementia. Daniel works through Worksheet 17 (Procedure Complexity Self-Rating). The hip replacement is Step 4 — inpatient, implant, general anesthesia. The verification work is more substantial than for the cataract surgery.

Daniel arranges a telemedicine second opinion through Cleveland Clinic Online Medical Second Opinion ($2,400; the family is paying it). The Cleveland Clinic orthopedic specialist reviews the father's case. The opinion: total hip replacement is appropriate; the surgical risk is elevated given the father's age and mild dementia but not prohibitive; the procedure should be performed at a hospital with full medical-surgical capability (not an ambulatory surgical center), with explicit attention to post-operative delirium prevention.

The Mexicali eye clinic had been a good match for the cataract surgery. For the hip replacement, the framework says: a higher-tier facility with inpatient capability and geriatric anesthesia expertise. Daniel and his sister consider a JCI-accredited tertiary hospital in San José in Costa Rica (JCI-accredited, established orthopedic program, geriatric-anesthesia expertise documented). The cost is higher than the Mexicali option would have been but well below the US price.

The family proceeds with the planning. Daniel is the companion (Chapter 34). The sister will visit at days 5 and 10. The father's US PCP has agreed to coordinate post-return follow-up. The PT will be at a US facility within 24 hours of return.

The framework expands with the procedure's complexity. The Step 4 procedure requires more verification than the Step 2 procedure. The discipline scales appropriately.

### Sources

AAOS clinical practice guidelines; *Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery* (JBJS); *Journal of Arthroplasty*; AJRR Annual Report; NJR Annual Report; peer-reviewed orthopedic surgical outcome literature.

## Chapter 28 — Cardiac Surgery

Cardiac surgery is the highest-clinical-stakes survey in this book. The procedures (coronary artery bypass grafting, valve replacement, valve repair, structural cardiac intervention) carry meaningful perioperative mortality even at top US tertiary centers. The procedures require ICU capability, immediate cath lab access, and full medical-surgical hospital infrastructure. The patient's underlying cardiac status often constrains medical-tourism options.

For most patients with stable cardiac disease, cardiac surgery abroad at a top tertiary center is a possible option but requires the framework's full discipline. For patients with acute or unstable cardiac status, cardiac surgery should not be a medical-tourism decision.

### Procedures

**Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).** Multi-vessel coronary artery disease. The most common cardiac surgery globally.

**Valve replacement.** Aortic, mitral. Mechanical prostheses require lifelong anticoagulation; biological prostheses have shorter durability but no anticoagulation requirement.

**Valve repair.** Mitral valve repair particularly; preserves the native valve when feasible.

**Septal defect repair.** Atrial septal defect (ASD), ventricular septal defect (VSD).

**Structural intervention:**
- TAVR (transcatheter aortic valve replacement).
- MitraClip (transcatheter mitral repair).
- Other structural interventions.

### Volume and mortality

US CABG volume: approximately 340,000 annually (STS National Database). US TAVR volume: growing rapidly, now exceeding surgical aortic valve replacement at many centers. Estimated 10,000–20,000 US patients travel abroad annually for cardiac procedures — concentrated at top tertiary centers, particularly the Indian cardiac corridor.

STS National Database 30-day mortality for isolated CABG at US centers: 1.5–2.5% (risk-adjusted; varies by center and patient profile). India top tertiary center mortality at Narayana Health, Apollo Hospitals Chennai, and similar reported in peer-reviewed literature: 1.0–2.0% — comparable to US median, sometimes better than US median.

The variance is the key. Top international tertiary centers can match US outcomes. Lower-volume international centers can have substantially worse outcomes. The variance within international care is wider than the variance within US care.

### The India tertiary corridor

The Indian cardiac surgery market is concentrated at a small number of high-volume tertiary centers:

- **Apollo Hospitals** (multiple locations; Chennai is the cardiac flagship).
- **Narayana Health** (Bangalore, Kolkata, Mysuru, others). Founded by Dr. Devi Shetty as a high-volume cardiac surgery program.
- **Fortis Healthcare** (multiple locations).
- **AIIMS** (All India Institute of Medical Sciences) and several teaching hospitals.

These centers operate at volumes that exceed many US programs (Narayana's Bangalore Health City performs over 30 CABGs per day at peak). The published outcomes in peer-reviewed Indian and international cardiology journals are well-regarded. The cost differential is large.

**Cost differential:** US CABG $80,000–$200,000 (depending on complexity, comorbidities, length of stay) → India top tertiary $5,000–$10,000; Thailand $15,000–$30,000; Turkey $20,000–$35,000; Mexico $25,000–$45,000.

### The structural-not-essentialist framing

This survey, like Volume Two's framing of the Dominican Republic data, is careful to distinguish destination from clinic. The recommendation is not "do not travel to India for cardiac surgery"; it is "do not travel to a non-tertiary center for cardiac surgery." India happens to have several of the world's top high-volume cardiac centers. India also has many lower-volume centers that do not match those outcomes. The destination is not the variable; the specific center is.

### The framework's discipline for cardiac medical tourism

Step 5 procedure. The framework's full discipline applies, with additional cardiac-specific requirements:

**Tertiary hospital verification.** The facility must have full medical-surgical hospital capability — ICU, immediate cath lab access, 24/7 cardiologist coverage, full anesthesia and perfusion staffing. Ambulatory surgical centers cannot host cardiac surgery; if a clinic markets cardiac procedures at an ambulatory facility, the framework says no.

**Surgeon verification.** Cardiothoracic surgery board certification (STS membership for US-credentialed; country-equivalent board for international). Procedure-specific volume — the surgeon should perform the specific procedure (CABG, aortic valve replacement, etc.) at meaningful volume.

**Anesthesia provider verification.** Cardiac anesthesia is a sub-specialty. Verify the anesthesia provider's cardiac anesthesia experience.

**Outcomes data.** The destination center should be able to provide its STS-like outcomes data (volume, 30-day mortality, complication rates) in writing. If the center cannot, the verification is incomplete.

**ICU capability.** Verify ICU bed availability, ICU staffing ratios, ICU-specific cardiology coverage.

**Transfer protocol.** What happens if a complication exceeds the facility's capability? Where is the nearest higher-level facility? What is the transfer time?

**Anticoagulation management for return flight.** Patients with mechanical valves require lifelong anticoagulation; the return-flight management of anticoagulation is a specific medical question. Patients with biological valves have shorter post-operative anticoagulation; the timing of return flight should be discussed explicitly.

### When cardiac medical tourism is reasonable

The categories where it can be reasonable:
- Stable cardiac patient.
- Mature surgical recommendation from US cardiology (not made under acute pressure).
- Robust pre-operative evaluation.
- Top-tier international tertiary center with documented outcomes.
- US-based cardiology continuity arrangement for post-return follow-up.
- Anticoagulation management plan for return flight worked out in advance.

### When cardiac medical tourism is wrong

The categories where it is wrong:
- Acute or unstable cardiac status (acute MI, decompensated heart failure, acute valve dysfunction).
- Significant comorbidities requiring rapid US follow-up.
- Patient unable to manage post-operative anticoagulation independently.
- Lack of US-based cardiology continuity arrangement.
- Lower-volume international centers without verifiable outcome data.

### Sources

STS National Database; ACC/AHA guidelines (American College of Cardiology / American Heart Association); *NEJM*, *Lancet*, *JACC*, *JAMA Cardiology*, *Annals of Thoracic Surgery*. Narayana Health and Apollo Hospitals published outcomes.

*A reusable copy of the cardiac-specific anesthesia provider checklist (named provider, cardiac anesthesia experience, ICU coverage, perfusion staffing) is Worksheet 6 (Anesthesia Provider Verification) in the back matter.*

## Chapter 29 — Oncology

Oncology is the most editorially-sensitive survey in the book. The category includes two very different things: evidence-based cancer care that happens to be available abroad (sometimes at much lower cost than at home), and unapproved alternative cancer therapies marketed to desperate patients. The framework treats them very differently. This chapter is about the evidence-based category; Chapter 30 covers the recommend-against pattern for alternative therapies.

### Scope of evidence-based oncology medical tourism

The legitimate cases:

**Surgical oncology** at top international tertiary centers with full tumor board capability. India's Apollo cancer centers, Singapore's National Cancer Centre, Mexico's INCan (Instituto Nacional de Cancerología), Israel's Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center, Germany's Heidelberg University Hospital. These centers perform standard-of-care surgical oncology at international tertiary tier.

**Specific chemotherapy regimens** that are not FDA-approved but are evidence-based in other regulatory jurisdictions. Some immunotherapy combinations, some cancer vaccines, some targeted-therapy combinations. The legitimate cases require careful navigation — the FDA's expanded access program (clinicaltrials.gov) is the right starting point for US patients seeking access to investigational drugs.

**Radiation oncology specialties.** Proton beam therapy has limited US availability; some patients travel to Mexico (Centro Oncologico Internacional), Czech Republic (Proton Therapy Center Czech), or Asia for proton beam treatment of specific cancer indications.

**Second-opinion treatment planning** at top international centers. India Apollo, Singapore NCCS, MSK International for US patients (Ch 15) — second-opinion review of US treatment plans, sometimes with adjustments.

### What is not in scope for the evidence-based survey

- Replacement of standard cancer care with unapproved alternative therapies.
- "Cure for cancer" marketing from clinics offering laetrile, Gerson protocol, high-dose vitamin C, oxygen therapy, hyperthermia, or other unapproved interventions.
- Treatment delay to seek alternative therapies when standard treatment would be effective.

These are covered in Chapter 30 (Patterns We Recommend Against), not here.

### Volume

Cancer treatment tourism volume is hardest to estimate of any category. Tens of thousands of US patients travel annually across multiple modalities. The volume for unapproved alternative therapies is harder to estimate but has been documented in investigative journalism on Mexican alternative cancer clinics, German biological cancer clinics, and other destinations.

### Cost considerations

Cancer surgery costs are often comparable or modestly less abroad at top tertiary centers. Chemotherapy drug costs in India are 10–30% of US prices (different drug supply chains, manufacturing, regulatory environments). Proton beam radiation: limited US centers and higher US costs make destination care meaningfully more affordable.

### Continuity-of-care reality

Cancer treatment is rarely a single episode. Most cancer care involves:
- Diagnosis and staging.
- Initial treatment (surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, immunotherapy — often a combination).
- Follow-up imaging and monitoring for several years.
- Surveillance for recurrence indefinitely.
- Adjustment of long-term hormonal therapy or maintenance regimens.

Medical tourism for the initial treatment phase requires US-based oncology continuity arrangement before traveling. The cancer patient who travels abroad for surgery without arranging US-based surveillance has a continuity-of-care gap that meaningfully affects long-term outcome.

### Framework discipline for oncology medical tourism

- The destination center must be a recognized cancer center (NCI-CCC-equivalent international designation). Apollo Cancer Centers, Singapore NCCS, Tel Aviv Sourasky, Heidelberg, etc.
- The center must have tumor board capability — multidisciplinary case review involving surgical, medical, and radiation oncology.
- US-based oncology continuity must be arranged in writing before traveling.
- A telemedicine second opinion from a US academic cancer center (MD Anderson, MSK, Mass General) is strongly recommended for any oncology medical-tourism decision.
- For clinical-trial access abroad: the FDA's expanded access program is the right first step before considering travel.

### Composite Scenario E — Karen and her mother

Karen, 51, school administrator. Her mother (74) is diagnosed with stage III breast cancer. The US oncologist's recommended treatment plan is standard: neoadjuvant chemotherapy (four cycles), then surgery (mastectomy with sentinel node biopsy), then radiation, then five years of endocrine therapy (aromatase inhibitor given the mother is postmenopausal). The estimated US out-of-pocket cost, with the mother's good insurance, is meaningful but not catastrophic.

Karen has read about cancer treatment abroad. She has been told India offers some of the same chemotherapy regimens at one-fifth the cost. She is not yet sure whether medical tourism is the right answer. She is also worried she is missing something — whether the US treatment plan is actually the best available.

Karen submits her mother's records to MSK International Second Opinion ($1,800; 12-day turnaround). The MSK breast oncology specialist reviews the records, the imaging, the pathology, and the proposed US treatment plan.

The MSK opinion confirms the US plan as appropriate: neoadjuvant chemotherapy is the right starting point; the regimen is standard (AC followed by taxane); the surgical plan is consistent with current guidelines; the radiation indication is clear; the endocrine therapy is appropriate. The MSK specialist notes one consideration: a specific genomic test (OncotypeDX or MammaPrint) might inform endocrine therapy duration and the question of whether chemotherapy is necessary given the tumor's molecular profile.

Karen brings the MSK opinion to her mother's US oncologist. The oncologist orders the genomic test. The result: the tumor is in the intermediate-risk band; chemotherapy benefit is borderline; the oncologist and Karen's mother decide together to proceed with chemotherapy given the patient's preference for the more aggressive approach.

The treatment proceeds in the US. The framework did its job: the second opinion confirmed the plan, refined one element, and gave the family confidence in the decision. Medical tourism was considered and rejected for this case — the financial differential did not justify the continuity-of-care complexity for a multi-year oncology treatment course.

The mother is at year 2 of follow-up at the time the book is written. She is doing well. The MSK opinion is in her records. The decision was the right decision for this family. The framework helped them see why.

### Sources

NCI publications and clinicaltrials.gov; ASCO position statements; FDA expanded access program documentation; *Journal of Clinical Oncology*; *JAMA Oncology*; *NEJM*; peer-reviewed oncology outcome literature.

## Chapter 30 — Patterns We Recommend Against

This is the editorial-position chapter. It identifies a small number of patterns in medical tourism where the documented evidence is sufficient that "ask better questions" framing is, in the book's judgment, insufficient. The book takes positions. It sources them. The reader who disagrees with the positions has access to the same sources and can reach their own conclusion.

The framing matters. The chapter does not recommend against the procedures these patterns describe. It recommends against the *patterns* — specific combinations of procedure, technique, facility, credentialing, or timing where the documented evidence supports the recommendation.

### 1. Transplant tourism in documented organ-trafficking corridors

The book takes the strongest editorial position on transplant tourism. The patient who travels abroad for organ transplant in specific documented corridors participates in a market that international professional societies, federal agencies, and human-rights tribunals have explicitly condemned.

**Sources:**
- *Canadian Medical Association Journal* (CMAJ): multiple peer-reviewed articles documenting Chinese organ-trafficking from prisoners of conscience.
- *The Lancet*: commentary and position pieces on transplant tourism.
- *China Tribunal Final Report* (2020): independent tribunal chaired by Sir Geoffrey Nice QC concluded that forced organ harvesting from prisoners of conscience continues in China.
- US State Department Trafficking in Persons (TIP) reports (annual): organ-trafficking sections.
- The Transplantation Society position statement on transplant tourism.
- WHO Guiding Principles on Human Cell, Tissue, and Organ Transplantation (2010 update).

**Documented corridors** in peer-reviewed literature:
- **China.** Kidney, liver, heart, lung — sourced from prisoners including political prisoners. The China Tribunal's findings on forced organ harvesting are detailed and sourced.
- **Pakistan.** Kidney — economically coerced donors documented in *Lancet* and *Transplantation* series.
- **Egypt.** Kidney — documented donor coercion.
- **Philippines.** Kidney — until 2008 ban; some illegal market persists.
- **Bangladesh.** Kidney — documented poverty-driven donor markets.

**The book's position:** do not travel to these corridors for organ transplant. The pattern is not consumer protection; it is participation in a market international professional societies and federal agencies have explicitly condemned.

**Distinguishing legitimate cross-border transplant programs:** kidney exchange consortia, paired exchange across friendly jurisdictions, living-related donor transplant performed at properly regulated international centers — these exist and are distinct from the trafficking corridors. The framework's discipline is to verify the specific program and its regulatory framework, not to assume all international transplant equals trafficking.

### 2. Stem-cell, regenerative, exosome, peptide, and longevity tourism

**Sources:**
- FDA Public Health Advisory letters on unapproved stem-cell products (2017, 2019, 2023).
- International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR) Consumer Guide to Stem Cell Therapies.
- Peer-reviewed harm case series in *Nature*, *Cell Stem Cell*, and ophthalmology literature (documented vision loss from intravitreal stem-cell injection).

**The pattern:** clinics market "stem cell therapy," "regenerative medicine," "exosome therapy," "peptide therapy," or "longevity treatments" for indications ranging from cosmetic to chronic-disease to anti-aging. Most products are not FDA-approved. Most clinics have no published clinical trials at the marketed indication. Most marketing claims are not supported by peer-reviewed evidence.

**The book's position:** treat the entire category as a warning, not a buyer's guide. The category is structured in a way that buyer-protection framing cannot improve outcomes — the products themselves are not regulated as medicine, the supply chain is opaque, and the outcome data is absent.

**Distinguishing FDA-approved cellular therapies:** CAR-T for specific cancers (approved indications only), FDA-approved cord-blood for specific hematologic conditions, FDA-approved bone marrow transplant. These are legitimate cellular therapies with regulatory oversight. The marketing umbrella of "stem cell" and "regenerative" includes these and many others; the framework distinguishes.

### 3. Unapproved alternative cancer therapies abroad

**Sources:**
- NCI factsheets on complementary and alternative medicine for cancer (cancer.gov).
- Peer-reviewed harm case series in *Journal of Clinical Oncology* and *JAMA Oncology* on patients who chose alternative therapies over evidence-based treatment.
- Investigative journalism (ProPublica, BBC, NYT, *Washington Post*) of specific clinics: Mexican alternative cancer clinics (Hoxsey, Gerson, others); German biological cancer clinics in Frankfurt and elsewhere; specific Texas-Mexico border clinics.
- FDA warnings on specific unapproved cancer treatments.

**The pattern:** clinics market unapproved cancer therapies — laetrile, high-dose vitamin C, oxygen therapy, hyperthermia, German biological cancer clinics, Mexican alternative cancer clinics, "natural" cancer treatments. The marketing targets patients with serious cancer diagnoses, often those who have been told their prognosis is poor with standard treatment. The documented harm pattern is patients dying of treatable cancers after spending savings on regimens with no clinical evidence.

**The book's position:** unambiguous. Do not delay or replace evidence-based cancer care with unapproved alternative therapies abroad. This is distinct from Chapter 29's coverage of evidence-based oncology medical tourism (clinical trial access, top tertiary center care at international cancer centers, specific approved-elsewhere chemotherapy regimens). The recommend-against pattern is the marketing of unapproved alternative therapies, often to vulnerable patients.

### 4. Surgery in non-accredited facilities

Carried forward from Volume Two of this series. The pattern: facilities that market surgical procedures without verifiable accreditation by a recognized body (JCI, AAAASF, ISO 9001 in combination with healthcare-specific accreditation, ISQua-recognized national bodies).

**Sources:**
- JCI, AAAASF, ISQua accreditation documentation and public directories.
- CDC outbreak investigations of surgical-site infection clusters at specific non-accredited facilities.
- Investigative journalism on facility-deficiency patterns.

**The book's position:** for any surgical procedure (Step 3 and above on the complexity ladder), the facility must hold verifiable accreditation by a recognized body. Facilities advertising "international standards" or "internationally accredited" without naming a specific body are not verifiable. The verification path: search the relevant body's public directory.

### 5. BBL with intramuscular fat injection

Carried from Volume Two. The CDC MMWR series on 93 US-citizen deaths in the Dominican Republic, 2009–2022, with 92 percent involving gluteal fat transfer, anchors the editorial position. The pattern is intramuscular fat injection technique in non-accredited facilities.

**The book's position:** BBL with intramuscular fat injection in non-accredited facilities should be avoided. The procedure has the highest mortality of any cosmetic procedure documented in published literature. Volume Two covers the technique-specific framework and the safer-pattern alternatives.

### 6. Unverifiable surgeon credentials

Carried from Volume Two. The pattern: clinics that cannot or will not provide the surgeon's verifiable credentials (board certification, registration number in the destination country's official register, country of certification).

**The book's position:** do not deposit, do not travel, do not consent for any procedure where the surgeon's credentials are not verifiable in a public registry before deposit. This is the single strongest predictor of documented patterns of harm.

### 7. Unverifiable anesthesia provider credentials

Carried from Volume Two. The pattern: clinics that cannot or will not provide the named anesthesia provider's credentials for procedures requiring sedation, regional, or general anesthesia.

**The book's position:** the anesthesia provider's credentials matter as much as the surgeon's. The CDC MMWR data on US-citizen cosmetic surgery deaths in the DR includes anesthesia-related deaths. The verification path is the same as for surgeons.

### 8. Surgery in the 12 months after major adverse life events

The pattern: cosmetic surgery, bariatric surgery, or other elective procedures undertaken within 12 months of major adverse life events (death of partner or parent, divorce, job loss, major diagnosis). Peer-reviewed outcome literature in cosmetic and bariatric surgery shows higher rates of dissatisfaction and regret in this window.

**The book's position:** not a categorical prohibition. The timing question. The framework's discipline is to delay elective procedures by 12 months from major adverse events when feasible. Some readers will choose to proceed; the framework is to make the choice with awareness of the published outcome literature.

### Framing summary

The book takes editorial positions on the eight patterns above because the documented evidence is sufficient that "ask better questions" framing is, in our judgment, insufficient. The positions are sourced. The reader can verify the sources independently.

The book does not take editorial positions on:
- Any specific destination country.
- Any specific facility (the framework provides verification tools; specific facility recommendations are not made).
- Any specific surgeon (same).
- Any specific implant brand or manufacturer.
- The patient's individual decision to proceed with a procedure once the framework has been applied.

The reader who applies the framework and reaches a decision the book would not have made is exercising the patient autonomy the framework is designed to protect. The patterns the book recommends against are the patterns where the framework, when applied, would itself recommend against the procedure.

## Chapter 31 — When the Framework Says Yes

The book has spent thirty chapters on the framework and the patterns to avoid. This chapter is the constructive complement: what success looks like when the framework holds. The composite scenarios that have been built across the previous chapters resolve here.

### What success looks like

A medical-tourism procedure that has gone well — the framework applied, the verification complete, the procedure performed at a verified facility by a verified surgeon, the records intact, the recovery on plan, the follow-up arranged — has a specific shape. It is undramatic. The patient is at year 1, year 5, year 10 with the procedure they sought, the cost savings they planned for, and the continuity of care they arranged.

The success is not the procedure alone. It is the procedure plus the framework. The patient who treats medical tourism as a project — with phases, deliverables, verification checkpoints, and contingency plans — has access to outcomes the patient who treats it as a transaction does not.

### Composite Scenario A continued — Marcus at 8 months

Marcus is now eight months post-procedure. The bilateral total knee arthroplasty was performed at a JCI-accredited tertiary hospital in San José in Costa Rica by a JCI-credentialed orthopedic surgeon. The surgeon's full name and Costa Rican cédula are in Marcus's records. The implant — Stryker Triathlon, both knees — is documented with manufacturer lot numbers and warranty registration. The anesthesia record is signed by a named board-certified anesthesiologist with documented orthopedic anesthesia experience. The operative report is two pages, signed.

The all-in cost was $14,800 for both knees, including a 14-day stay in San José for surgery, recovery, and the first post-operative follow-up. The True Cost Calculator that Marcus completed at week 5 of the 60-day plan estimated $27,000 all-in including the travel, the lost income, the complication reserve, and the US-based PT. The actual all-in cost ended up at $26,400 — within a few percent of the estimate.

Marcus returned to desk work at week 8. The PT was at a clinic 15 minutes from his house in Denver; the PT provider had agreed to accept records from the Costa Rican procedure before Marcus traveled. The PT lasted 8 weeks for the left knee and 6 weeks for the right knee. His US PCP saw him at week 4 and week 12 post-procedure; both visits were routine.

At month 8, Marcus is back to softball. The knee on the right side is fully recovered. The knee on the left side is at about 95% of expected function; one more month of focused PT will close the gap.

He has not yet decided what to do with the framework moving forward. His brother — the one who agreed to be the home contact during the trip — has told him he is considering hip replacement for his own arthritis in three years. The framework Marcus learned, and the records he has from his procedure, are now resources for someone in his family.

The procedure was successful. The framework was the reason it was successful. The two are not separable in Marcus's account of what happened. He says, at year 1 follow-up, that he wishes he had read the book six years ago — when the right knee first started hurting and he started ignoring it because the US prices were impossible. The framework, he says, is not what made the procedure good; it is what made the decision good. The decision is what made the procedure possible.

### What the framework produces over time

The success patterns share specific features:

- The patient has complete records. The operative report, the anesthesia record, the implant documentation, the discharge instructions, the post-operative photographs. The records survive the transition home; they continue to serve the patient at year 5, year 10.
- The patient has US-based clinical continuity. A primary care physician who knows the procedure was performed abroad and has the records. A specialist who can manage post-procedure follow-up when needed. The continuity is the difference between a closed-loop procedure and one that becomes the foundation for further care.
- The patient has financial closure. The True Cost Calculator was honest; the actual cost was within range; the patient did not need to take on undisclosed debt or compromise other financial goals.
- The patient has emotional closure. The decision was made on the patient's calendar, with the patient's framework, with the patient's chosen support. The patient does not feel that medical tourism happened *to* them; they feel they did medical tourism — actively, deliberately, with discipline.

The framework, applied, produces all four of these. The framework, not applied, often produces none.

### What this chapter is and is not

This chapter is not a celebration of medical tourism. It is an accurate description of what the framework can produce when it is applied seriously. The book is not promoting medical tourism; it is preparing readers who choose medical tourism to do it well.

Many readers, after applying the framework, will conclude medical tourism is not the right answer for their situation. That conclusion is also a success of the framework. The framework's job is to make the decision defensible. Both the decision to proceed and the decision not to proceed can be defensible.

# Part VI — Recovery, Records, Coming Home

## Chapter 32 — Before You Leave the Destination

The most important hours of medical tourism are the hours between the procedure and the flight home. The records you secure during this window are the records that serve you for the next decade. The procedure is over; the work is not.

This chapter is the operational discipline for the final hours in destination.

### The closing-clinic checklist

Before you leave the clinic for the last time — typically the discharge appointment or the final follow-up — you should have:

**Operative report.** Signed by the operating surgeon. Includes the procedure performed, the surgical findings, the implants placed (with manufacturer and model), any intra-operative complications, and the post-operative orders. In English or with certified translation. This is the most important single document.

**Anesthesia record.** Signed by the anesthesia provider. Includes the anesthetic agents used, the doses, the duration, any intra-operative anesthetic events.

**Discharge summary.** Includes the post-operative course in the destination, the current medications and dosing, the expected recovery trajectory, the flight-clearance criteria, the warning signs that warrant immediate medical attention.

**Implant documentation.** For any procedure involving implants (orthopedic, breast, cardiac, dental): the implant patient cards. Each card identifies the manufacturer, model, size, lot number, UDI (Unique Device Identifier), and the anatomic site of placement. The cards are physical documents the patient carries; they are also the basis for any future warranty claim or implant-specific tracking.

**Pathology report.** If tissue was removed and sent for pathology, the pathology report and the supporting microscopy slides if applicable.

**Pre-operative and post-operative imaging.** X-rays, CT scans, MRI, ultrasound. Both the images themselves (on USB drive or DICOM media) and the radiologist reports.

**Itemized invoice.** With all charges, all payments, and any outstanding balance. The currency, the exchange rate, the payment method.

**Warranty terms.** For implants and other procedure-relevant warranties. In writing.

**Photographs.** For cosmetic, reconstructive, and certain other procedures: pre-operative photos from the destination clinic's standardized views; post-operative photos at the discharge visit.

**Follow-up appointment schedule.** What in-destination follow-up was performed; what post-return follow-up the destination clinic recommends.

**Contact information.** The surgeon's direct contact (or the international patient department's senior coordinator) for at least 30–90 days post-procedure. The arrangement for handling clinical questions after return.

### Records format

The format of the records matters operationally:

**Operative report, anesthesia record, discharge summary, pathology report, radiologist reports:** PDF documents in English or with certified translation. Digital copies on USB drive plus printed copies for the carry-on.

**Imaging:** DICOM format on USB drive. DICOM is the medical imaging standard; any qualified US radiologist or specialist can open DICOM. Avoid clinics that provide imaging in proprietary formats or only in JPG/PNG image previews.

**Implant cards:** Physical patient cards from the manufacturer. The destination clinic should provide these; some manufacturers also have online registration portals that allow the patient to register the implant for warranty purposes.

### Translation considerations

For records in languages other than English, certified translation is the framework's discipline. Some destinations provide records in English by default; some provide bilingual records; some provide records in the destination language only.

For records in Spanish (Mexico, Costa Rica, Colombia, etc.), translation services are widely available at modest cost ($30–$80 per document for certified translation). For Hindi, Mandarin, Turkish, Korean, or other destination languages, certified medical translation services exist but at higher cost ($50–$150 per document).

Budget the translation cost into the True Cost Calculator. The records are not useful at home if they are not in a language the US clinician can read.

### The departure-day photograph

For cosmetic, reconstructive, and certain orthopedic procedures: take a final standardized photograph at the discharge visit. This becomes the baseline against which post-return recovery and final outcome are measured.

The photograph should be in the same lighting, angle, and pose as the pre-operative reference photograph. The destination clinic's photographer typically handles this; verify before the visit that the photographs will be provided in digital format.

### Records to carry vs. records to ship

For the flight home:
- **Carry-on:** physical implant cards; printed copies of operative report, discharge summary, and current medication list; USB drive with all digital records; any imaging media. The carry-on records are the records that matter if luggage is delayed.
- **Checked or shipped:** copies of the same; the originals if applicable.
- **Cloud backup:** before leaving destination, upload all digital records to a personal cloud (Google Drive, Dropbox, iCloud) accessible from home. Two-factor authentication.

The framework's discipline: at least three copies of the records, in at least two physical formats, in at least two locations.

The day-of-procedure verification (operating surgeon identity, anesthesia provider identity, no-non-emergency-additions waiver, financial settlement clarity) is captured separately in Worksheet 9 (Day-of-Procedure Checklist) in the back matter.*

**Imaging and lab:**
8. Pre-operative imaging (DICOM format).
9. Post-operative imaging.
10. Lab results from in-destination workup.
11. Specialist consultation reports (cardiology, anesthesiology pre-op).

20. Itemized invoice with all payments.

**Photographic:**
22. Pre-operative and post-operative photographs in standardized views.

### The records-packet cover sheet

Worksheet 10 in the back matter is the cover sheet. It summarizes the procedure, the providers, the implants, and the location of each record in the packet. The cover sheet is what a US clinician sees first; it should be readable in 60 seconds and direct the reader to the supporting documents.

### File formats and storage

**Physical binder:** the gold-standard storage. Three-ring binder with section dividers, printed copies of all paper documents, sleeves for the implant cards, a CD/DVD/USB envelope for imaging media. The binder lives at home and is the master copy.

**Personal cloud storage:** Google Drive, Dropbox, iCloud, OneDrive. All digital documents uploaded; folder structure that mirrors the binder. Accessible from anywhere with two-factor authentication.

**Trusted family member or attorney:** a duplicate copy of the cover sheet plus the most important documents (operative report, implant cards, current medication list) with a trusted family member or attorney. This is the contingency copy if the primary copies are lost or destroyed.

### What to do when the clinic will not release records

Some destination clinics resist releasing records — through inaction, language barriers, demand for additional fees, or genuine bureaucratic difficulty. The framework's discipline:

- Request the records in writing before the procedure (in the pre-deposit communication, in the consent forms).
- Request again during the in-destination stay (at the operative day, at the discharge visit).
- Request a final time at the discharge appointment, with the request and the response documented.
- If the clinic refuses or delays, escalate to the international patient department's senior staff, the medical regulator in the destination country, and the patient's home-country embassy as appropriate.

For US patients with credit-card payment of the procedure, persistent records refusal is grounds for chargeback initiation under the Fair Credit Billing Act. The threat of chargeback often produces records release.

## Chapter 34 — The Companion's Role

For medical tourism involving Step 3 or higher procedures (inpatient, general anesthesia, multi-day recovery), a companion is one of the highest-leverage decisions the patient makes. The companion is not the substitute for the patient's framework; they are the support that allows the framework to operate when the patient is not in a position to operate it themselves.

This chapter is the companion's role for medical tourism generally. Volume Two of this series covers the companion role for cosmetic surgery specifically.

### The pre-trip companion briefing

Before traveling, the companion should know:

- The procedure: what it is, what to expect, what could go wrong.
- The location: the destination, the clinic, the hotel, the alternate hospital if escalation is required.
- The framework: the patient has been working a framework; the companion should know what it requires.
- The medical history: the patient's current medications, allergies, baseline cognitive function, any specific concerns.
- The records plan: where the records will be stored, how to access them in an emergency.
- The escalation criteria: when to call the clinic, when to call emergency services, when to consider transfer to a higher-level facility.

The briefing is best done a week or more before travel, with the procedure documents in hand. A written briefing (one or two pages) summarizing the above is appropriate.

### Day-of role

On the day of the procedure:

- The companion accompanies the patient to the clinic.
- The companion may be present at the pre-operative consultation if the destination clinic permits.
- The companion is NOT a substitute for the patient's consent. Non-emergency consent decisions remain with the patient. (See Volume Two, Chapter 23, for the non-emergency consent rule.)
- The companion waits during the procedure (typically in a waiting area provided by the clinic).
- The companion is present in the recovery room when the patient regains consciousness.
- The companion notes anything unusual: medication errors, communication problems, post-operative findings the destination clinic does not adequately explain.

### Recovery-week medical observation duties

In the days following the procedure, the companion's role expands. The companion is the patient's eyes and ears when the patient is medicated, fatigued, or otherwise impaired. Specific observations matter:

- Vital signs trajectory (fever especially — fever above 101°F / 38.3°C warrants clinic contact).
- Wound appearance (changes in drainage, redness expansion, increasing pain).
- Mental status (confusion, particularly in older patients post-anesthesia).
- Signs of DVT/PE (calf swelling or pain; chest pain, shortness of breath, unilateral leg edema).
- Signs of infection.
- Medication adherence (the patient may forget doses or take incorrect doses while recovering).

The companion is not the clinician; the companion is the observer. The observation that matters most: changes from baseline. The companion who knew the patient before the procedure can recognize changes the clinic staff may miss.

### The non-emergency consent rule

Carried from Volume Two: the companion does NOT consent on behalf of the patient for non-emergency procedure changes or additions. If the destination clinic recommends a change to the procedure plan after the patient is sedated or otherwise unable to consent, the companion's role is to defer the decision until the patient can consent.

This rule applies particularly to:
- Procedure expansions ("while we have you under, we recommend also doing X").
- Additional procedures discovered intraoperatively.
- Equipment or implant substitutions.

Emergency surgical decisions (uncontrolled bleeding, organ injury, true life-threatening complications) are different — these are clinician decisions, not consent decisions, and the framework defers to clinical judgment in true emergencies.

### Escalation framework

The companion's escalation framework:

- **Routine concerns:** observe, document, mention at next clinic contact.
- **Concerning symptoms:** call the destination clinic. The international patient department typically has 24/7 contact.
- **Emergency symptoms** (chest pain, shortness of breath, confusion, severe pain, uncontrolled bleeding): call emergency services in the destination AND notify the destination clinic. If emergency services cannot respond in time or to appropriate level, transport to the higher-level hospital identified during pre-trip planning.

The framework's discipline: the companion has the destination's emergency number, the alternate hospital's contact, and the patient's records accessible at all times. The contingency plan was made before it was needed.

### The diaspora companion

For diaspora medical tourism, the companion is often a family member. The family member has language fluency, local knowledge, and personal investment in the patient's care. These are advantages.

The framework's discipline still applies. Family members can be reluctant to escalate. Family members can be invested in protecting the destination clinician's relationship (the "doctor we always go to" pattern). Family members can have different views on the appropriate trade-off between patient autonomy and family decision-making.

The framework's recommendation for diaspora companion arrangements: the patient and the family companion should explicitly discuss the framework, the consent rules, and the escalation criteria before traveling. The clarity reduces friction in the moments when friction would otherwise be expensive.

### Composite Scenario G — Daniel as companion

Daniel is at the destination hospital in San José, day three of his father's hip-replacement recovery. The procedure was uneventful; the surgeon's discharge note projected a routine course. Daniel's father is in a private room with intermittent compression devices on his calves, a low-molecular-weight heparin injection on the medication chart, and a physical therapist scheduled to begin ambulation later that afternoon.

Daniel is doing the companion's work as specified in the pre-trip briefing he and his sister built together. He has the records-packet checklist on his phone. He has the destination clinic's international-patient-department contact and the alternate higher-level hospital's contact saved alongside the local emergency number. He has the father's medication list, allergies, and baseline cognitive function in a one-page note he can hand to any clinician within thirty seconds.

The morning's observation: his father is more confused than yesterday. Not dramatically — answers questions, recognizes Daniel, oriented to place. But slower. The companion's discipline (Chapter 34) is to flag changes from baseline, not to diagnose. Daniel calls the international patient department. The nurse arrives within ten minutes. The internist on call evaluates the father, orders a brief workup (electrolytes, urinalysis), and confirms what Daniel had suspected: mild post-operative delirium in an older patient, common, usually transient, watch for worsening.

The note in Daniel's phone now says: *Day 3 morning. Mild confusion noted, slower responses. Internist evaluated 10:40. Workup ordered. Plan: hydration, minimize sedating medications, reassess at 14:00.* Daniel sends a copy to his sister in Boston and to his father's US PCP, who has been receiving daily updates.

By the afternoon physical therapy session, his father's orientation has improved. He stands, with the walker, for the first time. The physical therapist documents the milestone. Daniel takes a photograph for the records folder.

The companion did the companion's work. He observed, documented, escalated promptly, deferred clinical judgment to clinicians, and kept the home network informed. He did not consent to anything on his father's behalf. He did not minimize what he was seeing. He did not assume the destination clinic would notice what he had noticed. The framework operated because Daniel operated it.

The hip-replacement recovery continued without further events. The flight home was at day fourteen, with the surgeon's flight-clearance criteria met. The first US-based PT session was the morning after arrival.

## Chapter 35 — Arranging Follow-Up Care at Home

The post-return follow-up is not optional. Every medical-tourism procedure benefits from US-based clinical continuity. For some procedures (bariatric surgery, joint replacement, breast implants, cardiac surgery, oncology), the continuity is essential.

This chapter is how to arrange the follow-up before traveling — the framework's discipline that prevents post-return clinical gaps.

### Start before you travel

The framework's discipline: identify the US-based follow-up provider before booking the destination procedure. The arrangement is in writing, in advance. The patient who arrives home with destination records and no US-based clinician to receive them faces a delay at the worst time.

The arrangement varies by procedure:

- **Bariatric surgery:** US-based bariatric surgeon agreement for multi-year follow-up. Identified during the 60-day plan, confirmed in writing before booking.
- **Joint replacement:** US-based orthopedic surgeon or primary care physician with arthroplasty experience for the routine follow-up; PT provider arranged for the rehabilitation.
- **Breast implants:** US-based plastic surgeon or breast surgeon for the FDA-recommended surveillance schedule.
- **Cardiac surgery:** US-based cardiologist for the routine follow-up; cardiac surgeon contact for any concerns specific to the procedure.
- **Oncology:** US-based oncologist for the surveillance and any recurrence concerns.
- **IVF:** US-based OB-GYN or REI for pregnancy follow-up or future cycle planning.

### The willingness gradient of US-based providers

US-based providers have a range of willingness to accept patients from international procedures. The general gradient:

**Highest willingness:**
- Primary care physicians (most are willing to coordinate follow-up regardless of where the procedure was performed).
- US-based providers in the same network or hospital system as the patient's prior care.
- Providers who have professional relationships with the destination clinic or its US-based partners.

**Moderate willingness:**
- Specialists who do not perform the procedure themselves but follow patients (e.g., an internist following a post-bariatric patient).
- Providers in markets with substantial medical tourism patient populations (Phoenix, Miami, Houston, Los Angeles).

**Lower willingness:**
- US-based surgeons who specialize in the procedure performed abroad. The dynamic is partly economic (the surgeon did not perform the procedure; the surgeon is now asked to follow a case they would have charged for at home) and partly liability-related (the surgeon may be uncomfortable assuming clinical responsibility for a procedure they did not perform).
- Solo practitioners with limited capacity.
- Providers in tightly-managed care networks (some Kaiser, some Geisinger arrangements).

The framework's discipline: identify the willing providers before traveling. The conversation with a potential US-based follow-up provider should be transparent: "I am planning a procedure abroad. The records will be available before I see you. I am asking for [routine follow-up / surveillance / continuity of care]. What is your willingness and what is the cost?"

### The framing-the-request script

Carried from Volume One and Two. The framing that produces the best response:

"I am planning a [procedure] in [destination]. The records will be available to me before I see you. I am NOT asking you to substitute for the destination clinician or to take clinical responsibility for the procedure itself. I am asking for [specific follow-up service]: post-procedure check-in at 6 weeks, surveillance at 1 year, or coordination of any complications that may arise. Would you be willing to schedule that, and what is the cost?"

This framing does three things: it respects the US-based provider's professional independence; it limits the scope of the request to the manageable part; it makes the financial arrangement explicit. Most US-based providers respond positively to this framing.

### When local specialists are unwilling

If the specific specialist a procedure requires is unwilling to provide follow-up, the framework's options:

- **Primary care physician as coordinator.** The PCP can manage routine follow-up and coordinate referrals when specialist input is needed. Most PCPs are willing.
- **Different specialist in the same area.** Some markets have multiple specialists; one may be willing where another is not.
- **Telemedicine specialist from the destination.** The destination surgeon may offer post-return follow-up via telemedicine. Acceptable for some procedure categories; less suitable for procedures requiring physical examination.
- **Concierge medicine practices.** Some concierge medicine practices specifically accept medical-tourism patients. Higher cost; more flexibility.
- **Specialist relationships through patient advocacy organizations.** Some medical-tourism patient communities (online and in-person) have informal networks of US-based providers willing to follow medical-tourism patients.

The framework's discipline: do not abandon the follow-up because the first US-based specialist is unwilling. The continuity matters too much.

### Insurance and payment for home follow-up

Most US insurance covers US-based follow-up care that is medically necessary, regardless of where the original procedure was performed. The framework's discipline:

- For the follow-up appointment itself: verify in-network status and likely out-of-pocket cost before scheduling.
- For specific tests ordered by the follow-up provider (lab work, imaging): same verification.
- For ongoing surveillance (annual visits, periodic imaging): the cumulative cost matters; document expectations.

For procedures where the US insurance does not cover any portion of the procedure-related care (some elective cosmetic), the follow-up cost is out-of-pocket. Budget it into the True Cost Calculator.

### Composite Scenario C continued — Eleanor at year 1 follow-up

Eleanor is 14 months post-bilateral knee replacement, 9 months post-cataract surgery, 6 months post-dental rehabilitation. She has a thick records folder. The bilateral knee replacement was at a JCI-accredited tertiary hospital in San José; the cataract surgery was at the Mexicali clinic Daniel had used for his father (a connection Eleanor made through the medical-tourism patient community); the dental rehabilitation was in Los Algodones.

Her US PCP has reviewed all four sets of records — the three medical-tourism procedures plus the in-network hernia repair that happened at home. The PCP knows the implant brand and serial for both knees, knows the IOL brand for the cataract, knows the dental specifications. The records are filed in the patient's chart at the PCP's clinic.

At her year-1 follow-up appointment with the PCP, Eleanor discusses how she is doing. The knees are working as expected. Vision is better than it has been in fifteen years. Dental rehabilitation has held; one minor adjustment was made at a US-based dentist's office in month 8. The hernia repair was uneventful.

The PCP notes the follow-up surveillance recommendations from each destination clinician: annual joint replacement surveillance until further notice; the FDA-recommended breast-implant surveillance does not apply (no implants); routine dental follow-up every 6 months; no surveillance required for the hernia repair beyond the post-operative period.

The PCP also confirms what was already clear from the framework: Eleanor's HSA balance is now $4,200 after the procedures. She has time before the next major medical decision. The framework, applied across four procedures over 18 months, has delivered the result Eleanor planned for.

She mentions to the PCP that her brother in Phoenix is considering hip replacement, and is asking her about the framework. The PCP nods. The patient who applies the framework becomes a resource for the next patient who needs it.

The procedure abroad is not the end of medical tourism. The continuity at home is the framework's longer arc.

# Part VII — When Things Go Wrong

## Chapter 36 — Complications: Recognizing, Escalating, Transferring

Some procedures will have complications. Most are minor and manageable. Some are serious. A small number are emergencies that require immediate intervention. The framework's discipline is to recognize early, escalate promptly, and transfer to a higher level of care when warranted.

This chapter is the procedure-agnostic version of the complication framework. Procedure-specific considerations (BBL fat embolism, cardiac post-op anticoagulation, joint replacement DVT, etc.) appear in the procedure surveys and in the specialist volumes.

### Warning signs across procedure types

Recognize the patterns that warrant immediate attention:

**Fever above 101°F / 38.3°C.** Particularly with chills, malaise, or signs of localized infection. The destination clinic should be notified. For procedures with implants or surgical wounds, a fever is potentially a surgical-site infection that may require IV antibiotics and possibly readmission.

**Expanding redness at a surgical site or wound.** A small zone of erythema (redness) at a fresh incision is normal. Spreading redness, particularly if accompanied by increased warmth or pain, suggests cellulitis or surgical-site infection.

**Increasing pain after initial post-operative improvement.** The standard recovery trajectory is gradually decreasing pain. Pain that worsens after a period of improvement is concerning.

**Signs of DVT/PE.** Calf swelling, calf pain, calf warmth (DVT). Chest pain, shortness of breath, racing pulse, unilateral leg edema (PE). DVT and PE are particularly concerning post-orthopedic, post-cardiac, post-bariatric, and post-cosmetic procedures.

**Significant changes in mental status.** Confusion in an older patient post-anesthesia. Persistent confusion is post-operative delirium, which may require evaluation and intervention.

**Significant active bleeding.** Bleeding that does not stop with pressure, soaking through dressings, or hemorrhage from a surgical site requires emergency evaluation.

**Severe medication reactions.** Hives, swelling, difficulty breathing — anaphylaxis or severe allergic reactions. Immediate medical attention.

**Sudden vision change, sudden unilateral weakness or speech difficulty.** Stroke symptoms. Immediate emergency response.

**Severe headache, particularly with nausea/vomiting.** Could be intracranial bleeding or post-spinal-anesthesia headache. Immediate evaluation.

### The escalation framework

The framework's escalation is procedure-agnostic:

**Level 1 — Self-management with destination-clinic awareness.** Minor symptoms that the destination clinic's discharge instructions specifically address. Pain managed with prescribed medications. Slight wound oozing within normal range. The companion observes; the destination clinic is notified at the next routine contact.

**Level 2 — Destination clinic contact.** Symptoms that warrant clinical evaluation but are not emergencies. Fever above the threshold; expanding redness; increasing pain. The destination clinic's international patient department is contacted; the clinic determines whether in-person evaluation is needed.

**Level 3 — Clinic visit or emergency room.** Symptoms that require timely evaluation. The destination clinic is contacted; if the clinic recommends emergency evaluation, the patient and companion go to the destination clinic's emergency department or the alternate higher-level hospital identified during pre-trip planning.

**Level 4 — Emergency services.** Acute, life-threatening symptoms. Chest pain, severe respiratory distress, uncontrolled bleeding, stroke symptoms, anaphylaxis. Call emergency services in the destination first; notify the destination clinic second.

### When to demand transfer to a higher-level facility

For procedures performed at ambulatory surgery centers or smaller hospitals, the patient may need transfer to a higher-level facility (tertiary hospital with ICU) if a complication exceeds the original facility's capability. The framework's discipline:

- The destination clinic should have a transfer protocol documented and accessible. Verify before the procedure (this is one of the questions in Chapter 9 for Step 5 procedures).
- The companion has the alternate hospital's name and contact accessible.
- The decision to transfer is generally a clinical decision made by the destination clinician, but the patient and companion have the right to request transfer if they believe the situation warrants it.
- The financial responsibility for transfer and higher-level care varies; verify in advance whether the destination clinic's package includes complication management and what the cost would be if transfer to an unaffiliated facility becomes necessary.

### The "do not fly home" decision tree

For some complications, the patient should not fly home until cleared by the destination clinician. The framework's discipline:

- DVT or PE: do not fly until cleared.
- Active infection requiring IV antibiotics: do not fly until oral antibiotic conversion is complete.
- Recent abdominal surgery with possible postoperative ileus: do not fly until bowel function is restored.
- Post-anesthesia respiratory compromise: do not fly until pulmonary function is normalized.
- Open wounds or unhealed surgical sites in specific procedures: per the surgeon's flight-clearance criteria.

The "do not fly home" decision is the destination clinician's. The framework's discipline is to defer to that judgment, even when it is inconvenient. The cost of a delayed flight is modest; the cost of a complication mid-flight or worsening due to flight conditions can be severe.

### In-flight medical emergency protocol

If a medical emergency develops mid-flight:

- Notify the flight attendant immediately.
- The flight crew can request a medical professional aboard (announcement to passengers) and can connect to ground-based medical consultation.
- For severe emergencies, the flight may divert to the nearest suitable airport.
- The patient's records (carried in carry-on per Chapter 32) become critical for any ground-based clinical evaluation.

The framework's discipline: do not minimize symptoms mid-flight. The flight attendant and ground-based medical consultation are resources designed for exactly this situation.

## Chapter 37 — Complaints, Chargebacks, Legal Recourse Across Borders

When a medical-tourism procedure goes badly — either through clinical failure, contractual breach, or unexpected outcome — the patient has options. The options are different from US-domestic care; cross-border complaints, chargebacks, and legal recourse have specific frameworks.

### Document first, decide second

The single most important post-procedure rule: document before deciding. Even when emotional response is strong, the documentation determines what options are available later.

What to document:
- The procedure as performed (operative report, records, photos).
- The procedure as agreed (original quote, consent forms, pre-operative communication).
- Any discrepancies (procedure changes not consented to, billing surprises, undisclosed adverse events).
- Communications with the destination clinic, in chronological order.
- The patient's clinical course post-return.

Documentation in writing, time-stamped, with copies in multiple locations. The framework's discipline.

### Result dissatisfaction vs. clinical error

Two very different categories with very different response frameworks:

**Result dissatisfaction.** The procedure was performed competently; the patient is dissatisfied with the outcome. Examples: cosmetic result that the patient finds unaesthetic; functional result that fell short of patient expectations (knee that does not flex as well as hoped); recovery that was harder than anticipated.

Result dissatisfaction is real but is generally not grounds for a malpractice claim or chargeback. The framework's response: document the dissatisfaction, communicate with the destination clinician about possible revision or adjustment, evaluate whether revision is warranted clinically.

**Clinical error.** The procedure was performed in a way that fell below the standard of care; the patient experienced harm as a result. Examples: surgical-site infection from sterile-technique failure; implant substitution without consent; wrong-side surgery; foreseeable complication not adequately managed.

Clinical error is grounds for formal complaint, possible chargeback, and possible legal recourse. The framework's response is more aggressive.

### The 11 escalation channels

The full set of channels available for medical-tourism complaints:

1. **Direct communication with the destination clinic.** First step. The clinic's international patient department often resolves the simplest issues here.
2. **Escalation to clinic senior management.** If the international patient department does not respond adequately, escalate to medical director or hospital administration.
3. **Implant or device manufacturer.** If the issue involves a specific device that may have failed, the manufacturer has formal reporting and recall mechanisms.
4. **Destination country's medical regulator.** Each country has a medical regulator that accepts complaints from patients, including international patients. The complaint is documented; investigation may follow.
5. **Destination country's accreditation body.** If the facility is accredited (JCI, NABH, HAI), the accreditation body has a formal complaint process that can affect the facility's accreditation status.
6. **Destination country's specialty society** (where applicable).
7. **US credit-card chargeback.** Under the Fair Credit Billing Act, US patients can dispute charges for "services not rendered" or "services materially different from what was agreed." This is the most accessible remedy for many situations.
8. **Travel insurance or complication insurance claim.** If the patient carries either, file according to policy terms.
9. **US-based legal counsel.** For potential malpractice or fraud claims. US attorneys with cross-border medical-tourism experience are a small but growing specialty.
10. **Destination-country legal counsel.** For claims that will be filed in destination jurisdiction.
11. **Patient advocacy organizations and public communications.** Online reviews, patient communities, journalism. Last resort but sometimes the most effective.

The escalation is sequential. Start with direct communication; escalate when direct communication fails.

### Cross-border malpractice realities

Cross-border malpractice claims are substantially more difficult than US-domestic claims:

- **Jurisdiction.** Where does the lawsuit get filed? Usually in the destination country, under the destination country's tort law, with destination-country procedural rules.
- **Counsel.** Cross-border malpractice attorneys are rare. Most US malpractice attorneys do not handle cross-border cases. Many destination countries have limited bar capacity for medical malpractice.
- **Statute of limitations.** Varies by jurisdiction. Some destinations have statutes of limitations as short as one year for medical malpractice claims.
- **Damages.** Damage awards in medical malpractice vary enormously across jurisdictions. US-style damage awards are rare in most destinations.
- **Enforcement.** Even with a successful judgment in destination jurisdiction, collecting on the judgment may be difficult.

The realistic framing: cross-border malpractice recovery is rare. The framework's discipline is to prevent the situation that requires it. When prevention fails, the framework's response is documentation, formal complaints, chargebacks, and patient-advocacy communications — not assuming a malpractice claim will provide recovery.

### The patient-facing complaint script

For formal complaints to the destination clinic or to regulators, the framework's recommendation is a structured complaint letter:

- Patient identification (name, date of birth, dates of procedure).
- Procedure as performed (factual description).
- Procedure as agreed (per pre-operative communication and consent).
- Specific discrepancy or issue.
- Documentation attached.
- Specific remedy requested (refund, revision, compensation, formal response).
- Timeline expectation for response.

The script keeps the complaint factual, specific, and resolvable. Emotional language reduces the complaint's effectiveness; factual specificity increases it.

### Public reviews and online communications

Online reviews — Google, Yelp, RealSelf for cosmetic, specific medical-tourism review sites — are public communications. They reach future patients and reach the clinic.

The framework's discipline: direct communication and formal complaints first, public communications second. A patient who has not given the destination clinic an opportunity to resolve has not exhausted the framework. A patient who has exhausted the framework without resolution has the right to communicate publicly.

For public communications: factual, specific, dated, with documentation references where possible. Avoid speculation, accusation, or emotional language. The factual review reaches future patients; the emotional review reaches an audience.

## Chapter 38 — When the Outcome Disappoints: The Year After

Some medical-tourism procedures will not deliver the outcome the patient hoped for. The framework's discipline does not eliminate this possibility; it improves the odds and prepares the patient for the response.

This chapter is about year 1 — what to do when the outcome disappoints. It is also about year 5, year 10, the longer view that buyer-protection requires.

### The reader's seat at year 1

Year 1 is the first major reflection point. The healing is complete or near-complete. The functional or aesthetic outcome is visible. The financial cost is processed. The patient has a year of perspective.

Some year-1 outcomes are clearly good. Marcus at month 8 (Chapter 31) is on his way to a clear year-1 success. Eleanor at month 14 (Chapter 35) is similarly on track.

Some year-1 outcomes are clearly disappointing. A joint replacement that has not delivered the function expected. A cosmetic procedure with a result the patient does not love. A bariatric procedure with weight regain that exceeded expectation. A cardiac procedure with persistent symptoms.

Most year-1 outcomes are in between. Mostly working, with elements that exceeded expectation and elements that fell short.

The framework's year-1 discipline: assess honestly. Document. Communicate with both the destination clinician (where the procedure happened) and the US-based follow-up clinician (where the continuity lives). Identify what aspects of the outcome are clearly disappointing, what aspects are within normal variation, and what aspects require additional intervention.

### The 5-year question

Year 5 is when buyer-protection extends beyond procedure week. The questions:

- Has the result held? The implant, the cosmetic outcome, the weight loss, the cardiac function.
- Has the patient maintained the lifestyle, surveillance, or maintenance the procedure required? Bariatric patients with multi-year nutritional follow-up. Joint replacement patients with periodic imaging. Breast implant patients with FDA-recommended surveillance.
- Has the patient required revision or additional intervention? At what cost? With what continuity?
- Would the patient make the same decision again?

The year-5 reflection is the test of whether the framework's discipline produced a durable decision. The patient who is satisfied with the procedure but who has lost the records, who has no continuity arrangement, who cannot track the implant or maintain the surveillance — that patient has been ill-served by their own decision, even when the procedure went well.

The framework asks more of the patient than the procedure does. The reward is durability.

### Reversibility and revisability

Some procedures are highly revisable. Cosmetic procedures often can be adjusted. Hair restoration can be supplemented. Dental work can be modified.

Some procedures are partially reversible. Joint replacement can be revised but the revision is more complex than the primary. Bariatric surgery can be revised but with diminishing returns.

Some procedures are essentially irreversible. Hysterectomy. Major cardiac surgery. Significant body contouring.

The framework's discipline at year 1: understand where the procedure sits on the reversibility spectrum. Plan accordingly. The procedure with high revision potential (cosmetic) and the procedure with low revision potential (major cardiac) require different year-1 disciplines.

### The "what would I do differently" reflection

A teaching device, not a critique. The patient at year 1, looking back at the framework, can identify what worked and what they would change.

Things that often emerge:
- The verification work was useful. Patients rarely regret verifying credentials and accreditation.
- The independent second opinion was useful. The structured opinion shaped the decision in ways the patient now appreciates.
- The records discipline was useful. The patient who has the records is grateful at year 1, year 5, year 10.
- The continuity arrangement was useful. The US-based follow-up provider is the relationship that matters at year 5.

Things that some patients regret:
- Time compression. The 60-day plan compressed to 30 days or less; the framework was applied but with less margin.
- The decision to forgo telemedicine second opinion. The $1,500–$2,500 for a formal US-academic-center opinion is modest in retrospect for many patients.
- Excessive focus on cost. Patients sometimes report that the cost-focused decision overrode considerations that mattered more in the long run.

These are not universal regrets. The framework, applied, delivers good outcomes for most patients. The "what would I do differently" reflection is the patient's own honesty about where the framework could have been applied more rigorously.

## Composite Scenario L — What It Looks Like When It Works

Maria is fifty-two. School principal. Married, two adult children, lives in San Diego. Five years ago, her BMI was 41. She had type 2 diabetes, sleep apnea, and the early signs of cardiovascular risk her US primary care physician had been monitoring for three years. The US bariatric surgery options were available to her — her insurance covered the procedure — but the program at the major academic medical center had a 14-month waiting list and required a 6-month medically supervised weight-loss program before scheduling. Maria had been on the equivalent of 14 different weight-management approaches in the previous decade. She did not believe a 15th would change the trajectory.

Her sister-in-law, who had a sleeve gastrectomy in Tijuana three years earlier and was doing well, suggested Maria consider Mexico. Maria's first response was the same as most readers' — skepticism, mixed with the recognition that she did not have a clearly better alternative.

Maria found this book. Volumes One and Two were not yet written; this is the foundation volume Maria needed. She worked through the 60-day plan. The first three weeks were spent on the framework itself — reading the Five Rules, doing the Procedure Complexity Self-Rating (Worksheet 17), completing the Is Medical Tourism Right for Me Self-Test (Worksheet 13). The Step 4 placement of bariatric surgery told her the verification work would be substantial.

Week 4 was the independent second opinion. Maria submitted her records to Cleveland Clinic Online Medical Second Opinion — Cleveland Clinic's bariatric and metabolic institute is one of the most established bariatric programs in the United States and covers second opinions across the specialty. The opinion confirmed bariatric surgery was appropriate; the specific recommendation was sleeve gastrectomy over gastric bypass given her diabetes profile and the lower complication rate for sleeve in her demographic. The Cleveland Clinic opinion also noted the importance of multi-year follow-up — the recommendation was that she identify a US-based bariatric surgeon willing to manage the long-term continuity before booking the destination procedure.

Maria spent week 5 finding the US-based bariatric follow-up surgeon. She called four practices. Three declined to manage a Mexico-performed procedure. The fourth — a bariatric surgeon in San Diego who had been working with the Mexico patient population for years — agreed, with a clear arrangement: the surgeon would provide the post-operative follow-up at his standard fee schedule, would manage any complications that fell within his scope, and would refer to specialists for anything outside his scope.

Weeks 6 and 7 were the destination clinic selection. Maria identified three Tijuana centers with ASMBS-COE-equivalent recognition and published outcomes. She had calls with the surgeons at each. She verified the cédula profesional registrations. She verified the JCI accreditation status of the affiliated facilities. She compared the True Cost Calculator outputs. She chose a center that was not the cheapest but had the most established outcomes record and the surgeon she trusted most after the consultation calls.

Week 8 was confirmation and pre-operative completion. The pre-operative psychological evaluation (in San Diego, with a psychologist who works with bariatric patients) confirmed Maria was ready. The pre-operative nutritional consultation prepared her for the post-operative dietary requirements. The smoking cessation question was moot — Maria had not smoked in twenty years. The pre-operative liver-shrinking diet (standard for two weeks before sleeve gastrectomy) was completed.

Maria flew to Tijuana with her husband as companion. The procedure was performed at the Tijuana center as planned. The operative report was signed; the anesthesia record was complete; the discharge summary specified post-operative nutrition, medication, and follow-up schedule. The stay was four days post-procedure. The flight home was at day five, with the bariatric surgeon's flight-clearance criteria met.

The first year post-procedure was the work. Maria followed the nutrition protocol her US-based surgeon prescribed. She attended every follow-up appointment in San Diego. She tracked her weight, her diabetes labs, her vitamin levels (deficiencies are common post-sleeve; supplementation is the standard).

At year 1: BMI 32. Diabetes in remission off all medications. Sleep apnea resolved. The cardiovascular risk markers normalizing.

At year 5 — where the book finds her now — Maria's BMI is 28. The diabetes remission has held. She has gained back a small amount of weight (about 8 pounds) over the past two years, a pattern her US-based surgeon describes as common and not requiring revision. The annual surveillance imaging is normal. The nutritional supplementation continues.

She is at her surgeon's office for the annual visit. She has the original Mexico operative report in her records folder, along with the four subsequent annual follow-up notes. She knows the procedure's specifications: laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, performed with the surgeon she chose, at the facility she verified, at the cost she budgeted. The all-in cost five years ago was $9,800 — about a third of the US cash price and well within her HSA balance at the time.

She is thinking about her brother, who is also overweight and has been talking about bariatric surgery for two years. She has sent him the framework. She has told him, the year 5 reflection: the procedure was the easy part. The framework was the hard part. The framework was also the reason the procedure worked.

She tells her surgeon, at the year 5 visit, that she did not realize at the time how much of the success depended on the work she did before the procedure. The surgeon nods. He says he sees this pattern across his medical-tourism patient population. The patients who applied a framework do well; the patients who did not often do not. The procedure is a procedure; the framework is the decision.

## Glossary

**Accreditation** — A process by which an outside organization evaluates a clinic, hospital, surgical center, or system against stated standards (JCI, AAAASF, ISO 9001, ISQua-recognized country bodies). Necessary, not sufficient.

**AJRR** — American Joint Replacement Registry. The US joint replacement outcome registry operated by AAOS.

**ASMBS** — American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery. The US bariatric surgery professional society.

**Bariatric surgery** — Surgical treatment of severe obesity (sleeve gastrectomy, gastric bypass, duodenal switch).

**BBL** — Brazilian Butt Lift. Gluteal fat transfer. The highest-mortality cosmetic procedure in published literature. Recommend-against pattern in non-accredited facilities with intramuscular fat injection.

**BIA-ALCL** — Breast Implant-Associated Anaplastic Large Cell Lymphoma. A rare lymphoma associated with textured breast implants. FDA recommends symptom-based surveillance.

**CABG** — Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting. Surgical treatment of multi-vessel coronary artery disease.

**Cardiac surgery** — Surgical treatment of cardiac conditions (CABG, valve replacement, valve repair, structural intervention).

**CDC MMWR** — Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report. Federal-government primary source for public health data.

**CPT** — Current Procedural Terminology. US-standard procedure coding.

**DICOM** — Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine. The standard medical imaging file format.

**DVT/PE** — Deep Vein Thrombosis / Pulmonary Embolism. Clotting complications particularly relevant post-orthopedic, post-cardiac, post-bariatric.

**ESHRE** — European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology. International reproductive medicine society.

**FUE** — Follicular Unit Extraction. Hair restoration technique using individual follicle extraction.

**Facilitator** — A coordinator, agency, or concierge service arranging medical tourism. Four business models: commission, retainer, hospital-employed, transparent fee.

**HSA** — Health Savings Account. US tax-advantaged medical savings account.

**ICSI** — Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection. IVF variant for male-factor infertility.

**IOL** — Intraocular Lens. Implanted during cataract surgery.

**ISO 9001** — General quality-management system standard. Insufficient alone as healthcare accreditation.

**ISQua** — International Society for Quality in Health Care. Accredits accreditation bodies.

**IVF** — In Vitro Fertilization. Reproductive medicine procedure.

**JCI** — Joint Commission International. Major international hospital accreditation body.

**LASIK** — Laser-Assisted in Situ Keratomileusis. Refractive surgery for myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism.

**MAUDE** — Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience. FDA medical device adverse event database.

**NABH** — National Accreditation Board for Hospitals and Healthcare Providers (India). ISQua-recognized.

**NSQIP** — National Surgical Quality Improvement Program. US surgical quality registry.

**PGT** — Preimplantation Genetic Testing. PGT-A (aneuploidy), PGT-M (specific mutation), PGT-SR (structural rearrangement).

**SART** — Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology. US IVF outcome registry.

**Sleeve gastrectomy** — Most common bariatric procedure. Removal of lateral two-thirds of stomach.

**STS** — Society of Thoracic Surgeons. US cardiac surgery professional society and outcome registry.

**TAVR** — Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement. Structural cardiac intervention.

**TKA** — Total Knee Arthroplasty.

**THA** — Total Hip Arthroplasty.

**UCR** — Usual, Customary, and Reasonable. US insurance reimbursement rate schedule.

**WHCRA** — Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act (1998). US federal law requiring insurance coverage of reconstruction after mastectomy.

## Appendix Disclaimer

This book is general educational material. It is not medical advice, surgical advice, anesthesia advice, mental-health advice, legal advice, insurance advice, financial advice, travel-safety advice, or a substitute for professional evaluation. Reading this book does not create a doctor-patient, surgeon-patient, anesthesiologist-patient, mental-health-clinician-patient, attorney-client, insurance-adviser, or fiduciary relationship with Tabiji, the editor, reviewers, publisher, or any contributor.

The book covers medical tourism across many procedure categories — dental, cosmetic surgery, hair restoration, orthopedic surgery, bariatric surgery, cardiac surgery, ophthalmology, reproductive medicine, oncology, diagnostic and preventive care. Each category has its own risk profile that no general guide can adequately summarize. The full risk picture of any procedure you are considering must come from a qualified clinician who has examined you in person.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has documented patterns of harm in international medical care: 93 US-citizen deaths in the Dominican Republic 2009–2022 with 92 percent involving gluteal fat transfer (CDC MMWR 73(3)); *Mycobacterium abscessus* surgical-site infection outbreaks in destination cosmetic surgery facilities; cancer-treatment fraud patterns in unapproved-alternative-therapy clinics. The Food and Drug Administration has issued repeated warnings about unapproved stem-cell products. The State Department's annual Trafficking in Persons reports document organ-trafficking corridors. The National Cancer Institute has documented harm patterns from unapproved alternative cancer therapies. This book takes editorial positions on patterns where the documented evidence is sufficient that "ask better questions" framing is, in our judgment, insufficient. Those positions appear in Chapter 30.

Medical tourism can involve pain, infection, bleeding, hematoma, seroma, nerve injury, scarring, sensation changes, allergic reactions, anesthesia complications including aspiration and malignant hyperthermia, fat embolism (particularly with BBL), venous thromboembolism and pulmonary embolism, capsular contracture (with breast implants), implant rupture or migration, asymmetric or unsatisfactory outcomes, need for revision, prolonged recovery, post-operative depression and other mental-health responses, surgical-site infection, sepsis, cardiac arrhythmia, cardiac arrest, stroke, and death. Medical tourism can add language barriers, distance from the treating clinic, different legal systems, documentation challenges, insurance exclusions, accreditation gaps, and difficulty obtaining follow-up care.

Cross-border malpractice recovery is materially more difficult than domestic recovery. Some procedures require continuity-of-care arrangements that medical tourism cannot deliver. The book redirects clinical questions to qualified professionals throughout because those redirections are the substance of the framework.

The worksheets and scripts in this book are planning aids. They are meant to help readers ask questions, organize documents, verify credentials, compare quotes, prepare a companion, and preserve records. They do not tell readers which procedure to choose, which clinic to use, which facility to trust, whether to travel, whether to fly after surgery, whether symptoms are urgent, or how to treat any condition.

Concerning symptoms after any procedure — chest pain, shortness of breath, calf swelling or pain, fever at or above 101°F / 38.3°C, expanding redness, severe pain disproportionate to expected recovery, significant active bleeding, signs of severe infection, severe medication reactions, significant changes in mental status, breathing difficulty, sudden vision change, sudden unilateral weakness or speech difficulty, severe headache, or any other urgent concern — warrant immediate evaluation by qualified local emergency professionals. The local emergency number varies by country; 911 is the US number and does not work in many destinations.

The editorial positions in Chapter 30 reflect Tabiji's reading of the published evidence as of this edition. They are not claims that any specific clinic, surgeon, country, or procedure is unsafe in every case; they identify patterns where the documented harm rate is, in our judgment, sufficient to recommend against the pattern. Other claims will stay where the evidence supports them.
