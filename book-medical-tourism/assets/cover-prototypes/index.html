The Medical Tourism Field Guide — cover prototypes

Tabiji Field Guides — Volume Three. Series accent for V3: muted slate blue (#3A5566) and quiet teal (#1F6470) per PLAN §17. V1 used deep forest green, V2 used warm brick red on cream; V3 takes a passport-tone palette so the three volumes harmonize on the shelf.
Direction 1 · Series-Consistent · Recommended

Saul Steinberg / New Yorker editorial line

Spare confident black-ink line on cream. Solitary figure at a small table with an open world atlas, a stack of printed quotes, a coffee cup with steam, and a desk lamp casting a slate-blue glow. Visually rhymes with V2 (which chose this direction) — same series visual identity, V3 accent.

Pros: Sophisticated, restrained, "literary nonfiction" tone. Series continuity with V2. Slate-blue accent reads as institutional / passport-tone. Generous upper-third space for clean title overlay. Loose figure with no identifiable face.

Cons: Risk of looking like V2's twin rather than a distinct volume. Title typography will need to carry the differentiation work.

Verdict: Recommended for series identity. If you want a buyer to recognize "this is the Tabiji Field Guide series" from across a room, this is the one.
Direction 2 · Adventure-Comic Alternate

Hergé / Tintin ligne-claire

Clean uniform black-ink outlines, flat saturated color, mid-century European adventure-comic feel. A traveler at an airport gate reviewing a checklist, slate-blue tail-fin on a stylized airliner visible through the window, wall clock reading 5:40.

Pros: Strong instant read of "considered international travel." More commercial / accessible than Steinberg. The traveler reads as prepared rather than rushed — matches the framework-discipline editorial position.

Cons: Risk of glamorizing medical tourism — the adventure-comic style can read as commercial / promotional, which is exactly the framing the book opposes. Also the rendered composition has an awkward "comic panel inside a frame" issue that would need post-production work. Traveler's face is more identifiable than the brief asked for.

Verdict: Strong style but tonal mismatch with a buyer-protection book.
Direction 3 · Conceptual · Designer-Forward

Saul Bass mid-century conceptual

Bold flat areas of slate-blue and teal with razor-sharp graphic edges, mid-century silkscreen texture, a single sand-gold meridian line tracing a great-circle arc across a geometrically reduced globe held in a stylized open palm. Cinematic, conceptual, institutional.

Pros: The most graphic-design-forward of the four — feels like a hardcover from a serious publisher. Slate-blue and teal palette is fully integrated rather than added as accent. Conceptual symbolism (the considered hand carrying the global decision) is on-message. Strong shelf presence at thumbnail size.

Cons: Breaks series visual identity with V2 — Bass conceptual is a different visual language than Steinberg editorial-line. Renders as a "poster on a wall" with a slate-blue surrounding mat that would need cropping out for the actual cover.

Verdict: The most professional-looking and institutional cover; consider this if you're willing to evolve V3 away from V2's visual language.
Direction 4 · Narrative · Kitchen-Table

Norman Rockwell editorial illustration

Warm naturalistic painting in the Saturday Evening Post tradition. A person at a wooden kitchen table at evening, leaning on one elbow, with an open atlas, a printed treatment quote, a notebook, a phone face-down, glasses, and a slate-blue pendant lamp shade. Honey-amber palette with slate-blue accents on the lamp, mug, and book spine.

Pros: Maps directly to Marcus's Chapter 1 opening scene ("kitchen table at eleven o'clock at night"). Most narrative of the four — readers will feel the moment of considered deliberation. Highest emotional pull.

Cons: Face is identifiable (middle-aged man clearly rendered) — would need a regen with face turned further away. Also: "TREATMENT QUOTE" text appears on the paper, which the no-text rule forbids — would need editing or regen. Warmer palette than V3's brief; the slate-blue is present but secondary. Breaks series visual language with V2's spare line work.

Verdict: Most narrative-evocative of the four; requires a regen or post-production cleanup to remove the text and turn the face further away.
