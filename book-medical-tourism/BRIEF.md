**Editor:** Bernard Huang
**Series:** Tabiji Field Guides (Volume 3)
**Predecessors:** Volume 1 — *The Dental Tourism Field Guide* (2026). Volume 2 — *The Cosmetic Surgery Field Guide* (2026).

## 1. Why this brief exists, and the strategic problem

Volumes 1 and 2 were procedure-specific buyer-protection books: dental tourism, then cosmetic surgery and hair restoration. Both targeted readers who had already chosen a procedure category and needed a careful field guide to navigate the decision.

Volume 3 — "medical tourism itself" — is a different commercial and editorial problem. The reader is *earlier in the funnel.* They have not yet picked a procedure. They have heard from a friend, read an article, scrolled an Instagram ad, received a quote they cannot afford at home, or have a chronic condition with multiple potential interventions. They are asking the broader, earlier question: *should I even consider care abroad, and if so, how do I think about it?*

Three competing positions exist for a book at this scope. Each has commercial and editorial trade-offs that materially affect what gets written, who reviews it, what audience it reaches, and whether it succeeds.

### Position A — The Survey Book

Cover every procedure category in moderate depth: dental, cosmetic, orthopedic, bariatric, cardiac, oncology, ophthalmology, fertility, and so on. Each gets a chapter.

- **Strengths:** comprehensive on the surface; serves a single reader who wants one book.
- **Weaknesses:** thin per-procedure treatment; readers of V1 or V2 feel cannibalized when they buy V3 and find a 6,000-word dental chapter they already paid for elsewhere; industry data dates fast; the legal-disclaimer surface area is enormous (you are now claiming to discuss surgical risks across ten specialties); reviewer panel balloons to needing a sub-specialist per chapter.

### Position B — The Foundation Book

Cover the *system* (insurance, accreditation, pricing, the facilitator industry) and the *decision framework* (the Five Rules, the Seven Leverage Points, the procedure-complexity ladder) at depth. Include shorter procedure surveys (1,500–2,500 words each) that point readers to specialist volumes for deep dives. Take an editorial position on a small number of patterns the evidence demands.

- **Strengths:** largest addressable audience (anyone exploring medical tourism, not just one procedure); front-door of the funnel that drives sales to V1, V2, and future volumes; teaches a framework that does not date; matches the established Tabiji voice; the strongest SEO position (high-volume keyword "medical tourism guide"); legally defensible because procedure-specific clinical claims live in the specialist volumes.
- **Weaknesses:** requires disciplined editing to feel like a book and not a long preface; readers who *already* have a procedure picked may prefer V1 or V2 directly.

### Position C — The Industry Exposé

Investigative reporting about the global medical-tourism industry — money flows, facilitator incentive structures, accreditation politics, regulator gaps, documented harm patterns.

- **Strengths:** distinct genre, journalistic credibility, no direct competitor.
- **Weaknesses:** drifts away from the buyer-protection voice that defines Tabiji; narrower audience (industry readers rather than patients); requires investigative reporting capacity Tabiji does not currently have; longer production timeline.

### The recommendation: Position B, with selective elements of A and C

Volume 3 should be **the foundation book** — the book a careful adult reads *before* picking a procedure. It teaches a decision architecture that applies to any procedure, surveys the major procedure categories at the appropriate depth (a survey is not a deep dive — it is a map and a set of pointers), and takes a sharp editorial position on the small number of patterns where the evidence demands one.

This is the strongest commercial play because the addressable audience is materially larger than V1 or V2 alone, the SEO position is strongest at the general-keyword level, and the book functions as a feeder for the specialist volumes rather than competing with them. It is also the strongest editorial play because the framework it teaches is the framework V1 and V2 already use, just at a higher level of abstraction.

Position A is the wrong play because it produces a thinner book that competes with the specialist volumes the series has already invested in. Position C is the right book for a different publisher.

A reasonable secondary consideration: this book should arguably have been Volume 1. It is the logical foundation. Because V1 and V2 already shipped, V3 must be positioned in marketing and back-matter copy as *the foundation volume you can also read first* — not as "the third one." Series numbering stays chronological for KDP metadata (V3 ships third) but copy emphasizes the foundation framing.

## 2. Title and subtitle

**Title:** *The Medical Tourism Field Guide*

**Subtitle:** *A Buyer-Protection Guide to Healthcare Decisions Abroad*

Reasoning:

- "Medical Tourism Field Guide" is series-consistent (matches *Dental Tourism Field Guide* and *Cosmetic Surgery Field Guide*) and matches the high-volume search term "medical tourism guide."
- "A Buyer-Protection Guide" maintains the brand framing established in V1 and V2.
- "Healthcare Decisions Abroad" — the word *Decisions* is deliberate. It signals this is a decision-framework book, not a procedure-by-procedure encyclopedia. *Abroad* is the international cue without the country-specific framing of either V1 or V2.

Alternates considered and rejected:

- *Care Across Borders* — too literary, low search volume.
- *Before You Travel for Care* — emotional hook but not series-consistent.
- *The Medical Tourism Field Guide: How to Think About Healthcare Abroad* — wordy.
- *The Foundation Field Guide* — series clever but unsearchable.

## 3. Audience

### Primary audience

1. **Adults considering medical care abroad who have not yet picked a procedure.** The single largest segment. They have a vague problem (cost, access, denial, chronic condition) and need to know whether medical tourism is a reasonable answer for them.
2. **Adults with a chronic condition and multiple potential interventions** — e.g., a 60-year-old with a deferred knee replacement, a dental rehab need, and a hernia repair on the horizon. They are evaluating medical tourism as a portfolio decision, not a single procedure.
3. **Adult children researching options for a parent** — particularly for procedures the parent is anxious about or has been quoted at home for amounts the family cannot absorb.
4. **U.S. patients hit by an insurance denial, deductible, or coverage cliff** — the cost-crisis reader. Often researches under time pressure.
5. **Diaspora / first- and second-generation immigrants** considering care in their country of origin (where family is, where language is native, where prices are lower). The book speaks to this reader directly and respectfully — diaspora medical tourism is a different decision from "save money abroad."

### Secondary audience

6. Readers who already finished V1 or V2 and want the broader context.
7. Healthcare workers, patient advocates, consumer-protection researchers, journalists, policy analysts.
8. Family members and companions of patients in any of the primary segments.

### Who the book is NOT for

- People looking for clinic rankings. The book does not rank clinics, surgeons, or destinations, and will say so explicitly.
- People looking for a destination travel guide that happens to include medical procedures. The book does not glamorize destinations.
- People looking for clinical advice. The book repeatedly redirects clinical questions to qualified professionals.

## 4. Scope — what's in, what's out

### In scope

**Systems content (Part I):** the medical-tourism economy, why prices differ across borders, accreditation regimes (JCI, AAAASF, country bodies), the U.S. insurance landscape, the facilitator industry, the regulator landscape, the cross-border malpractice reality.

**Decision-framework content (Parts II–V):** the Five Rules adapted for general application; the Seven Leverage Points; the procedure-complexity ladder; researching a procedure independently; reading clinical literature; evaluating destinations; quotes, contracts, deposits, financing.

**Procedure surveys (Part VI):** ten survey chapters at 1,500–2,500 words each. Each survey covers what is distinctive about that procedure category for medical tourism, the credentialing and accreditation specifics, the typical destinations and cost differentials, the cross-border quality-of-evidence reality, and where to go for deeper specialist treatment (cross-references to V1, V2, and to authoritative external sources).

**Recovery and complications (Parts VII–VIII):** the records packet, the companion's role, follow-up at home, complaints, chargebacks, cross-border malpractice — all at a level that applies across procedures.

**Editorial position chapter (Part IX):** patterns the book recommends against — transplant tourism, stem-cell and "regenerative" tourism, unapproved alternative cancer therapies, surgery in unaccredited facilities, any procedure with unverifiable provider credentials.

### Out of scope

- **Procedure-specific deep dives** that V1 and V2 already deliver. Dental and cosmetic surgery get *surveys* in V3, not chapters. The surveys point to the specialist volumes.
- **End-of-life and assisted-dying tourism** (Switzerland Dignitas, etc.). Outside the buyer-protection frame; deserves its own treatment.
- **Pediatric medical tourism.** Different decision architecture (parental consent, pediatric specialists, school disruption). May be a future volume.
- **Wellness tourism** unless framed as a warning category (longevity clinics, NAD+ infusions, peptide tourism, stem-cell "rejuvenation") — included under "patterns we recommend against."
- **Veterinary tourism, cosmetic dentistry-only treatments, elective cosmetic dentistry** — already covered in V1.

### Scope creep risk and the cut-line

The single biggest risk for V3 is scope creep. The temptation to add "just one more procedure survey" is real. The cut-line:

- **Ten procedure surveys total. No more.**
- **Each survey 1,500–2,500 words. Hard cap at 3,000.**
- **No survey replaces a specialist-volume deep dive. If a reader needs more, the survey ends with cross-references to V1, V2, or external authoritative sources.**

## 5. Editorial voice and posture

Identical to V1 and V2:

- **Calm, considered, buyer-protection.** Not alarmist, not glamorous, not commercial.
- **Buyer's-seat editorial voice.** Bernard Huang is a consumer journalist, not a clinician. The book reports patterns, asks questions, provides scripts. It does not prescribe.
- **"We" (editorial plural) + "you" (reader).** First-person "I" only in About-the-Editor and in scripted patient sentences.
- **No clinic, surgeon, facilitator, manufacturer, insurer, or destination endorsements.** The book recommends against specific *patterns*; it does not recommend *for* specific entities.
- **Sourced clinical claims.** Every clinical claim traces to a citable source (peer-reviewed literature, CDC, WHO, FDA, accrediting body publications, national regulator guidance).
- **No commercial relationships with industry actors.** The book does not accept referral fees, sponsorship, or paid placement.

## 6. Editorial position — patterns the book recommends against

V2 established the editorial precedent of taking explicit positions on patterns where the evidence is strong enough that "ask better questions" framing is insufficient. V3 extends this with broader scope.

The candidate list of patterns the book recommends against in low-regulation settings, subject to plastic surgeon, oncologist, transplant surgeon, and malpractice attorney reviewer sign-off:

1. **Transplant tourism in the documented organ-trafficking corridors.** The published evidence (CMA Journal, Transplantation, The Lancet, U.S. State Department reports) documents trafficking of organs from political prisoners (China), executed prisoners (China, Iran), and economically coerced donors (Pakistan, Egypt, Philippines, Bangladesh). The book takes the strongest possible position against participation. Patients on transplant lists should know that traveling to these destinations participates in a market the U.S. and most professional societies have explicitly condemned.

2. **Stem-cell, regenerative, exosome, peptide, and longevity tourism.** Repeated FDA warnings about unapproved stem-cell products marketed for cosmetic and chronic-disease indications. The book treats the entire category as a warning, not a buyer's guide.

3. **Unapproved alternative cancer therapies abroad** — laetrile, high-dose vitamin C, oxygen therapy, German biological cancer clinics, Mexican alternative cancer clinics, and similar. The documented harm pattern is patients dying of treatable cancers after spending savings on unapproved therapies. The book is unambiguous.

4. **Surgery in non-accredited "boutique" or "luxury" facilities regardless of country**, particularly when accredition is implied via vague language ("internationally trained team," "European-standard facility") without verifiable accreditation by a recognized body.

5. **Any procedure where the surgeon's board certification, registration number, and country of practice cannot be verified in a public registry before deposit.**

6. **Any procedure where the anesthesia provider's credentials cannot be verified in the destination country's official register.**

7. **BBL with intramuscular fat injection in non-accredited facilities** (carried over from V2 because the evidence base demands consistency; V2's full treatment is cross-referenced).

8. **Surgery during the first 12 months after any major adverse life event** — death of a partner or child, divorce, job loss, major diagnosis. The published literature on cosmetic surgery (and increasingly on bariatric surgery) shows higher rates of post-operative distress and regret in this window. The book frames this as "the timing question," not a categorical prohibition.

**Open decision: confirm editorial willingness to take positions 1 (transplant), 2 (stem cell), and 3 (alternative cancer) at the strongest level.** These are the three positions most likely to attract pushback from industry actors. The published evidence supports each. The risk is reputational rather than legal.

## 7. Series fit, branding, and back-matter framing

V3 fits the series in voice, structure, and editorial position but has one structural distinction worth naming.

V1 and V2 used a 7-part structure built around chronological journey stages (before quote → quote → due diligence → deposit → before OR → recovery → procedure-specific cautions). V3 follows a different logic because the reader is one step further back in the journey:

- **Part I — Why medical tourism exists (system context)**
- **Part II — The decision architecture (the framework)**
- **Part III — Researching your procedure (general)**
- **Part IV — Evaluating destinations (general)**
- **Part V — Quotes, contracts, money (general)**
- **Part VI — Procedure surveys (the ten survey chapters)**
- **Part VII — Recovery and coming home (general)**
- **Part VIII — When things go wrong (general)**
- **Part IX — Patterns we recommend against (editorial position)**
- **Closing — Composite Scenario + Final Note**

9 parts vs. V1/V2's 7. The expansion is the systems part at the beginning and the procedure-surveys part in the middle.

### Series back-matter framing

The About Tabiji section in V3 should add a paragraph telling readers:

> *This is Volume 3 of the Tabiji Field Guides series. You can read it first, even if you already have a specific procedure in mind. The decision framework here applies to dental, cosmetic, orthopedic, bariatric, cardiac, ophthalmic, reproductive, and oncology care abroad. For procedure-specific depth on dental tourism, see Volume 1; for cosmetic surgery and hair restoration, see Volume 2.*

This positions V3 as the foundation regardless of release order.

## 14. Risks I am tracking

1. **Scope creep.** The single biggest risk. Could easily balloon to 150K+ words trying to be everything. The cut-line in §4 must hold.

2. **Procedure-survey accuracy.** Each survey needs specialty review. If we cannot secure a reviewer for a specialty within the budget or timeline, the survey is pulled (not shipped under-reviewed).

3. **Going stale.** Industry data changes (accreditation directories update; insurance products change; popular destinations shift). The book locks its data points to "as of [publication year]" and commits to edition updates rather than continuous revision.

4. **Cross-volume cannibalization.** Readers may feel that buying V3 after V1 or V2 is redundant. Mitigation: V3's dental and cosmetic surveys are short (1,500 words) and explicitly point to the specialist volumes. V3's framing is foundation-first, not survey-first.

5. **Legal exposure for Part IX (recommend-against).** Strongest legal risk. Mitigation: malpractice attorney reviews Part IX specifically; every recommendation is sourced; the chapter is framed as "patterns the evidence supports recommending against," not "specific clinics or destinations are unsafe."

6. **Transplant tourism framing.** The strongest editorial position in the book. Mitigation: extensive sourcing (CMA Journal, Transplantation, U.S. State Department, professional society position statements); careful language that distinguishes documented organ-trafficking corridors from legitimate cross-border transplant programs; legal review before ship.

7. **The diaspora reader.** The book speaks to readers considering care in their country of origin. The framing must be respectful — diaspora medical tourism is a different decision from "save money abroad" and should not be flattened into the cost-savings frame.

8. **The cost-crisis reader.** The book speaks to readers under financial duress. The framing must avoid both moralizing ("you should have planned better") and false reassurance ("medical tourism will solve your problem"). Honest, considered, non-judgmental.

9. **Best-seller pressure.** The user has flagged that their job is on the line. The path to a best-seller is *not* trying to write a viral book; it is writing a *durable* book that ranks for high-volume keywords and converts readers into V1 and V2 buyers. The metric to target is sustainable monthly sales over 12–24 months, not a launch spike.

## 17. Bottom line

Volume 3 is the most strategically important book in the Tabiji Field Guides series. It is the front door of the funnel — the volume that converts general medical-tourism curiosity into committed readers of V1 and V2 — and it has the largest addressable audience of the three.

It is not a survey book that tries to replace V1 and V2, and it is not an industry exposé that drifts from the Tabiji voice.

The path to a best-selling book is not virality. It is durability — ranking for high-volume keywords ("medical tourism guide"), converting readers into long-term Tabiji audience, and serving as the cross-sell to the specialist volumes for years. That is how this series compounds.

The risks are real and managed: scope creep (cut-line in §4), procedure-survey accuracy (specialty reviewers), legal exposure for the recommend-against chapter (attorney review), and cross-volume cannibalization (foundation-book framing rather than survey framing).
