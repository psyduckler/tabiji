# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The sixty-five scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: A stranger who approaches you first is almost certainly running a script

This is the single most important habit you can take to Portugal. The instant a smiling local steps into your path uninvited at an airport arrivals hall, a famous palace entrance, a tram stop, a historic plaza, a bar strip, or the cliff top of a famous viewpoint, the interaction that follows is a rehearsed script. You are not being greeted. You are being sorted.

Portugal's scam ecosystem is less confrontational than Brazil's or Italy's, but its tells are the same. At Faro Airport (FAO), a man holding a generic "TRANSFERS" sign outside the arrivals doors quotes €65–€85 cash for a ride that Bolt charges €45–€55 with a digital receipt. On Lisbon's Rossio and Restauradores squares after 10 PM, a young man steps into a tourist's path and murmurs "hashish? cocaine? good price" — the product is always fake (soap, oregano, baking soda) and a second "dealer" is waiting nearby to appear as "plainclothes police." In Albufeira's Oura Strip, a young woman with a clipboard offers a "free scratchcard prize" that leads to a bar-ushering scheme with €200–€800 bar bills. At Lagos's Praça Gil Eanes, a well-dressed man claiming to represent an orphans' charity offers a gift-wrapped perfume bottle — the bottle is empty, the "donation" slip is the ask, and a pickpocket accomplice is already behind you. On Sítio da Nazaré's cliff top, a non-local "photographer" offers to rent you a seven-skirt costume for €10–€25 and then demands €30–€60 at the end for "multiple photos."

None of these interactions start with you asking for help. That is the tell. Portuguese people are warm and helpful, but they do not generally approach foreign tourists at airports, palace gates, or famous viewpoints to volunteer "transfers," "free prizes," or "special drinks."

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: when any stranger starts a conversation with you at an airport, a pier, or the entrance to a famous site, reply "*obrigado, não*" (thank you, no — pronounced *oh-bree-gah-doo now*, feminine *obrigada*) and keep walking. Do not break stride. Do not engage. If the person follows you, repeat the phrase once, louder. Portuguese politeness conventions do not require you to listen to a sales pitch, and walking away is not rude.

*You will see this pattern in: the FAO Airport sign-holder transfer scam (Faro), the Tram 28 approach-pickpocket lead-in (Lisbon), the Rossio drug-tout (Lisbon), the Oura Strip scratchcard scheme (Albufeira), the Lagos orphans' perfume hybrid, the Sítio seven-skirts photo-tout (Nazaré), the Praça do Comércio tuk-tuk tout (Lisbon), and almost every tout scene in the 65 scam entries that follow.*

## Pattern 2: "The meter is broken" or "fixed price" means you are being overcharged

The second pattern is taxis and transfers. Portugal has **Bolt** and **Uber** in every major city and at both international airports (LIS and FAO), and they are metered, transparent, and cheap — you see the price before you book, and you pay through the app. But a meaningful share of tourist rides begin with a licensed taxi driver refusing the meter and quoting a flat price — always 2× to 5× what the app would charge.

From Lisbon Airport (LIS) to the centre (8 km, 15 min), the real Bolt or Uber fare is €12–€16; the "meter-off fixed price" at the rank is €40–€80. From Faro Airport (FAO) to Albufeira (40 km), the Bolt/Uber fare is €45–€55; the taxi-rank fixed quote runs €80–€120. From FAO to Lagos (90 km), Bolt/Uber is €100–€120; taxi-rank quotes reach €160–€220. From Funchal Airport (FNC) to Funchal centre, the legitimate metered fare is €25–€30; the fixed-price quote runs €35–€50. From Praia da Nazaré to Sítio (1.5 km post the elevator closure), the legitimate metered fare is €4–€6; the taxi-rank quote runs €8–€15.

The "the meter is broken" line has a Portugal-specific companion: "*your card didn't process — pay cash*" at the end of a Bolt or Uber ride. The app has already charged the fare. The driver is asking for a second, off-app payment. Refuse politely and file an in-app complaint.

The defense is short and universal. Open Bolt or Uber and book the ride on the app; the price is shown up front, paid digitally, and there is no meter-is-broken conversation. If you must take a street taxi, insist on the meter (*taxímetro*) and photograph the license plate from the rear windscreen before departure. Never hand a credit card to any taxi driver. If a driver demands a cash top-up for a "special surcharge" that doesn't exist in the app, refuse politely, exit the vehicle at the next public place, and report via the in-app safety button. For late-night arrivals at any airport, pre-book a transfer via Welcome Pickups, Blacklane, or your hotel rather than improvising with whoever is in the arrivals hall.

*You will see this pattern in: the LIS / FAO / FNC airport taxi overcharge, the Coimbra-B station rank inflation, the Uber "extra cash" demand at drop-off, the Nazaré post-elevator-closure shuttle-van and tuk-tuk overcharge, and the Cascais-Lisbon train-to-taxi transfer upsell.*

## Pattern 3: Prices that aren't posted will surprise you later — and "couvert" is the Portuguese specialty

The third pattern is the unposted price, and in Portugal it has three specific forms you need to know by name.

The first form is the classic tourist-trap unposted price. Restaurants on Lisbon's Rua Augusta, Porto's Cais da Ribeira, Faro's Cidade Velha, Lagos's Rua 25 de Abril, Cascais's Baía, Sintra's Volta do Duche, and Funchal's Mercado dos Lavradores upper floor will sometimes — not always — quote you a price that is deliberately vague. "*Fresh fish* — market price." "Today's catch." "A small tasting." Each of these phrases hides a later surprise. On Nazaré's Avenida da República, a "fresh sea bass" on the display tray billed at €65–€90 per kilo turns into €120–€150 per person after the waiter reads the fish as "0.8–1.2 kg each"; the same fish at a residential-price venue a block inland is €35–€55 per kilo. At Mercado dos Lavradores, upper-floor fruit vendors press "free tasting — try everything!" on tourists and then assemble a bag billed at €17–€72 for produce that costs €3–€8 at any Pingo Doce. On Praça Gil Eanes (Lagos), a "traditional cataplana" quoted at "per-person market price" returns €45–€65 per head for a stew that runs €18–€25 at honest venues two streets away.

The second form is the **couvert** — the Portuguese name for the bread, olives, butter, cheese, pâté, and sometimes tapas-style plates that arrive at your table unordered within two minutes of sitting down. Each item carries a per-person charge of €2–€6 that does not appear on any posted menu. The total "couvert surprise" for a couple is typically €15–€35 before ordering a single dish. In Porto's Ribeira, Lisbon's Alfama, Sintra's Volta do Duche, and every Algarve old town, the couvert is the overcharge vehicle.

The third form you need to know by name is the **"não pedi, não pago" rule** — Portuguese consumer law. Under Decreto-Lei 24/96 and consistently upheld in Portuguese consumer courts, any item that arrives unordered is not payable. You have the legal right to refuse the couvert, hand it back, and say "*obrigado, não*." The manager knows this. A citation to the rule ("*não pedi, não pago*") ends the conversation every time.

The defense across all three is a single habit, repeated before every transaction: ask for the price in writing, or point to a posted menu, *before* anything happens. For fish or shellfish by weight, ask to see the scale and get the total in euros confirmed before it is cooked. For couvert, the moment bread, olives, or a tapas plate arrives, say "*obrigado, não*" and hand it back — do not eat anything first. Any "tourist tax" on a restaurant bill is illegal: Portugal's tourist tax is €2–€4 per person per night (varies by municipality), maximum 7 nights, and applies only to overnight accommodation — never to restaurants.

*You will see this pattern in: every Portuguese restaurant chapter in this book — Lisbon Alfama + Baixa + Bairro Alto, Porto Ribeira + Baixa, Sintra Volta do Duche, Funchal Rua de Santa Maria + Mercado dos Lavradores, Faro Cidade Velha, Albufeira Oura Strip British-pub row, Lagos Rua 25 de Abril, Cascais Marina, Coimbra Baixa + Rua Ferreira Borges, and Nazaré Avenida da República.*

## Pattern 4: The damage claim, the skimmed card, the "market price" weigh

The fourth pattern is the claim after the fact. You rent something, you buy something by weight, or you use an ATM — and the operator later points at an imaginary or pre-existing scratch, an inflated scale reading, or a cloned-terminal charge, and demands or has already taken money.

The rental-damage variant at Faro Airport runs like this: Klass Wagen, Goldcar, Centauro, OK Mobility, or a smaller Algarve operator at the rental-car pickup area hands you a car with duct-taped wheel arches, points at a pre-existing scratch you never noticed, and demands €800–€2,500. The fuel gauge reads "full" at pickup and "three-quarters" on paperwork; the return inspector demands €120 for "fuel." The defense is the same one that works everywhere in the world: take a video walk-around narrating every visible mark *before* you accept the car, photograph all four sides plus the roof, wheels, undercarriage, fuel gauge, and odometer, hand over a photocopy of your passport rather than the original, and if a post-return damage claim arrives two to four weeks later, dispute with your credit card within 48 hours using your photo evidence.

The **skimmed-ATM** variant is specifically Portuguese and deserves a name. Standalone machines labeled **Euronet** or **Travelex** in Portuguese tourist areas are NOT bank ATMs — they are independent payment processors that charge 8–15% "Dynamic Currency Conversion" (DCC) fees on top of your home bank's ATM fee, AND are the primary sites for skimming-device installation. Two weeks later, a fraudulent charge appears from another city. The defense is specific: use only **Multibanco** ATMs inside actual bank branches (Caixa Geral de Depósitos, Millennium BCP, Santander, Novo Banco, BPI) during business hours; bank branches have CCTV, security, and teller assistance. If you must use a standalone machine, always choose "NO CONVERSION" or "EUR" on the currency screen, shield the keypad with your hand, and check the card slot for loose fixtures.

The **unofficial-parking-attendant** variant is found at Cascais's Praia do Guincho, Nazaré's Praia do Norte / Farol lighthouse, and several Algarve beach car parks. Men in high-visibility vests direct you to "reserved" lots that are actually public street parking and demand €5–€15 cash. The defense: legitimate Portuguese municipal parking is either free on-street or metered via EMEL (Lisbon) or Cascais Parques machines that accept card or coin — never cash handed to a person in a vest.

*You will see this pattern in: the Faro Airport Klass Wagen + Goldcar + Centauro damage claims, the Lagos regional rental-operator overcharge, the Funchal Madeira Rent-a-Car / RentCarMadeira deposit-retention, the Euronet / Travelex DCC + skimming pattern at every major tourist zone, the Praia do Guincho parking-attendant scam (Cascais), the Praia do Norte big-wave parking scam (Nazaré), and the Algarve Via Verde toll double-billing on rental cars.*

## Pattern 5: An "official" is an official only at an official office

The fifth pattern is the stranger in authority. Real Portuguese police — PSP (Polícia de Segurança Pública) in blue uniforms in cities, GNR (Guarda Nacional Republicana) in green uniforms outside cities and at rural checkpoints, and PSP Turismo (tourist police) in clearly-badged vests at major tourist sites — operate only from official stations and posted checkpoints. They do not conduct on-the-spot "fines" against foreigners for nonexistent violations. They do not ask to search your wallet at a bar. They do not demand cash payable on the spot.

The *faux policier* pattern runs in three forms. The first is the Rossio "plainclothes police" companion to the drug-tout scam. After a first "dealer" has offered fake hashish, a second man approaches claiming to be plainclothes police, demanding €100–€300 "fine" or "bribe" to avoid arrest. The defense is a specific sentence: "*mostre-me o seu cartão de identificação da PSP — eu quero ir à esquadra*" (show me your PSP ID — I want to go to the police station). Real plainclothes officers would produce the ID. A fake one will vanish. The second is the Praça do Comércio "official guide" offer — a man with a lanyard approaches tourists offering a "guided tour of Lisbon Castle for €30 per person." Portuguese palaces and castles do not use lanyard-approached freelance guides; all genuine guided tours require advance booking at the venue or a licensed agency. The third is the Pena Palace entrance "skip-the-line guide" who corners a tourist, collects €50–€100 for a "VIP entry ticket" that turns out to be the same €14 time-slot ticket everyone else has.

The defense is simple and universal. If anyone on the street identifies themselves as an official or a guide and wants to collect money from you, refuse and walk into the nearest well-lit shop or hotel lobby. For real emergencies, call **112** (the pan-European emergency number). For tourist-specific help, PSP Turismo Lisbon's English-speaking line is **+351 21 342 1634**, 24/7 (direct-dial phones for every city are listed in Appendix C). A real officer will have no problem with this. A fake one will leave immediately.

*You will see this pattern in: the Rossio "plainclothes police" companion scam (Lisbon), the Praça do Comércio "official guide" offer (Lisbon), the Pena Palace "skip-the-line guide" at Sintra, the Timeshare "prize representative" street approach in Vilamoura and Albufeira, and the Coimbra "university combined-ticket" tout at Porta Férrea.*

## Pattern 6: If you pay before you have the thing — and if you pay off-platform — you probably won't get the thing

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch by reading the fine print after you have committed — so this pattern is really about what to pin down before you click *Confirm* or hand over cash. And in Portugal, it has a specific and dangerous modern form: **the off-platform Booking.com payment request**.

**Platform bookings have chargeback rights. Off-platform bookings do not.** Booking.com, Airbnb, Expedia, Hotels.com, and VRBO hold your payment and offer dispute resolution if something goes wrong. Once you leave the platform — pay via bank transfer, WhatsApp link, SEPA direct, or a link in an email that says "please re-verify your card" — you lose every consumer-protection protection that made the platform safe.

The scam patterns that exploit this:

You book a Sintra guesthouse on Booking.com; 24–48 hours before arrival, you receive a WhatsApp message from the "host" claiming your card "didn't process" and asking you to re-enter it at a link. The link is a phishing clone at *booking-support.com* or *bk-secure.pt*. Your card is charged €500–€2,000 in fraudulent transactions within days. You see a stunning Ribeira apartment (Porto) on Idealista at 40% below Booking.com prices; the landlord asks for a SEPA bank transfer deposit; the address is real but the listing was scraped from a genuine property the scammer has no connection to. You book a Nazaré big-wave-season apartment direct via a Facebook Marketplace post at 50% below market; the "owner" cancels your confirmed booking 48 hours before arrival citing "property damage" and offers to re-book you off-platform at a "discount"; the off-platform booking never materializes. You pay a tuk-tuk tout at Praça do Comércio €150 cash for a "full Lisbon tour" that is supposed to include a "palace tasting" — which turns out to be a commission-driven shopping stop at a tile shop, and the tuk-tuk leaves without completing the itinerary.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction you make in Portugal. Book accommodation only through platforms that offer pay-through-platform guarantees and dispute resolution (Booking.com, Airbnb, VRBO, Expedia, Hotels.com) — never by Facebook, Instagram, Idealista direct-contact, or SEPA bank transfer to an unknown IBAN. When a host messages "your card didn't process" or "re-verify at this link," recognize it as the off-platform phishing signal and refuse; log into Booking.com directly via the app (not via the message link) and contact the platform's support. Verify any property on Google Street View before you depart. Pay every rental, excursion, tour, or hotel by **credit card** (Visa, Mastercard, American Express) rather than SEPA, debit, or bank transfer — only a credit card gives you a chargeback right, and you can reverse a fraudulent charge within thirty to sixty days. For major events — Carnaval in Madeira, NOS Alive, Web Summit Lisbon, São João festival in Porto, the Nazaré big-wave season, or Algarve peak summer — book 6–9 months ahead through recognized platforms; the off-platform discount is always a scam, without exception.

### One more: the "commission stop" pattern on day tours

Portugal has its own set of tour-adjacent traps worth knowing. Any guided Lisbon tour that includes an unscheduled stop at an "azulejo tile workshop," a "port wine tasting experience," or a "Belém pastry factory" is almost certainly routing you to a commission-paying partner. Any Sintra day-trip bus tour from Lisbon that includes a "Cabo da Roca viewpoint" stop also typically includes a commission-driven shopping halt. Any Douro Valley wine day-trip from Porto that includes stops at "three typical wineries" often follows a circuit that pays commissions to the operator on every bottle you buy. This is not an emergency — you can simply decline to buy — but budget thirty extra minutes into your tour for it, and never feel obligated to purchase.

*You will see this pattern in: the Lisbon and Porto Booking.com property-inbox phishing pattern, the Sintra Airbnb cancel-and-re-list off-platform fraud, the Ribeira Idealista SEPA-transfer scam, the Nazaré big-wave-season accommodation re-booking scam, and every Lisbon/Porto tour-office "combined cruise + cellar + fado" or "full Sintra palace tour" commission-bundling offer.*
