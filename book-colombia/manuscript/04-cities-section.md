# The Scams: City by City {-}

The chapters that follow document every scam reported across ten Colombian destinations. Cities are ordered by how most visitors actually travel: starting with Bogotá, the Andean capital, then west to Medellín's *paisa* heartland, north to Cartagena's Caribbean walled city, south to salsa-capital Cali, and east to Santa Marta on the Caribbean coast — followed by five day-trip and weekend destinations: Guatapé (the painted-zócalos pueblo and La Piedra del Peñol), Salento (the wax-palm Cocora Valley in the Eje Cafetero), Parque Tayrona (the Caribbean jungle-to-beach park), San Andrés (the seven-color Caribbean island), and Villa de Leyva (the colonial weekend town on the Boyacá altiplano).

If you're hitting only a few of these, skip to the chapter you need. If you're doing the full loop, skim them all — the same core scams recur with local variations. The scopolamine drink-spiking that plays every night at Parque Lleras in Medellín plays identically at Bogotá's Zona T, Cali's Granada nightlife district, Salento's Calle Real, and Villa de Leyva's Calle Caliente. The restaurant "servicio incluido" overcharge documented on Plaza Santo Domingo in Cartagena repeats on Plaza Mayor in Villa de Leyva and on Guatapé's malecón. The yellow-taxi street-hail that generates the Bogotá *paseo millonario* also fuels the El Poblado–Guatapé private-driver markup, the Pereira and Armenia airport no-meter taxi trap on the way to Salento, the ADZ airport overcharge on San Andrés, and the Bogotá–Villa de Leyva hostel-driver pitch. Learn the pattern in one city, and you'll spot it the instant you step out of the next airport taxi.

## The Ten Destinations at a Glance {-}

![Bogotá — Plaza de Bolívar under the Andes](assets/cities/bogota.jpg)

**Bogotá** — The Andean capital at 2,640 meters, home to the Gold Museum and the country's densest concentration of its most dangerous scam: the *paseo millonario* express kidnapping. A yellow taxi hailed on the street near Plaza de Bolívar or La Candelaria at night stops to "pick up a cousin," and the next six hours are spent at ATMs under the eye of an armed second man. The 2025 US State Department Colombia advisory specifically names Bogotá's *paseo millonario* as the leading cause of financial loss affecting American tourists. Also: the La Candelaria fake-tour-guide shakedown, the Emerald Trading Center counterfeit-stone upsell, the Monserrate street-agent "VIP funicular" pitch, and the Zona T scopolamine drink-spiking that mirrors Medellín's Parque Lleras pattern.

![Medellín — Metrocable above the paisa hillsides](assets/cities/medellin.jpg)

**Medellín** — *Paisa* innovation, eternal-spring weather, and the country's most sophisticated scopolamine-setup ecosystem. A Tinder match in El Poblado leads to a bar near Parque Lleras, the attractive local insists on ordering a round of *aguardiente*, and the rest of the night disappears. Reports to the Policía de Turismo de Medellín tripled between 2023 and 2025. Also: the Comuna 13 graffiti-tour deposit fraud from unverified Facebook operators, the Guatapé WhatsApp-apartment scam targeting foreign day-trippers, the JMC airport taxi-vs.-DiDi overcharge dance, and the Parque Lleras restaurant bill-padding that mirrors Cartagena's walled-city pattern.

![Cartagena — walled-city bougainvillea under the Caribbean](assets/cities/cartagena.jpg)

**Cartagena** — The walled UNESCO city, Caribbean heat, and the country's most pervasive tourist-restaurant overcharging cluster. Plaza Santo Domingo seafood restaurants that quote in dollars with inflated exchange rates; the "rose seller" script that places a flower in your companion's hand and then demands COP 50,000; the Bocagrande and Playa Blanca jet-ski operators who demand your passport as illegal deposit; the Rosario Islands "all-inclusive day tour" tout whose boat either doesn't show or lands on a worse beach than promised. The walled city is safe to walk at night — it is the single safest tourist district in Colombia. The scams here are almost entirely financial.

![Cali — salsa dancers on a Granada terrace](assets/cities/cali.jpg)

**Cali** — The salsa capital of the world, anchoring the Valle del Cauca, and the country's second-most-active scopolamine ecosystem after Medellín. The Tinder match in Granada leads to a bar, the *aguardiente* arrives dosed, and the rest of the night plays out on the same mechanic as Medellín. Cali's specific variants: the CLO airport "meter broken" taxi trick that Google Maps immediately exposes, the fake salsa-lesson "private studio" upsell, and the San Antonio fake-authority drug search targeting solo foreign travelers. Cali is the Colombian city where an app-only transit rule (never a street taxi) matters most.

![Santa Marta — the malecón at Caribbean dusk](assets/cities/santa-marta.jpg)

**Santa Marta** — Colombia's oldest surviving city, the Tayrona jungle-to-beach coast, and the gateway to the Lost City trek. The El Rodadero to Centro Histórico taxi bill-switch that palms your 20,000-peso note and returns a 2,000; the malecón phone-snatch-via-photo-offer, where a man asks to "use your phone to take a picture" and runs; the Taganga dive-shop "broken regulator" damage claim; the scooter-rental passport-deposit demand (illegal under Colombian law); and the Tayrona National Park "private guide required" gate pitch, which the park does not actually require. Santa Marta and Taganga demand more situational awareness than Cartagena but less than Bogotá.

![Guatapé — La Piedra del Peñol above the painted zócalo pueblo](assets/cities/guatape.jpg)

**Guatapé** — The painted-zócalos Antioquian pueblo and La Piedra del Peñol monolith, two hours east of Medellín on the El Peñol reservoir. The dominant scam is the El Poblado hostel "private driver" markup — touts in Medellín hostels selling COP 250,000 private rides for a route that runs COP 18,000–22,000 by Terminal del Norte cooperative bus. At the rock, fake parking attendants and fake "VIP stair access" shake down arriving cars; on the malecón, "private" boat tours run identical loops to the cooperative shared boat at ten times the price. The single violent risk is the El Peñol–Guatapé highway corridor, where motorcycle robbery teams target visible jewelry — *El Colombiano* documented an October 2025 case in which a US tourist was shot six times on this road after resisting a stickup.

![Salento — Cocora Valley wax palms above the coffee axis](assets/cities/salento.jpg)

**Salento** — The postcard pueblo of Colombia's Eje Cafetero coffee axis, gateway to the Valle de Cocora wax palms (the world's tallest palm species), and the most-visited rural destination in the country. The risks cluster on transport chokepoints: PEI and AXM airport no-meter taxi traps quoting COP 250,000–350,000 for what should be a metered COP 80,000–120,000 ride; the Salento → Cocora Willys jeep "private" pitch at COP 80,000–150,000 for what's a COP 8,000–10,000 cooperative seat; and the mid-trail horseback extortion in the Cocora *bosque de niebla* that demands COP 100,000–200,000 once you're an hour into the woods. The coffee-farm tour ecosystem runs a parallel pattern of unregistered "agencies" taking deposits for tours that don't show.

![Parque Tayrona — Cabo San Juan jungle and Caribbean coves](assets/cities/tayrona.jpg)

**Parque Tayrona** — The 150-square-kilometer Caribbean park east of Santa Marta where the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta descends through cloud forest to the sea. The friction is concentrated at the El Zaino gate (touts running a "park closed today" redirect to a non-existent "alternative entrance" 15 km up the road) and inside the park (captive-market vendor overcharging, "private boat" pitches that load you onto the same cooperative boat at four times the price, and most consequentially, theft from the Cabo San Juan locker bank during overnight stays). Defense at the gate is the Parques Nacionales website (open/closed status published the same day); defense in-park is a small dry-bag worn on your body for valuables.

![San Andrés — seven-color Caribbean above the Seaflower reef](assets/cities/san-andres.jpg)

**San Andrés** — The largest island of Colombia's Caribbean overseas department, 750 km north of the mainland, anchored on the Spratt Bight beach hotel strip in North End. The mandatory scam is the *Tarjeta de Turismo* resale fraud — every visitor must buy a USD 35 tourist card before boarding the flight, but resellers in Bogotá, Medellín, and at the San Andrés airport sell it at USD 80–150 with claims of "express processing" or "VIP queue" (neither exists). The other three signature patterns: the ADZ airport-to-hotel taxi overcharge (real rate COP 15,000–25,000; tout rate COP 80,000–150,000); the golf-cart rental damage-deposit trap that withholds USD 200–500 cash deposits over fabricated scratches; and the Johnny Cay "VIP private boat tour" pitch that loads you onto the cooperative shared boat anyway.

![Villa de Leyva — Plaza Mayor under the Boyacá altiplano sky](assets/cities/villa-de-leyva.jpg)

**Villa de Leyva** — The 450-year-old colonial weekend town on the Boyacá altiplano, three hours north of Bogotá, anchored on Plaza Mayor (the largest cobblestoned plaza in the Americas). Risks are entirely financial: the Bogotá hostel "private driver" markup at COP 350,000–600,000 for a route the Terminal del Norte cooperative bus runs at COP 25,000–35,000; the dual-menu USD/COP markup on Plaza Mayor restaurants and the tourist-menu switch (the dish brought differs from the dish ordered, price unchanged); the Festival de Luces y Cometas weekend (early December) lodging fraud at COP 800,000–1,500,000 per night for properties that don't exist; the Iguaque and Pozos Azules fake-guide pattern at the trailheads; and the Calle Caliente bar drink-spike that mirrors Bogotá's smaller-scale Zona T pattern.
