Cartagena de Indias is Colombia's Caribbean jewel — a 450-year-old walled city inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984, with bougainvillea over pastel stucco, Spanish colonial balconies over narrow cobblestone streets, and the Castillo San Felipe de Barajas anchoring the skyline at dusk. Cruise ships dock daily at the Manga terminal, the walled-city restaurants fill nightly, and the beaches at Bocagrande, Playa Blanca, and the Rosario Islands run a parallel economy of jet-ski rentals, catamaran day trips, and beach-massage vendors. Cartagena is also, per Cruise Lines International Association data, the most visited Colombian city among international day-trippers.

The critical Cartagena pattern is financial, not violent. The walled city is the single safest tourist district in Colombia — the Policía de Turismo presence is heavy, CCTV coverage is comprehensive, and violent crime against foreign visitors is rare. What trips up travelers here is overcharging, specifically in three forms.

The first is **restaurant USD-pricing with inflated exchange rates**. Walled-city restaurants on Plaza Santo Domingo, Calle del Arsenal, and around the Plaza de San Diego routinely quote menu prices in US dollars at internal exchange rates 15 to 30 percent worse than the current interbank rate. A USD 45 seafood platter settles in pesos at COP 180,000–200,000, while the same platter at a peso-menu restaurant three streets inside Getsemaní costs COP 85,000–110,000. The English menu is the second trap: many walled-city restaurants keep two menus — one in Spanish at honest peso prices and one in English at inflated dollar prices. Ask for *la carta en pesos*, read every line item before ordering, and pay with a foreign credit card (Visa or Mastercard) rather than cash — card issuers apply the interbank rate automatically, which always beats the restaurant's internal USD rate.

The second is **Rosario Islands all-inclusive day-tour fraud**. The Islas del Rosario are a genuine archipelago forty-five minutes by boat from Cartagena, with legitimate day tours that include transport, lunch, and snorkeling for roughly COP 150,000–200,000 per person. The scam variant: a street tout near the Muelle de los Pegasos terminal (or a Facebook ad targeting foreign travelers) sells an "all-inclusive Rosario, Playa Blanca, lunch, and drinks" package for USD 80–120 per person. The boat may be an unlicensed craft without life jackets, the lunch may be a substandard beach shack, or the tour may drop you at Playa Blanca (on the mainland) rather than at the actual Rosario Islands, then charge "extras" at triple price throughout the day. Book only through a licensed operator with a physical ticket booth inside the Muelle de los Pegasos terminal, a posted license number, and an itinerary that names specific islands.

The third is the **Bocagrande and Playa Blanca jet-ski, boat-rental, and beach-massage ecosystem**. Unlicensed jet-ski operators demand your passport as "deposit" (explicitly illegal under Colombian law), then refuse to return it at the end of the rental until you pay USD 200–500 for "damage" — typically to a throttle or hull that was pre-existing. Beach-massage vendors on Playa Blanca approach relentlessly, often starting to massage without consent and demanding payment afterward. The countermeasures are simple: **never hand over your original passport as deposit**; only a photocopy is legal. Book water sports through your hotel concierge, which will vet the operator. On Playa Blanca, say *"no, gracias"* firmly and decisively to every approach, and keep walking; touts disengage within ten seconds if you do not engage.

Beyond these three, Cartagena has the rose-seller pressure scam on Plaza Santo Domingo (a woman places a rose in your companion's hand and demands COP 50,000), the occasional fake-tour-guide approach in Getsemaní, and the ever-present lightly-pushy vendor ecosystem outside the Castillo San Felipe. None of these are dangerous. All are financial. All are defeated by the same *no, gracias* and a steady walk.

Cartagena is remarkably comfortable to visit. The walled city is entirely walkable. Taxi rides from the airport are short — CTG sits fifteen minutes out, with a posted flat rate of roughly COP 30,000–40,000 to the walled city at the official counter. Daytime photography, walking tours, and dinner on Plaza Santo Domingo are the signature experience here, and all of them are low-risk. The caveats above are the full list of what to watch for.
