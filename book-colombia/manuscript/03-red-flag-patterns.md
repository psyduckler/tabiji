# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The thirty-three scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns, and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: A stranger who approaches you first is almost certainly running a script

This is the single most important habit you can take to Colombia. The instant someone steps into your path uninvited near Plaza de Bolívar in Bogotá, on the Carrera Séptima pedestrian mall, at Parque Lleras in Medellín's El Poblado, inside Cartagena's walled city on Plaza Santo Domingo, on the Caribbean boardwalk at Santa Marta, or in Cali's Granada nightlife district, the interaction that follows is a rehearsed script. You are not being greeted. You are being sorted.

Colombia's scam ecosystem is unusually warm, and the openers almost always sound friendly, flirtatious, or helpful. At Parque Lleras in Medellín, a young woman with near-fluent English walks up, asks where you are from, and insists on ordering a round of *aguardiente* or a cocktail from a roaming waiter — the drink arrives spiked with scopolamine. In Bogotá's Zona T, an attractive local offers to "continue the night at another bar," and the second venue is where the scopolamine is dosed. On Plaza Santo Domingo in Cartagena, a woman places a rose in your companion's hand and then demands COP 50,000 for the gift. On the Santa Marta malecón, a man offers to take your photo with your phone and runs with it. In Cali's Granada, the identical Tinder-match-to-scopolamine pattern plays out nightly.

None of these interactions start with you asking for help. That is the tell. Colombians are famously warm, but they do not generally chat up foreign tourists on the street to buy them drinks, give them roses, or take their photos.

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: when any stranger starts a conversation with you near a famous site, a bus station, or a bar, reply *"no, gracias"* — firmly — and keep walking. Do not break stride. Do not engage. If the person follows you, repeat the phrase once, louder, and step into the nearest shop, café, or hotel lobby. Colombian politeness conventions do not require you to accept a rose, sit through a sales pitch, or share a drink with a stranger, and walking away is not rude. If the interaction is a drink offer at a bar from someone you just met, refuse politely, order your own drink directly from the bartender, and watch it poured.

*You will see this pattern in the Medellín scopolamine Tinder-setup, the Bogotá Zona T "continue the night" drink-spiking, the Cartagena Plaza Santo Domingo rose-seller pressure scam, the Santa Marta malecón phone-snatch-via-photo-offer, the Cali Granada bar-chat scopolamine, and almost every approach scene in the city chapters that follow.*

## Pattern 2: "The meter is broken" means you are being overcharged — or worse

The second pattern is taxis. Colombia's street-taxi economy is enormous, and a meaningful share of tourist rides begin with the driver refusing the meter, declining the app, or quoting a flat price. In Bogotá, this extends into the most dangerous scam in the country: the *paseo millonario* (millionaire's ride), in which the driver stops partway to "pick up a cousin" — a second man, armed — and the next several hours are spent at ATMs withdrawing your daily maximum.

Colombia's defensive architecture differs from Mexico's or Argentina's, and that matters. Uber operates in a legal gray zone: rides are legal, but drivers have occasionally been fined, so the app prompts you to ride in the front seat "as if you were a friend of the driver." **DiDi** and **InDrive** are fully legal, widely used, and cheaper. Both run driver-rating systems with real consequences for bad actors. In Cartagena and Santa Marta, where Uber coverage is thinner, Cabify fills the gap. The choice is almost always an app rather than a street taxi — and at airports, always the official taxi-rank counter.

In **Bogotá**, the El Dorado airport (BOG) taxi-rank counter posts a flat fare of roughly COP 50,000–65,000 to downtown — insist on that. Arrivals-hall touts quote USD 30–50. Street taxis hailed in La Candelaria at night after Plaza de Bolívar tours are the highest-risk population for *paseo millonario*; **never** hail a yellow taxi on the street in Bogotá after dark. In **Medellín**, the JMC airport sits forty-five minutes out of town, and the airport-rank taxi costs roughly COP 120,000 — DiDi or InDrive does the same trip for COP 80,000–90,000. In **Cartagena**, the CTG airport-rank rate to the walled city is COP 30,000–40,000; an Uber pickup requires walking out of the airport zone proper. In **Cali** (CLO) and **Santa Marta** (SMR), the same pattern: official rank for flat taxi, app for everything else.

The defense is short and universal. In every major Colombian city, use an app (DiDi, InDrive, Uber, or Cabify) rather than a street taxi. Pricing is transparent, the driver is rated, and there is no "meter-is-broken" conversation. For airport pickups, use the **posted-flat-rate counter** inside the terminal; never follow a tout to the curb. **Never hail a yellow taxi on the street in Bogotá at night.** If you must take a street taxi elsewhere, say calmly *"Taxímetro, por favor"* ("Meter, please"). If the driver refuses, get out. If the route deviates, watch Google Maps in real time from the back seat and ask, calmly, why the route is not matching.

*You will see this pattern in the Bogotá paseo-millonario express kidnapping, the Cartagena airport-taxi fixed-fare gambit, the Medellín ride-hailing "unmetered" variant, the Cali airport "meter broken" trick, and the Santa Marta taxi-bill-switch counterfeit-change scam.*

## Pattern 3: Prices that aren't posted will surprise you later

The third pattern is the unposted price. Colombian restaurants — in Cartagena's walled city along Calle del Arsenal and around Plaza Santo Domingo, on the Medellín Parque Lleras strip, on the Bogotá Zona G and Parque 93 restaurant rows, and on every tourist-facing malecón in Santa Marta and Taganga — will sometimes quote a price that is deliberately vague. *"Precio especial."* *"El servicio."* *"Propina incluida."* *"Después hablamos."* Each of these phrases hides a later surprise.

The **cubierto**, or service charge, is the key concept. A legitimate Colombian service charge is typically 10 percent, added as *propina voluntaria* — a voluntary tip, and by Colombian law you can refuse it. Tourist-strip restaurants in the walled city go further. They add a 10 percent service charge *plus* an 8 percent cover (*cubierto*) that was not on the menu, then hand you a bill in US dollars at a tourist-unfriendly exchange rate. A party of four at a Plaza Santo Domingo seafood restaurant can walk out paying USD 200 in fees and currency markup alone, on top of the actual food.

The English menu is the second trap. Many restaurants in Cartagena's Getsemaní, in Medellín's El Poblado, and in Bogotá's Zona G keep two menus: a Spanish-language menu at honest peso prices, and an English menu with the same items marked up 30 to 60 percent, often priced in dollars. The waiter hands you the English menu automatically when you sit down.

The defense is a single habit, repeated before every transaction: ask to see the printed menu before you sit down, photograph the total bill before you pay, and — if there is any doubt — ask *"¿Puedo ver el menú en español, por favor?"* When the bill arrives, read it line by line and ask about any unrecognized item. The 10 percent *propina* can be declined — say *"sin propina, por favor"* — if the service did not warrant it. Pay in Colombian pesos using a foreign credit card where possible: Visa and Mastercard apply the current interbank exchange rate, which is almost always better than the restaurant's tourist dollar rate, and you retain chargeback rights if a fraudulent charge appears.

*You will see this pattern in the Cartagena walled-city restaurant USD-pricing gambit, the Medellín Parque Lleras bill-padding, the Bogotá G-Zona "servicio incluido" auto-add, the coffee-tour Eje Cafetero "premium tasting" upsell, and the Santa Marta Taganga beach-club menu switch.*

## Pattern 4: The "scratch on the car," the "damage deposit," the "broken jet-ski"

The fourth pattern is the damage claim. You rent a scooter in Santa Marta's Rodadero, and when you return it, the operator points at a pre-existing scratch and demands USD 300 for "damage." You rent a jet-ski on the Cartagena beaches, and the "broken throttle" is discovered only after you have handed over a passport as deposit. You rent a car at Medellín's JMC airport, and the drop-off "dent inspection" uncovers damage that was not there when you handed the vehicle over.

Cartagena's beach jet-ski and boat-rental economy is the epicenter of the damage-claim variant. Unlicensed operators on Playa Bocagrande and Playa Blanca demand your passport as deposit (illegal under Colombian law), then report "damage" when you return the craft, and refuse to release your passport until you pay USD 200–500. The passport-deposit demand is the single biggest tell that you are dealing with an illegitimate operator.

The defense is photographic, and it is mandatory. Before you accept any rental — car, scooter, jet-ski, boat, bicycle, or anything else — take eight to twelve date-stamped photos of the item from every angle, including close-ups of any existing damage, the odometer or hour meter, and any accessories included. **Never** hand over your original passport as deposit. Colombian law prohibits this; only photocopies of the ID page are legal. If an operator demands the original, walk away and find a licensed rental company (Avis, Hertz, or Europcar for cars; any shop affiliated with your hotel for water sports). Pay every rental with a credit card — never a debit card or cash — since only a credit card gives you chargeback rights if a fraudulent "damage" charge appears. For Cartagena water sports, book through your hotel concierge, who will vet the operator.

For **Santa Marta and Taganga beach theft**, the defense is the same habit as on every Caribbean coast: never leave anything visible on your towel when you swim. Take a small dry bag into the water with phone, wallet, and passport, or leave everything in the hotel safe and bring only what you need. If your phone is snatched despite the precautions, file a *denuncia* at the nearest CAI (Comando de Atención Inmediata) police post within twenty-four hours; the police report is required for your insurance claim.

*You will see this pattern in the Cartagena jet-ski "broken throttle" passport-deposit scam, the Cartagena Playa Blanca beach-massage harassment-to-theft sequence, the Santa Marta scooter-rental damage claim, the Medellín rental-car drop-off dispute, and the general "leave your passport as deposit" illegal-operator tell.*

## Pattern 5: An "official" is an official only at an official office

The fifth pattern is the stranger in authority. Real Colombian police — **Policía Nacional** in dark-green uniforms, **Policía de Tránsito** in yellow-and-green with reflective vests, **Policía de Turismo** in distinctive tourist-patrol gear, and **Migración Colombia** officers in dark-blue uniforms at airports and border posts — operate only from official stations, checkpoints, and their marked patrol motorcycles or cars. They do not demand to see the cash in your wallet on the street. They do not ask for a "fine" payable in cash on the spot. They do not conduct drug checks on sidewalks in tourist areas.

The *falsos policías* pattern runs across Colombian cities in two main forms. The first, documented repeatedly in Bogotá's Candelaria and Medellín's El Poblado, is the "counterfeit check" or "drug search." Two men in polo shirts flash unofficial laminated cards, claim they are checking for counterfeit currency or for drugs near a nightlife area, and ask to "see" the bills in your wallet or the contents of your pockets. That is when they lift cash or swap a genuine 50,000-peso bill for a counterfeit. The second, concentrated at Bogotá's El Dorado airport and at Cali's terrestrial bus terminal, is the fake Migración or fake customs check: a man in a high-visibility vest tells tourists their entry stamp is "incomplete" and a fine must be paid to avoid a problem leaving the country. Real Migración Colombia officers do not do this. Real Colombian customs officers do not do this.

The defense is simple and universal. If anyone on the street identifies themselves as an official and wants to examine your wallet, passport, pocket contents, or phone, refuse and walk into the nearest well-lit shop or hotel lobby. Say calmly, *"Vamos al CAI"* ("Let's go to the tourist-police post"). Dial **123** from your phone and put the call on speaker if the person has not already left. Real officers have no problem with this. Fake ones leave immediately. Each major Colombian city has a **Policía de Turismo** office with its own dedicated number; the full list is in the appendix.

*You will see this pattern in the Bogotá Candelaria fake-plainclothes drug-search, the Medellín El Poblado "counterfeit inspection" variant, the Cartagena walled-city fake-tour-guide authority gambit, the Cali fake-bus-terminal inspector, and the Santa Marta border-stamp-fine trick.*

## Pattern 6: If you pay before you have the thing, you probably won't get the thing

The sixth pattern is the one you can catch only by reading the fine print after you have committed — so this pattern is really about what to pin down before you click *Confirm* or hand over cash.

You book a Cartagena Rosario Islands "all-inclusive day tour" from a street tout, pay in cash, and the boat either never shows up or drops you on a substandard beach with extras billed at triple price. You book a Medellín Comuna 13 graffiti tour through a Facebook page that turns out to use stolen photos, arrive to find no tour booked, and lose a USD 50 deposit. You book a Guatapé overnight package via WhatsApp to a "host" whose apartment, on arrival, does not exist. You hand a Bogotá street agent USD 200 for a "VIP Monserrate funicular package" that was never purchased. You pay a deposit on a Cartagena walled-city apartment by Western Union to a "landlord" whose property is actually someone else's Airbnb, photographed from the booking site.

All of these work because you committed before you knew the terms — and then the terms, the vendor, the venue, or the platform were not what you thought they were.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction you make in Colombia. Book accommodation only through platforms that offer pay-through-platform guarantees and dispute resolution (Booking.com, Airbnb, Expedia, Agoda, Hotels.com) — never through Facebook or WhatsApp, and never by direct wire transfer to a private Colombian Bancolombia account or to Western Union. Verify any property on Google Street View before you depart: a legitimate Colombian apartment has visible signage and appears on at least one established booking platform. Pay every rental, excursion, or hotel by credit card (Visa, Mastercard, or American Express) rather than debit, cash, or wire transfer — only a credit card gives you a chargeback right, and you can reverse a fraudulent charge within sixty days.

For **Cartagena Rosario Islands tours**, book only through a licensed operator verified by the Cartagena tourism office — the mainland ferry terminal at Muelle de los Pegasos has a legitimate rank with posted prices. For **Medellín Comuna 13 graffiti tours**, book through a hostel-recommended guide with a public TripAdvisor or Viator listing. For **Guatapé day trips**, book through your Medellín hotel concierge or an established aggregator (GetYourGuide or Viator). For **Bogotá Monserrate**, walk up from Carrera 2 or take the funicular directly — never buy from a street agent. For **long-distance buses**, use the terminal ticket windows directly at the **Terminal de Transporte de Bogotá**, the **Terminal del Norte de Medellín**, or **Cartagena's** new terminal — never a street agent.

### One more: the emerald-scam ecosystem

Colombia is the world's largest emerald producer, and Bogotá's **Emerald Trading Center** on Avenida Jiménez is a legitimate wholesale district — but it is also the epicenter of counterfeit and dyed-quartz sales to tourists. Street vendors along Carrera Séptima and touts who "befriend" you near the Gold Museum will invite you to a "private dealer" for a "\$1,200 emerald" that is either synthetic, heavily dyed, or valued at pennies on the international market. Real Colombian emeralds are sold with a certificate from a named gemological laboratory — GIA or AIGS are the internationally recognized authorities. A certificate from an unknown Bogotá laboratory is not a certificate; it is part of the scam. If you want a real emerald, buy from a licensed Bogotá jeweler (Galería Cano, Lladro) or from **Mr. Emerald** in the Emerald Trade Center — not from a street introduction.

*You will see this pattern in the Cartagena Rosario Islands tout-boat scam, the Medellín Comuna 13 fake-tour-guide deposit fraud, the Guatapé WhatsApp apartment scam, the Bogotá emerald counterfeit ecosystem, the Cartagena walled-city Airbnb impersonation, the Bogotá Monserrate street-agent VIP pitch, and the general "wire a deposit before you arrive" scam that hits every Colombian destination.*
