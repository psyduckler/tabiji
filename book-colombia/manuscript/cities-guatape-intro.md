Guatapé is a small painted town two hours east of Medellín, in the Antioquia department, on the southern shore of the Embalse del Peñol-Guatapé — a reservoir created in the 1970s when EPM dammed the Nare River and submerged the original village of Old Peñol. The pueblo today is famous for two things: the **zócalos** (vivid bas-relief panels at the base of every house in the centro histórico), and **La Piedra del Peñol**, a 220-meter granite monolith with a 740-step staircase up the side that delivers the country's most photographed view. Guatapé draws roughly 1.5 million visitors a year — overwhelmingly day-trippers from Medellín — and almost every scam in this chapter exploits the gap between what travelers expect a guided pueblo day to cost and what the DIY route actually costs.

The dominant risk in Guatapé is **not violent**. The pueblo's centro histórico is heavily touristed, well-policed, and broadly safe at all hours. The real exposure is on the road in (Medellín → Guatapé private-driver markups), at the rock (fake parking attendants and fake "VIP stairs" at La Piedra), on the water (overpriced "private" reservoir tours that are actually shared boats), and in agency packaging (Medellín-based "all-inclusive" tours that bill the entrance fee, the boat, and lunch as cash extras at the destination). Each of these is a 4–12x markup over the legitimate cost, and each is defeated by the same defensive habit: **price the DIY route before you accept any agency or hostel-tout pitch**.

The single exception to "not violent" is the El Peñol–Guatapé highway corridor, particularly the stretch known as **Sector La Réplica**, where motorcycle robbery teams target pedestrians and cyclists wearing visible jewelry. *El Colombiano* documented an October 2025 incident in which a US tourist resisted a motorcycle stickup on this road and was shot six times, requiring an air evacuation to Rionegro. The lesson the local *Policía de Turismo* repeats is the Antioquia rule of *no dar papaya* — leave gold chains, watches, and DSLR straps in the hotel safe before walking the highway, and if a motorcycle pulls alongside and a parrillero draws a weapon, **hand over the item without struggle**. The chain is not worth the hospital bed.

The legitimate route for an independent Guatapé day costs roughly **COP 90,000–110,000 all-in**: a Sotrasanvicente or Transoriente bus from Medellín's Terminal del Norte (COP 18,000–22,000 each way, departing every 15–30 minutes from bay 14), the official La Piedra del Peñol entrance fee (COP 25,000), the cooperative shared-boat reservoir loop (COP 25,000–35,000 per seat from the malecón pier), and a centro almuerzo (COP 20,000–30,000). The agency-packaged equivalent runs COP 200,000+ once the on-day cash extras are added. The Terminal del Norte is one stop on **Metro line A (Caribe station)** — a 30-minute, 2,800-COP metro ride from El Poblado. If a hostel tout pitches a "private driver" at COP 250,000 per person, you are being asked to pay a 12x markup to skip a metro ride that takes half an hour.

Guatapé rewards skepticism toward every printed flyer and every clipboard-carrying tout in El Poblado, Provenza, and on the malecón itself. Buy bus tickets at the Terminal del Norte cooperative kiosk window. Pay the rock entrance fee only at the staircase-base ticket booth. Buy boat seats at the cooperative kiosk on the embarcadero pier with a printed receipt. Demand the *Registro Nacional de Turismo* (RNT) number on every agency voucher before paying any deposit. And when in the centro, ask for the *carta en pesos* — Guatapé's restaurants run the same dual-menu USD/COP markup pattern as Cartagena's walled city.
