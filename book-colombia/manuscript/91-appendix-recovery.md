# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are organized by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## If a scopolamine ("burundanga") drugging is suspected — go to a hospital first

This is the single most important paragraph in this book. If you wake up with missing memory, missing cash, or missing time, or you feel strangely compliant after accepting a drink from a stranger in any Colombian nightlife district, **go directly to a hospital emergency room before you do anything else**. Scopolamine clears from urine within twelve hours and from blood within twenty-four; toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A medical exam is also the single most important piece of evidence your travel-insurance provider, credit-card issuer, and embassy will ask for.

The hospitals with the strongest emergency toxicology capability and English-speaking staff in each major Colombian city are:

- **Bogotá**: Fundación Santa Fe de Bogotá (Carrera 7 No. 117–15), Hospital Universitario San Ignacio (Carrera 7 No. 40–62), Clínica del Country (Carrera 16 No. 82–57).
- **Medellín**: Hospital Pablo Tobón Uribe (Calle 78B No. 69–240), Clínica Las Américas (Diagonal 75B No. 2A–80), Hospital Universitario San Vicente Fundación.
- **Cartagena**: Hospital Bocagrande (Calle 6A No. 6–91), Hospital Universitario del Caribe (Calle 29 No. 50–50).
- **Cali**: Fundación Valle del Lili (Carrera 98 No. 18–49), Clínica Imbanaco (Carrera 38 No. 5A–100).
- **Santa Marta**: Clínica El Prado (Calle 31 No. 19–44), Hospital Universitario Fernando Troconis.

Ask for an *análisis toxicológico* (toxicology panel) and request that scopolamine specifically be tested — some labs screen for common drugs but not for scopolamine unless prompted. Colombian private hospitals bill travel insurance directly once a *Guarantee of Payment* is in place; public hospitals treat emergencies without up-front payment and bill afterward.

Only after medical care should you file a *denuncia* with the Policía de Turismo. The order matters.

## In the first fifteen minutes (non-medical scams)

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — an emerald "dealer's" shop, a tour-operator office, a restaurant with a suddenly inflated bill, or a taxi that has detoured to a "friend's" emerald shop — do not argue, do not negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. Get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. A signed credit-card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, dial **123** (Colombia's unified emergency line). For medical emergencies specifically, **125** (ambulance, in some cities) also connects.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection (Google Maps dropped-pin is ideal), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward. If a pickpocket team is still in the area, walk 100 meters in any direction before you pull out your phone to screenshot.

If your bag or wallet was just stolen in the street, shout **"¡Al ladrón!"** ("Thief!") — Colombian shopkeepers and passers-by will often help, and phone snatches in Bogotá and Medellín are sometimes recovered when the shout goes up fast. In a Bogotá TransMilenio station or a Medellín Metro station, the station staff are the closest authority; they can call the Policía and hold security-camera footage for you.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest **CAI** (Comando de Atención Inmediata, the small neighborhood police post found every few blocks) or, better, to the nearest **Policía de Turismo** office. Major city addresses:

- **Bogotá Policía de Turismo**: Carrera 13 No. 26–62, +57 1 337-4413.
- **Medellín Policía de Turismo**: Carrera 66 No. 48–110 (near the Parque Arví Metrocable), +57 4 511-1111.
- **Cartagena Policía de Turismo**: Calle 32 No. 3–14 in the walled city, +57 5 660-2218.
- **Cali Policía de Turismo**: Avenida Colombia No. 4–47, +57 2 885-3999.
- **Santa Marta Policía de Turismo**: Calle 22 No. 5–85, +57 5 421-3300.

Say *"Quiero hacer una denuncia"* ("I'd like to file a report"). You will walk out with a stamped *denuncia* (police report) that includes a reference number. This is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

Colombia operates an online *denuncia virtual* system at **denunciavirtual.policia.gov.co** that allows you to file most non-emergency reports from a hotel lobby. For violent crimes, drink-spiking cases, or any incident where evidence needs to be physically collected, in-person at a Policía de Turismo office is still required.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from Colombia and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call. If the charge was a restaurant overcharge, a tour-operator "deposit," or a rental "damage" advance, also call your bank's fraud line directly — the number on the back of your card — because fraud desks sometimes freeze faster than the Visa or Mastercard emergency lines. If the card is Colombian-issued (Bancolombia, Davivienda, BBVA Colombia, Banco de Bogotá, Banco Popular, Scotiabank Colpatria), all major banks run 24/7 card-loss lines at the numbers printed on the back of the card.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the *denuncia* reference number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Visa and Mastercard both offer strong chargeback rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend credit over debit for every Colombia trip.

For tourism-specific complaints against a registered business (a hotel that charged a phantom "tourist tax," a restaurant that padded a bill with an undisclosed cover charge, a tour operator that didn't deliver), file with the **Superintendencia de Industria y Comercio (SIC)** at **sic.gov.co** — Colombia's consumer-protection and competition regulator, which handles complaints under **Ley 1480** (the Colombian consumer-protection statute). SIC complaints are online, free, and accepted in Spanish; many Colombian tourist-facing businesses know which complaints reach the SIC, and a filed complaint sometimes produces a restitution offer that the shop refused to make on the day of the incident.

For emerald-sale fraud specifically, also file with **Mincomercio** (the Ministry of Commerce) via its consumer-complaints portal; the emerald trade is specifically regulated, and repeat-offender "private dealers" have had their operating licenses revoked after traveler complaints.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. US citizens should contact the US Embassy in Bogotá (Carrera 45 No. 24B–27); UK, Canadian, Australian, Irish, French, German, and most European citizens all have embassies in Bogotá; full list in Appendix C.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the *denuncia* reference number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position. The major dispatchers serving the Latin-American market — Allianz Global Assistance, AXA Partners, World Nomads, Chubb, Zurich, IMG Global — all operate 24/7 English-language lines for travelers and have local medical-provider networks throughout Colombia.

If the scam involved an off-platform rental or a wire-fraud hotel booking, open the dispute on the booking platform (Booking.com, Airbnb, Expedia, Agoda) as well as with your card issuer. Platforms can sometimes recover deposits the card company cannot, and Airbnb in particular has a dedicated Latin-America fraud team.

## Within the first week

File a formal consumer-protection complaint with the **Superintendencia de Industria y Comercio (SIC)** online at **sic.gov.co/proteccion-al-consumidor**. Regulators do not recover individual losses, but their actions against repeat-offender tour operators, rental shops, and hotels are one of the few effective pressures on the scam economy. Tourist-facing businesses know which complaints reach the regulator; a filed complaint sometimes produces a restitution offer.

For overcharging by licensed taxis, file a parallel complaint with the **municipal taxi authority**. Bogotá's is the **Secretaría Distrital de Movilidad** (movilidadbogota.gov.co, with a dedicated taxi-complaint form). Medellín's is the **Secretaría de Movilidad de Medellín**. Cartagena, Cali, and Santa Marta each have equivalent municipal secretariats. With the taxi's license plate number (always photograph it before getting out), the authority can identify the driver, issue fines, and sometimes produce compensation.

For banking and payment disputes — a charge your Colombian bank refuses to reverse, an ATM skim you consider the bank should cover, a disputed wire-transfer fraud — escalate to the **Superintendencia Financiera de Colombia** at **superfinanciera.gov.co**, the regulator for the Colombian banking sector.

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business's name, the address, the exact transaction details, the *denuncia* reference number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name and location only. Colombian defamation law is strict — stick to verifiable facts and receipts.

## If your phone or SIM was stolen

Call your carrier immediately to suspend the line. The three major Colombian carriers — **Claro** (dial **\*611** or **\*111**), **Movistar** (dial **\*611**), and **Tigo** (dial **\*123**) — all operate 24/7 stolen-device lines. Suspending the line prevents the thief from using your number to receive SMS two-factor-authentication codes and from running up international-roaming charges. If you are on a tourist eSIM (Airalo, Saily, Holafly, etc.), the app will let you suspend the line in a few taps; do that before the *denuncia*.

File a *denuncia* at the nearest Policía de Turismo office and include your phone's **IMEI** number (the fifteen-digit serial; find it on the original box, in your Apple or Google account, or by dialing \*#06# *before* the theft). The IMEI lets police flag the device and is required for any insurance claim. Colombia operates a centralized **BDA (Base de Datos Administrativa)** for stolen-phone IMEIs — a flagged phone becomes unusable on any Colombian carrier network, which significantly reduces the market for stolen devices.

If your SIM was the target — for example, a SIM-swap scam to intercept your bank's one-time codes — contact your bank's fraud desk before anything else; SIM-swap fraud moves faster than theft recovery, and the first hour is decisive.

## If the scam involved a *paseo millonario* (express kidnapping)

Once you are safe and at your hotel or another public place, go directly to the nearest **Sijín** (Seccional de Investigación Criminal) office of the Policía Nacional, not just the regular CAI. *Paseo millonario* is a kidnapping case under Colombian law — *secuestro simple*, Article 168 of the Penal Code — and the Sijín has the investigative authority to pursue ATM surveillance footage, phone records, and taxi-plate traces. The Bogotá Sijín main office is at Calle 71 No. 11–34.

Do not delay. ATM surveillance footage is retained for 30–60 days at most, and taxi plate records degrade fast. Every hour matters.

Notify your embassy, your travel-insurance provider, and your credit-card issuer within the same day. Most major travel-insurance policies cover *secuestro simple* cash losses up to a per-incident limit; the *denuncia* from the Sijín is the required documentation. Your credit-card issuer may be able to reverse cash-advance transactions made at ATMs during the incident, particularly those made under duress and those concentrated within an unusually short window.

For US citizens, the US Embassy in Bogotá operates a dedicated American Citizen Services line (+57 1 275-2000, ext. ACS) with after-hours emergency support. Embassy personnel can coordinate English-speaking medical care, counsel on the insurance claim, and liaise with Colombian investigators.

## A final note

The Colombia-specific reality is that *paseo millonario* and scopolamine cases together account for the significant majority of serious foreign-tourist scam losses. Both follow a medical-first, rapid-*denuncia* response pattern that differs from the financial-only recovery playbook that fits Argentina, Mexico, or Spain. If something serious has happened, the first hour — medical first if drugs were involved, Sijín first if it was a kidnapping, *denuncia* fast in every other case — is worth more than any subsequent week.

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate a replacement. Consular registration — STEP for US citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, LOCATE for UK, ROCA for Canadians, and the Irish equivalent — is worth doing before you travel, specifically for situations like this.
