Parque Nacional Natural Tayrona is the 150-square-kilometer Caribbean park east of Santa Marta where the **Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta** — the world's highest coastal mountain range — descends through cloud forest, palm jungle, and granite-strewn coves to the sea. The park is administered by **Parques Nacionales Naturales de Colombia** and accessed primarily through one of two ranger-staffed entrances: **El Zaino** (the eastern entrance, the standard route to Cabo San Juan and Arrecifes) and **Calabazo** (the western entrance, the trailhead for the *Pueblito* indigenous-village hike). Annual visitation runs around 400,000, with peak compression in December–January and Semana Santa, when daily caps are sometimes enforced at the gate. Tayrona is a one-hour drive from Santa Marta's centro and ninety minutes from the **Aeropuerto Internacional Simón Bolívar (SMR)**.

Tayrona's risks split into two categories: **financial scams clustered at the El Zaino gate** and **inside-the-park overcharging plus property crime**. The El Zaino gate is the single highest-friction point — a daily-rotating cast of touts, fake "guides," and fake "rangers" works the road in front of the official ticket booth, telling arriving tourists in surprisingly fluent English that "the park is closed today" and steering them toward an "alternative entrance" 15 km up the road that does not exist. The redirect ends at a private finca that charges COP 80,000–120,000 per person for "park access" through a back trail to nowhere. Defense: **the park's open/closed status is published the same day on the Parques Nacionales Naturales website at parquesnacionales.gov.co**; closures are normally only announced for ecological recovery (typically February and June, plus occasional unscheduled closures for indigenous ceremonial purposes). The legitimate El Zaino entrance fee is roughly **COP 80,500 for foreign adults in high season, COP 67,500 in low season**, paid at the official booth that issues a printed park-system ticket.

Inside the park, the dominant pattern is **captive-market pricing**. The walking distance from El Zaino to **Cabo San Juan** is about 2.5 hours through humid jungle (or 45 minutes by horse from Cañaveral, COP 50,000–70,000 round-trip with a cooperative *arriero*). At Cabo San Juan, a small dock operates the **boat-ferry return service** to Taganga (a fishing village on the Santa Marta side, also reachable by bus), with a posted shared-seat rate around **COP 80,000 per person** for the 90-minute coastal run; the scam variant is a "private express boat" pitch quoting COP 250,000–400,000 for the same shared boat. In-park *quioscos* (the cooperative-run snack stands) sell water and arepas at posted board prices (water around COP 5,000, arepas COP 8,000–12,000); unregistered vendors who set up away from the kiosks routinely charge COP 15,000–25,000 for the same items, then refuse change. Carry plenty of small bills.

The most consequential in-park risk is **theft from the Cabo San Juan locker bank**. The cooperative-run lockers above the beach — the kiosk where the hammocks and tents are also rented for the night — are the standard place travelers stash phones, wallets, and passports while swimming. Reports on r/Colombia and on the Tayrona-traveler Facebook groups in 2024 and 2025 describe a recurring pattern of theft from these lockers during overnight stays, particularly when the kiosk is unattended. The defenses are mundane but effective: **carry a small dry-bag with your passport, phone, cards, and most of your cash on your body in a secure compartment** even when swimming (the bag tolerates submersion); leave only the locker fee (around COP 5,000) and a change of clothes in the locker; photograph everything you put in the locker before closing it. The Cabo San Juan **Pueblito Trail** also runs a fake-indigenous-guide pattern in which a man claiming to be a community elder demands a "coca-leaf ceremony fee" of COP 50,000–100,000 mid-trail; the legitimate Kogui community access through Pueblito requires no such payment, and any genuine indigenous interaction is mediated through Parques Nacionales-vetted guides.

Tayrona rewards a few small habits. Confirm the park's open/closed status on the Parques Nacionales website the day you go. Pay only at the official El Zaino booth and keep the printed ticket on your person all day. Carry a small dry-bag for valuables and never trust the locker for items of value. Hike with at least one companion. Buy your boat-ferry seat at the Cabo San Juan cooperative dock with a printed receipt, not from a "private express" tout. The park itself is one of the most beautiful in the Americas, and the day or overnight is consistently rated the highlight of Caribbean Colombia for visitors who prepare.
