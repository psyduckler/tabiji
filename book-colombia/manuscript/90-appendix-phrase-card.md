# Appendix A: Colombian Spanish Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Colombia. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, followed by the **Spanish** text and a simple pronunciation guide in italics.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. Say the first three or four out loud a few times. You will feel much less awkward using them when the moment comes.

## A note on pronunciation

Colombian Spanish — especially the *bogotano* variety — has a well-earned reputation as one of the clearest Spanish dialects in Latin America. Three signatures help distinguish it from Mexican, Argentine, or Caribbean Spanish.

First, the **double-L (ll)** and the **y** are pronounced as a clear English "y," exactly as in "yellow." *Calle* ("street") is *KAH-yeh*. *Yo* ("I") is *yoh*. *Pollo* ("chicken") is *POH-yoh*. (This differs from Argentine Spanish, which uses a "zh" or "sh" sound.) On the Caribbean coast — Cartagena, Santa Marta, Barranquilla — the *ll* is often even softer, almost glided.

Second, the **s at the end of a syllable** is crisp and clearly pronounced in the Andean interior (Bogotá, Medellín, and the coffee region) but aspirated or dropped on the Caribbean coast. *Más o menos* in Bogotá sounds like *mahs oh MEH-nohs*; in Cartagena it softens to *mah oh MEH-noh*. This coastal dropping is the single most audible marker of *costeño* Spanish.

Third, Colombia uses **usted** (the formal "you") with near-universal frequency — even among friends, even between siblings, even between parents and children. Where a Mexican says "¿Cómo estás?" (informal *tú*), a Colombian almost always says "¿Cómo está?" (formal *usted*). For tourist purposes you do not need to master the informal *tú* forms — the *usted* forms work everywhere in Colombia, and using them is more polite rather than less.

A few pointers that will get you 80 percent of the way:

- Colombian Spanish vowels are clean and short: *a* is "ah," *e* is "eh," *i* is "ee," *o* is "oh," *u* is "oo." Never a schwa.
- The letter *h* is silent — *hola* is "OH-la," never "HO-la."
- The letter *j* (and *g* before *e* or *i*) is a throaty English "h" — *jamón* is "ha-MOHN," *Getsemaní* is "het-seh-mah-NEE."
- The letter *ñ* is "ny" — *mañana* is "ma-NYAH-na."
- The letters *ll* and *y* in Colombia are pronounced as a clear English "y" — *llamo* is "YAH-moh," *playa* is "PLAH-yah."
- The letters *c* before *e* or *i* and the letter *z* are pronounced as English "s" — *cinco* is "SEEN-koh," *Cartagena* is "kar-tah-HEH-nah." Colombia uses the **seseo**.
- The Colombian accent places a clear stress on the penultimate syllable of most words, making Colombian Spanish unusually easy for English speakers to follow.

## A note on politeness

Colombian Spanish is warmly formal. Colombians are famous — genuinely famous, not stereotypically — for addressing strangers, shopkeepers, taxi drivers, and hotel staff with *señor*/*señora*, *don*/*doña*, or *joven*/*jovencita*. Adding *por favor* at the start or end of a request and *muchas gracias* after any interaction carries extraordinary weight. Colombian politeness also allows extended warmth — a *bogotano* shopkeeper will often say *con mucho gusto* (literally "with much pleasure") instead of *de nada* for "you're welcome," and reciprocating with *muy amable* ("very kind") is natural.

The single most important register: say *usted* to every stranger in Colombia. It is never wrong.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**  
¿Cuánto cuesta?  
*KWAN-toh KWES-tah?*

**How much does it cost?** (alternative)  
¿Cuánto vale?  
*KWAN-toh BAH-leh?*

**Please turn on the meter.**  
Encienda el taxímetro, por favor.  
*En-SYEN-dah el tahk-SEE-meh-troh, por fah-VOR.*

Say this the instant you get into a taxi. Colombian licensed taxis all have meters and are required to use them. That said: in Bogotá, an app (DiDi, InDrive, Uber, Cabify) is almost always safer than a street taxi. This phrase is the backup when you must take a street taxi elsewhere.

**Can I see the menu in Spanish, please?**  
¿Me muestra la carta en español, por favor?  
*Meh MWES-trah la KAR-ta en es-pah-NYOL, por fah-VOR?*

In Colombia, "menu" is *la carta* — the full list with prices. If the waiter has handed you an English menu with markups, asking for the Spanish one is the single most effective push-back.

**Does this include the service charge?**  
¿Está incluida la propina?  
*Es-TAH een-kloo-EE-dah la proh-PEE-nah?*

The Colombian service charge is called *propina voluntaria* (voluntary tip), typically 10 percent, and by law it is optional. Confirm whether it has been added, and decline if you prefer.

**I don't want the voluntary service charge.**  
Sin propina, por favor.  
*Seen proh-PEE-nah, por fah-VOR.*

Use this if the bill includes the voluntary tip and you have already decided not to pay it — service was poor, or you prefer to tip in cash directly to your server.

**Do you accept credit cards?**  
¿Aceptan tarjeta?  
*Ah-SEP-tahn tar-HEH-tah?*

Card acceptance is near-universal in Bogotá, Medellín, Cartagena, Cali, and all major tourist zones; some smaller coastal restaurants and Tayrona park-entrance vendors are cash-only.

**In dollars or pesos?**  
¿En dólares o en pesos?  
*En DOH-lah-res oh en PEH-sohs?*

Many Cartagena walled-city restaurants quote in US dollars at a tourist-unfriendly exchange rate. Ask for the peso price explicitly, and pay in pesos with a foreign credit card — Visa and Mastercard apply the interbank rate automatically, which will always beat the restaurant's internal "dollar rate."

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**  
No, gracias.  
*Noh, GRAH-syas.*

The single most useful phrase in this appendix. Said firmly, without slowing your pace. Walking away is not rude; it is Colombian-normal.

**I'm not interested.**  
No me interesa.  
*Noh meh een-teh-REH-sah.*

Stronger than *no, gracias*. Use this when a rose seller, a fake emerald dealer, or a street-tour tout keeps talking after the first refusal.

**Please leave me alone.**  
Déjeme en paz, por favor.  
*DEH-heh-meh en pahs, por fah-VOR.*

Literally "leave me in peace, please." Used firmly but without a raised voice. Colombian Spanish uses *déjeme* (formal *usted*) rather than the Argentine *déjame*.

**Don't touch me.**  
No me toque.  
*Noh meh TOH-keh.*

Say this clearly and step back. Most touts will disengage immediately rather than escalate. Again *usted* form: *no me toque*, not *no me toques*.

**Excuse me, let me through.**  
Con permiso.  
*Kohn per-MEE-soh.*

For clearing a path when a pickpocket team or a crowd of rose sellers is blocking you on Plaza Santo Domingo or outside the Gold Museum.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I'm calling the police.**  
Voy a llamar a la policía.  
*Boy ah yah-MAR ah la poh-lee-SEE-ah.*

This is the firmest card in the deck. Colombia's unified emergency number is **123**. Say the phrase, hold up your phone, and start dialing. Most scam operators back down the moment they hear *policía*.

**I'll call my bank.**  
Voy a llamar al banco.  
*Boy ah yah-MAR al BAHN-koh.*

Say this if a restaurant, taxi driver, or tour operator disputes a credit-card transaction. Calling your card issuer from the table signals that the charge is going to be disputed.

**I was scammed. Please help.**  
Me estafaron. Ayúdeme, por favor.  
*Meh es-tah-FAH-rohn. Ah-YOO-deh-meh, por fah-VOR.*

The word *estafaron* ("they scammed me," in the impersonal they-form) is the Colombian Spanish construction for "I was scammed." Use this when speaking to a *Policía de Turismo* officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, a fellow traveler, or any bystander you want to enlist. Colombian Spanish uses *ayúdeme* (formal *usted*) rather than the Argentine *ayudame*.

**Please give me an itemized bill.**  
La cuenta detallada, por favor.  
*La KWEN-ta deh-tah-YAH-dah, por fah-VOR.*

Asking for *la cuenta detallada* forces the establishment to account for every item, and it is the most polite version of "I do not accept this charge." A legitimate place will hand it to you without complaint. A tourist trap will stall.

**I didn't order this.**  
No pedí esto.  
*Noh peh-DEE ES-toh.*

Say this calmly about any unrequested dish, bottle, bread basket, or "service" that appears on your bill. In Colombia you are not legally required to pay for food or drinks you did not order — Ley 1480 (the Colombian consumer-protection statute) protects you here.

**This is not what we agreed.**  
Esto no es lo que acordamos.  
*ES-toh noh ess loh keh ah-kor-DAH-mohs.*

A short, firm phrase for taxi fares that exceed a quoted price, tour-operator price-creep, and rental "damage" disputes.

**These notes are counterfeit.**  
Estos billetes son falsos.  
*ES-tohs bee-YEH-tehs sohn FAL-sohs.*

If you receive a counterfeit peso bill at an exchange or as change, say this calmly to the cashier and request a replacement. A legitimate establishment will swap it immediately — counterfeit circulation is a problem they want to manage. A scam operator will stall.

**I will not give you my original passport.**  
No le doy mi pasaporte original.  
*Noh leh doy mee pah-sah-POR-teh oh-ree-hee-NAHL.*

This is the specific phrase for Cartagena and Santa Marta rental operators who demand your passport as deposit. Colombian law does not permit this; offer a photocopy instead. If they refuse the photocopy, walk away — they are not licensed.

## Getting help

**Where's the nearest police station?**  
¿Dónde está la estación de policía más cercana?  
*DOHN-deh es-TAH la es-tah-SYOHN deh poh-lee-SEE-ah mahs ser-KAH-nah?*

"CAI" (Comando de Atención Inmediata) is the informal name for the small neighborhood police post found every few blocks in Bogotá and Medellín.

**Where is the tourist police?**  
¿Dónde está la Policía de Turismo?  
*DOHN-deh es-TAH la poh-lee-SEE-ah deh too-REES-moh?*

Every major Colombian city has a dedicated *Policía de Turismo* unit. Bogotá's is at Carrera 13 No. 26–62, Medellín's at Carrera 66 No. 48–110, Cartagena's at Calle 32 No. 3–14 in the walled city. Full list in Appendix C.

**I'd like to file a report.**  
Quiero hacer una denuncia.  
*KYEH-roh ah-SER OO-nah deh-NOON-syah.*

The word *denuncia* is the Colombian Spanish term for a criminal report. You can file it in person at any Policía de Turismo office, or online at **denunciavirtual.policia.gov.co**. You will receive a printed or digitally-issued *denuncia* with a reference number — this is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

**Is there someone who speaks English?**  
¿Hay alguien que hable inglés?  
*Eye AHL-gyen keh AH-bleh een-GLESS?*

Colombian Policía de Turismo units in Bogotá, Cartagena, and Medellín have English-speaking officers. At smaller CAIs and in coastal tourist towns, English coverage is patchier; persistence helps.

**Please call 123.**  
Llame al 123, por favor.  
*YAH-meh al oo-noh-dohs-TRES, por fah-VOR.*

The unified emergency line **123** works on every mobile phone in Colombia — even without a SIM, even on a locked screen. Dispatchers speak intermittent English in Bogotá; Spanish is safer.

**Help! Thief!**  
¡Socorro! ¡Al ladrón!  
*Soh-KOH-rroh! Al lah-DROHN!*

*Ladrón* is the Colombian Spanish word for "thief." Shouted in a crowded market or street, this is understood by every Colombian bystander as "someone stole from me, help me stop them." Colombian crowds often do help — phone-snatches are sometimes recovered when the shout goes up fast.

**Please help me.**  
Ayúdeme, por favor.  
*Ah-YOO-deh-meh, por fah-VOR.*

## Reading Colombian peso amounts

Colombian peso prices are written with the symbol **\$** — yes, the same sign as US dollars; context disambiguates, and when US dollars are meant, Colombians usually write **USD**, **US\$**, or **dólares**. Decimal conventions are European: a comma is the decimal separator (\$3,50 = three pesos fifty centavos, which almost never matters because Colombia has no circulating coin smaller than 50 pesos), and a dot is the thousands separator (\$1.500 = 1,500 pesos, not one dollar and fifty cents). This is the opposite of English conventions and is the single biggest source of tourist confusion at checkout.

In 2026, the key denominations in circulation are:

- Coins: \$50, \$100, \$200, \$500, \$1,000 (most common; used for taxi fares and café tips).
- Notes: \$2,000, \$5,000, \$10,000, \$20,000, \$50,000, \$100,000 (the 100,000-peso note was introduced in 2016 and is the largest in circulation).
- One US dollar ≈ 3,500–4,200 Colombian pesos, depending on the month. The rate swings meaningfully with US interest-rate policy.

| Amount | Written | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 pesos | mil pesos | *meel PEH-sohs* |
| 5,000 pesos | cinco mil pesos | *SEEN-koh meel PEH-sohs* |
| 10,000 pesos | diez mil pesos | *dyess meel PEH-sohs* |
| 20,000 pesos | veinte mil pesos | *BEYN-teh meel PEH-sohs* |
| 50,000 pesos | cincuenta mil pesos | *seen-KWEN-tah meel PEH-sohs* |
| 100,000 pesos | cien mil pesos | *syen meel PEH-sohs* |
| 500,000 pesos | quinientos mil | *kee-NYEN-tohs meel* |
| One million | un millón | *oon mee-YOHN* |

If a vendor quotes a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it out:

**Please write the number down.**  
¿Me lo puede escribir, por favor?  
*Meh loh PWEH-deh es-kree-BEER, por fah-VOR?*

Almost every vendor will oblige, often on the back of a receipt or a paper napkin.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Colombia, polite firmness works better than raised voices — raising your voice reads as losing your composure and rarely helps your case. A calm *no, gracias* is already a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *déjeme en paz* ("leave me in peace") and *no me toque* ("don't touch me") are both firm without being rude, and *voy a llamar a la policía* ("I'm calling the police") is the firmest card in the deck.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar — and a calm *no, gracias, señor* with a *por favor* at the end is, in itself, proof you know enough Spanish that the interaction is going to be harder than they expected.
