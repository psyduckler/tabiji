Villa de Leyva is a 450-year-old colonial weekend town in the **Boyacá** department, three hours north of Bogotá by road, set on a high-altitude plain at 2,140 meters between the desert badlands of Sutamarchán and the cloud-forest **Santuario de Fauna y Flora Iguaque**. The pueblo's defining feature is **Plaza Mayor** — at 14,000 square meters, the largest cobblestoned plaza in the Americas, ringed by whitewashed colonial facades and the **Iglesia Parroquial** at its eastern end. The town is a national monument, motor-vehicle-restricted in the centro histórico, and one of the dominant weekend destinations for Bogotanos. Most international visitors arrive on a day trip or a weekend out of Bogotá; the closest airports are El Dorado (BOG) and Tunja's regional terminal. Public buses run from Bogotá's Terminal del Norte and Terminal Salitre for **COP 25,000–35,000 each way**, three to four hours each direction with the Tunja transfer.

Villa de Leyva's risks are almost entirely financial and concentrated in three windows: **the Bogotá → Villa de Leyva transport ramp**, **the Plaza Mayor restaurant strip**, and the **Festival de Luces y Cometas weekend** in early December. There is no significant violent-crime exposure to foreign visitors in the centro or on the standard day-trip routes; the *Policía de Turismo* presence on Plaza Mayor is constant during weekends, and the colonial grid is well-lit at night.

The dominant scam is the **Bogotá hostel "private driver" markup**. The legitimate Terminal del Norte bus to Villa de Leyva runs every 90 minutes via Tunja, with **Cooperativa Flota Valle de Tenza** and **Libertadores** the two main operators, at COP 25,000–35,000 each way. A Bogotá hostel tout pitches a "private driver" at COP 350,000–600,000 per person (round trip) on the basis that the bus is "unsafe," "closed," or "doesn't run on weekends" — none of which is true. The bus terminal at Villa de Leyva is a five-minute walk from Plaza Mayor; arriving by bus means you are in the centro within minutes of getting off. Defense: **book through the cooperative kiosk at Terminal del Norte** (Metro line A, Caribe station), or use a hotel-vetted shared van service that quotes the price up front in writing.

The Plaza Mayor restaurant strip runs the same **dual-menu USD/COP markup pattern** as Cartagena's walled city — many restaurants on the plaza keep two menus, one in Spanish at honest peso prices and one in English at inflated dollar prices that quote unfavorable internal exchange rates. The other restaurant pattern unique to Villa de Leyva is the **tourist-menu switch**: the dish brought to the table differs from the dish ordered (a smaller portion of *trucha* in place of the *cordero* you ordered, for example), with the price unchanged. Defense: ask for *la carta en pesos*, photograph the menu and the order before the food arrives, and pay with a foreign credit card. The legitimate price for a standard *trucha* lunch on Plaza Mayor is COP 30,000–50,000; an English-menu price quoting USD 35–50 for the same plate is the markup signal.

The third pattern is the **Festival de Luces y Cometas weekend** (the December lights and kites festival), the single highest-traffic weekend on the Boyacá calendar. Lodging prices on Booking and Airbnb during this weekend run 4–8x the off-season rate, but a separate scam pattern — coordinated through Facebook Marketplace, Instagram DMs, and a few Calle 12 "agencies" — sells "exclusive Festival de Luces packages" at COP 800,000–1,500,000 per night for properties that either do not exist or are not the property pictured. Defense: **book only through Booking.com, Airbnb, or directly through a hotel website with a verifiable physical address** — never via a Facebook Marketplace listing or a WhatsApp-only "agency." The Festival itself is a legitimate civic event running since 1985; the scam ecosystem just exploits the demand surge around it.

Beyond these three, Villa de Leyva runs a **fake-guide pattern at Iguaque and Pozos Azules** — men with knock-off lanyards selling "trail access" or "guide service" at the entrances to the *Santuario Iguaque* and the *Pozos Azules* turquoise lagoons. Defense: the Iguaque entrance fee (COP 30,000 for foreign adults) is paid only at the official Parques Nacionales booth at the trailhead, and Iguaque does not require a guide for the day-hike loop. Pozos Azules is a privately-operated nature reserve about 5 km from the centro with an entrance fee around COP 20,000 paid at the official gate. The **Calle Caliente bar strip** off Plaza Mayor runs a smaller-scale version of Bogotá's drink-spike pattern; the standard Colombian rule applies — order every drink yourself directly from the bartender and watch it poured.

Villa de Leyva rewards a few small habits. Take the Terminal del Norte cooperative bus, never a Bogotá hostel "private driver." Ask for *la carta en pesos* and photograph the menu before ordering. Book Festival de Luces lodging only through Booking, Airbnb, or a verified hotel. Pay Iguaque and Pozos Azules entrance fees only at the official gates. The pueblo is otherwise one of the gentlest weekends in Colombia.
