Bogotá is Colombia's capital, the Andean highland city at 2,640 meters that most international visitors reach through El Dorado International Airport (BOG) — one of Latin America's busiest by passenger volume, alongside Mexico City and São Paulo. The metropolitan area holds eight million residents. The *centro histórico* of La Candelaria, the Plaza de Bolívar, the Gold Museum, and Monserrate are the obvious tourist draws, while the northern neighborhoods — Zona T (also called Zona Rosa), Parque 93, and Usaquén — form the city's main nightlife corridor. Above all, Bogotá is the densest concentration of the country's single most dangerous scam category for foreign visitors: the *paseo millonario*, or "millionaire's ride."

A *paseo millonario* works like this. You hail a yellow taxi on the street — a completely ordinary-looking licensed cab, license plate visible, meter running. After a few minutes the driver stops to "pick up a cousin" or "grab a friend from around the corner." A second man climbs in and, within seconds, produces a short folding knife or a small-caliber handgun held at waist level. For the next four to six hours you are driven from ATM to ATM withdrawing your daily maximum, until your bank account's daily cap or the closing hour of 9 or 10 p.m. ends the cycle. You are then released in a semi-rural area and left to find your own way back. The 2025 US State Department Colombia country advisory specifically names Bogotá's *paseo millonario* as the leading cause of financial loss affecting American citizens in Colombia. Reports surged in the 2025 tourist season and have not abated.

The critical defensive habit, repeated across every major English-language travel forum since 2023, is this: **never hail a yellow taxi on the street in Bogotá, especially after dark**. Use **DiDi**, **InDrive**, **Uber** (in its legal-gray ride-in-front-seat mode), or **Cabify** exclusively. The apps provide transparent pricing, driver ratings, and a digital trace that makes *paseo millonario* effectively impossible — the drivers who take app rides are not the same population as the drivers who work the *paseo millonario* circuit. For airport arrivals, use the official posted-flat-rate taxi counter inside El Dorado's Terminal 1 arrivals hall, where license plates are recorded on the ticket and drivers are tracked.

Beyond the *paseo millonario*, Bogotá has a smaller set of tourist-financial scams. Street agents on Carrera Séptima pitch a "VIP Monserrate funicular package" that is invariably a USD 20–30 overcharge on a COP 27,000 ticket available at the funicular base. La Candelaria fake tour guides — men without Ministerio de Turismo-issued guide licenses — approach solo foreign walkers and propose a "walking tour" that ends at a friend's leather shop or emerald dealer. The **Emerald Trading Center** on Avenida Jiménez is a legitimate wholesale district, but it is also the epicenter of synthetic-emerald and dyed-quartz sales to tourists, invariably via a "private dealer" introduction. The Zona T bar district runs a scopolamine drink-spiking pattern identical to Medellín's Parque Lleras (see the Medellín chapter). And Bogotá's El Dorado airport runs the standard "flat fare" taxi-tout ecosystem in the arrivals hall — ignore it and walk to the official counter.

Bogotá rewards preparation. Use an app for every ride. Carry the minimum cash that fits in your front pocket — COP 200,000 is enough for a day. Leave your passport in the hotel safe and carry a photocopy. Photograph your taxi's license plate before you get in. Save the GAULA anti-kidnapping line **165** in your phone, along with the Sijín main line **+57 1 515-9111**. Most first-time visitors leave Bogotá having paid full tourist prices but without incident. The ones who lose serious money almost always share one decision: they hailed a yellow taxi on the street at night.
