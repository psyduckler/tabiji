# How to Use This Book {-}

Colombia is a traveler's paradise — Cartagena's walled city at dusk, Medellín's Metrocable gliding over the *paisa* hillsides, Cali's salsa clubs, San Andrés snorkel reefs, the Tayrona jungle-to-beach coast, and Bogotá's Gold Museum all within one passport stamp. It is also, after two decades of tourism growth, a destination where a single careless decision in the wrong bar can cost you everything in your wallet, the PIN to your ATM card, and a lost forty-eight hours of memory. Scopolamine — the Colombian plant-derived drug nicknamed *burundanga*, or "Devil's Breath" — turns drink-spiking into what the US State Department now calls "the most common method of robbery affecting US citizens" in Bogotá. A *paseo millonario* (millionaire's ride) is what Bogotá cab drivers and their ATM-stop accomplices call the six-hour kidnapping they run on tourists who hail a yellow taxi off the street. A significant share of Tinder matches in El Poblado are the first move in a group scopolamine setup. None of this is in the guidebooks.

We want you to come home with stories about the *arepas de chócolo* at a *paisa* grandmother's table, the waterfall hike to Cabo San Juan, a coffee cupping in the Eje Cafetero, and a salsa lesson that actually stuck — not about a \$3,000 ATM withdrawal made while drugged, a \$1,200 counterfeit emerald, or a *paseo millonario* that started with the wrong yellow taxi.

That is why this book is short and practical. It documents thirty-three specific scams that trip up first-time visitors to Colombia, and it tells you the exact moves that stop them. Nothing in it is theoretical. Every scam has been reported in the Colombian press (*El Tiempo*, *Semana*, *El Espectador*, *El Colombiano*), cross-referenced against Policía Nacional de Turismo records, the US State Department Colombia country advisory, and firsthand traveler accounts from Colombia-focused travel communities.

## How each scam is written

Every scam entry follows the same short format. First, the name of the scam, the category it falls into (nightlife, transport, overcharging, theft, and so on), and how serious and how common it is. Then two short sections: **How this scam works**, a plain narrative of how it unfolds in real time, with real peso and dollar amounts where we have them; and **How to avoid it**, the specific moves that shut it down. Each entry ends with where it happens and a four-panel comic showing the exchange, so you can spot the pattern visually, not just in words. The comics are drawn in the magical-realism watercolor tradition, a visual homage to Gabriel García Márquez's Macondo, in the warm tropical palette that runs from the Caribbean coast through the Andes.

## The single most important chapter

The next chapter, **The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns**, is the most important part of this book. Most scams in Colombia are variations on six patterns. Learn the patterns and you will spot a new scam the moment it starts — even one that is not in this book. Spend ten quiet minutes on that chapter before your trip. The rest of the book will then read as practical examples of patterns you already understand.

## The three bonuses at the back

After the city chapters, you will find three appendices that we hope you treasure more than the city chapters themselves.

The first is the **Colombian Spanish exit-phrase card**. Short phrases you can say, word for word, at the moments when your politeness would normally get you into trouble. Colombian Spanish is famously clear — Bogotá is often called the city with the clearest Spanish in Latin America — and the phrases here will carry even with an accent, as long as you say them firmly.

The second is the **recovery playbook**. A step-by-step flow of what to do in the first fifteen minutes, hour, day, and week after a scam. Most scam books tell you how to avoid scams. If the worst happens, you want this. It is especially important for scopolamine cases, which have a distinct medical-first flow.

The third is the **emergency contacts appendix**. Policía Nacional (123), Policía de Turismo, Bomberos, the CAI Turístico posts, Migración Colombia, embassies, and the nearest trauma-capable hospitals, organized so you can find what you need in seconds.

## A suggested way to use the book

A week or two before your trip, read the first two chapters and skim the three appendices. Read the chapters for the cities you will be visiting. Take a screenshot of the phrase card and the emergency contacts on your phone. If anything in this book turns out to apply during your trip, open it again in the taxi or in your hotel room and check what to do.

You will almost certainly not need most of it. That is the point.

*¡Hola! Buen viaje.* — hello, and safe travels.
