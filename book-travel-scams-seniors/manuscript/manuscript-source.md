# Travel Scams for Seniors

*A Clear, Calm Guide to Staying Safe, Confident, and Independent Abroad*

---

## Reader Note

This book is not here to make travel feel smaller. It is here to help you keep traveling with more confidence.

Most people you meet on a trip are ordinary people doing ordinary jobs. Most taxi drivers, hotel clerks, guides, shopkeepers, waiters, and fellow travelers are not trying to harm you. But travel does place you in situations where you are tired, carrying valuables, making quick decisions, and operating without the familiar signals you use at home.

Scams grow in that space.

The purpose of this guide is to slow the risky moments down. You will learn how common travel scams work, why they work, what to say, how to leave, and what to do if something has already happened. You will also learn how to prepare your documents, money, phone, medication, and emergency contacts before you leave home.

You do not need to become suspicious of everyone. You need a few repeatable habits.

## Important Safety and Accuracy Note

This book provides general travel-safety education. It is not legal, medical, financial, insurance, or law-enforcement advice. Rules vary by country and change over time. Before travel, verify destination-specific details through official sources, including your country’s travel advisory pages, the destination’s embassy or consulate, your airline or cruise line, your bank, your insurer, and your medical professional.

This edition is written primarily for U.S. travelers. If you are not a U.S. citizen or resident, use your own government’s travel advisory service, embassy or consulate network, health coverage rules, and fraud-reporting channels. When this book refers to the U.S. Department of State, the Smart Traveler Enrollment Program (STEP), Medicare, FTC, FBI, or U.S. embassy/consulate procedures, treat those as U.S.-specific resources.

For U.S. travelers, the U.S. Department of State publishes destination pages, travel advisories, passport guidance, emergency information, and the Smart Traveler Enrollment Program. Medicare usually does not cover health care outside the United States, with limited exceptions; verify your own coverage before travel. Medication legality also varies by destination and transit country.

All scenes and characters in this book are illustrative. Any resemblance to actual persons is coincidental.

# Part I — The Senior Traveler’s Scam Defense System

Part I builds the foundation for the rest of the book. Before we study taxis, ATMs, fake officials, hotel calls, medical emergencies, or a stranger reaching for your suitcase, we need a way to understand what is happening in the first place.

Scams do not work because travelers are foolish. They work because travelers are human.

## Chapter 1: Why Travelers Get Targeted—and Why It Is Not Your Fault

### A traveler at the edge of the square

Imagine this.

You have just arrived in a beautiful old city after an overnight flight. Your hotel room was not ready, so you left your bags with the front desk and walked toward the main square. The stones are uneven. The church bells are ringing. A waiter is setting out lunch menus. You are trying to remember whether your adult daughter said to use the credit card with no foreign transaction fee or the other one.

A smiling man steps beside you.

“Welcome! First time here?”

You smile back. Why not? He seems friendly.

He points to your shoulder. “Oh, you have something on your jacket.”

There is a smear of mustard-colored liquid on your sleeve. You are embarrassed. He produces a napkin. Another person appears with water. For ten seconds you are focused on the stain, the apology, the confusion, and the wish not to be rude.

Later, your wallet is gone.

This is not a story about stupidity. It is a story about attention. Your attention was moved, quite deliberately, from your pocket to your sleeve. The people involved did not need to overpower you. They only needed to create a small problem and act helpful while your normal judgment was occupied.

That is the central lesson of travel scams: the target is often not your intelligence. The target is your attention, your manners, your fatigue, your trust, your fear, or your desire to solve a problem quickly.

### Quick take: You are not the weak link

> **Quick Take**
>
> - Travelers get targeted because they are temporarily outside familiar routines.
> - Scammers look for moments of pressure, confusion, politeness, fatigue, and urgency.
> - Older travelers may be targeted because scammers assume they carry cash, value courtesy, or are reluctant to make a scene.
> - Being targeted is not evidence that you are careless.
> - Your best defense is a simple system you can use even when tired.

### Why travelers make attractive targets

Travel changes the ordinary rules of daily life. At home, you know which ATM you use, how much a taxi should cost, whether a person at your door belongs there, and which phone calls you ignore. On the road, even simple tasks can require judgment.

You may be asking yourself:

- Is this taxi official?
- Is this fee normal?
- Should I hand over my passport at the hotel desk?
- Is this person really a train employee?
- Do I tip here?
- Why is this ATM asking a question I do not recognize?
- Should I keep walking or respond politely?

None of these questions means you are unprepared. They are normal travel questions. But they create openings. A scammer’s job is to step into that uncertainty and offer an answer that benefits them.

Scammers also know that travelers often carry the exact items criminals want: passports, credit cards, phones, cash, prescription medications, and hotel key cards. A traveler’s phone may contain email, banking apps, airline apps, family contacts, medical information, and two-factor authentication messages. A passport can be used to cause serious administrative trouble even when it cannot easily be used as a complete identity on its own.

The U.S. Department of State encourages older travelers to plan carefully for health, documents, emergency contacts, accessibility, and destination conditions before international travel [^state-dept-age-65]. That guidance is not a warning that older adults should stay home. It is a recognition that good preparation protects the trip.

### Why older travelers may receive special attention

Many older adults are experienced travelers. Some have crossed oceans, raised families, run businesses, managed military moves, handled medical crises, negotiated contracts, or cared for aging parents. They are not beginners at life.

Still, scammers may make assumptions about older travelers. Those assumptions may be unfair, but they influence how criminals choose targets.

A scammer may assume an older traveler:

- carries more cash than younger travelers;
- has valuable jewelry or a better credit card;
- will be polite even when uncomfortable;
- may not want to appear confused;
- may be slower to move away from a crowded situation;
- may be traveling with medications or medical devices;
- may have adult children who can be frightened by an emergency call;
- may be less familiar with app-based payments, QR codes, rideshare systems, or digital ticketing;
- may hesitate before challenging someone who appears official.

These assumptions do not have to be true to create risk. A scammer only needs to believe they might be true.

That is why this book speaks to both older travelers and adult children. The goal is not for adult children to take over. The goal is for families to build a shared plan so that a scammer cannot use distance, embarrassment, or urgency to divide people from one another.

### The difference between vulnerability and blame

There is a painful word that often appears in conversations about scams: vulnerable.

Some people hear it as an insult. They hear, “You are weak.” That is not what this book means.

A person can be vulnerable in a particular situation without being weak as a person. A professional athlete is vulnerable when walking on an icy sidewalk. A surgeon is vulnerable when driving in an unfamiliar city with signs in another language. A retired judge is vulnerable when a fake hotel clerk calls at midnight and says the credit card machine lost the payment record.

Vulnerability is often situational. Travel creates situations.

You can be strong, wise, and experienced—and still be tired after a flight. You can be financially careful—and still be distracted when a stranger drops coins at your feet. You can be skeptical—and still feel alarm when someone says your grandchild has been injured abroad. You can be independent—and still need help reading a small ticket machine screen in bright sun.

Blame asks, “What is wrong with you?”

Safety asks, “What situation did the scammer create, and what system would interrupt it next time?”

We choose safety.

### Concrete scene: the helpful stranger at the ticket machine

You are at a busy train station. The ticket machine is in a language you do not read well. A line is forming behind you. You can feel people waiting. You are aware that your spouse is standing ten feet away with the luggage.

A young woman steps forward. “Here, I help. This machine is stupid.”

She taps quickly. The screen changes. She says, “Card.”

You insert your card. She reaches toward the keypad. “No, no, press here.”

This is the scam moment beginning. It may or may not be a scam. She may be kind. But the pattern is risky: an unexpected helper, a payment device, speed, embarrassment, and physical closeness.

Your defense is not to decide whether she is a good person. Your defense is to use a rule:

> “Thank you. I do not accept help at payment machines. I will ask staff.”

Then you step away, even if the line sighs behind you.

A good person will understand. A scammer will press harder.

### Red flags: when helpful becomes hazardous

> **Red Flags**
>
> Be cautious when a stranger:
>
> - moves close to you at an ATM, ticket machine, or payment terminal;
> - touches your bag, clothing, phone, wallet, or luggage without permission;
> - creates a mess, dropped item, sudden question, or commotion;
> - insists you must act now;
> - tells you not to ask hotel staff, police, airline staff, or family;
> - asks to see or hold your passport, card, phone, or cash outside a normal official setting;
> - offers help but becomes irritated when you decline;
> - claims a rule changed and only they can fix it.

### Scams exploit good qualities

Many scams work because they exploit virtues.

Politeness becomes a trap when you keep listening after your instincts say to leave.

Generosity becomes a trap when a staged charity appeal pressures you in public.

Responsibility becomes a trap when a fake official says you have broken a rule and must pay immediately.

Love becomes a trap when a caller says your child or grandchild is in danger.

Independence becomes a trap when embarrassment keeps you from calling someone for a second opinion.

Trust becomes a trap when a friendly stranger asks you to step away from the crowd.

The answer is not to stop being polite, generous, responsible, loving, independent, or trusting. The answer is to give those qualities boundaries.

Politeness with boundaries sounds like this:

- “No, thank you.”
- “I do not make payments on the street.”
- “I will speak with my hotel.”
- “Please step back.”
- “I need time to check.”
- “I do not discuss money by phone.”

These sentences are short on purpose. In pressure moments, long explanations invite debate. Short sentences create space.

### Scripts: respectful ways to refuse

> **Scripts**
>
> **At a payment machine:** “Thank you, but I don’t accept help when my card is out.”
>
> **With an insistent vendor:** “No, thank you. I’m leaving now.”
>
> **With a fake emergency call:** “I’m going to hang up and call the family number I already have.”
>
> **With someone claiming to be official:** “I will handle this at the police station, embassy, airline desk, or hotel front desk.”
>
> **With a pushy stranger:** “Step back, please.”
>
> **When embarrassed:** “I need a minute.”

Practice matters. These lines can feel rude the first time you say them. But you are not required to provide unlimited access to your attention.

### What “official” can look like

Many travelers are trained by life to respect uniforms, badges, desks, clipboards, and confident voices. That is usually good. But scammers understand symbols of authority. They may use reflective vests, fake IDs, lanyards, radios, laminated cards, or official-sounding language.

The question is not, “Does this person look official?”

The better question is, “Can I verify this through a channel I choose?”

If someone says they are from your hotel, call the front desk using the number in your booking confirmation or the hotel’s official website—not the number they give you. If someone says they are police and you are uncertain, ask to go to a marked police station or speak with your hotel. If someone says your tour has changed, contact the tour company through the app, email, or phone number you already had before the approach.

### Why “just be careful” is not enough

Families often tell older travelers, “Just be careful.” It is meant with love, but it is too vague to be useful.

Careful how? Careful when? Careful with whom? What should you do when a taxi driver says the hotel is closed? What if your phone is stolen? What if a caller says your son has been arrested? What if your medication is questioned at security? What if a stranger says you dropped money?

A useful safety plan is specific.

Instead of “be careful,” try:

- “Use the ATM inside a bank when possible.”
- “Keep one passport copy in your bag and one digital copy accessible.”
- “If anyone calls about a family emergency, hang up and call the family verification number.”
- “Do not hand your phone to strangers for photos unless you are comfortable losing sight of it.”
- “Enroll in STEP for international travel so the U.S. embassy or consulate can send safety information and help contact you in an emergency [^step].”
- “Check the State Department travel advisory for each destination before you go [^state-dept-travel-advisories].”

Specific rules reduce decision fatigue.

### Checklist: the traveler’s mindset

> **Checklist: Before you read further**
>
> - I accept that being targeted is not my fault.
> - I understand that scams often target attention, not intelligence.
> - I will not let embarrassment stop me from pausing.
> - I will use short refusal scripts.
> - I will verify through channels I choose.
> - I will talk with my travel partner or family about emergency procedures before leaving.
> - I will treat pressure as a warning sign.

### The first rule of Part I

The first rule is simple:

> **If someone is rushing you, your answer is no until you have time to verify.**

This rule will appear again and again because it works in many settings. It works with fake taxi dispatchers, fake hotel calls, fake police fines, fake emergency messages, fake ticket helpers, fake charity collectors, and fake bank alerts.

A real professional can usually wait while you verify. A scammer needs speed.

## Chapter 2: The Scam Moment: How Pressure Replaces Judgment

### The narrow hallway in the mind

A scam often begins with a narrowing.

One minute, you have choices. You can ask the hotel. You can call your daughter. You can step away. You can compare prices. You can look up the official rule. You can sleep on it.

Then something happens.

A man says the train is leaving now.

A woman says your card did not work and the hotel will cancel your room.

A caller says your grandson is crying and needs bail money.

A driver says the bridge is closed and only he knows the route.

A stranger says plainclothes police are checking tourists for counterfeit cash.

Suddenly your choices seem to shrink. The hallway gets narrow. You feel you must solve the problem immediately.

That is the scam moment.

### Quick take: pressure is the product

> **Quick Take**
>
> - The scam moment is the point when pressure tries to replace judgment.
> - Pressure can feel like urgency, fear, embarrassment, guilt, attraction, confusion, or authority.
> - Your goal is not to win an argument. Your goal is to create time and distance.
> - A pause is a safety tool.
> - Any demand for secrecy, speed, or immediate payment deserves verification.

### What pressure feels like in the body

Pressure is not only a thought. It is physical.

You may notice:

- warmth in your face;
- a tight chest;
- shallow breathing;
- a sudden wish to comply so the moment will end;
- trouble reading the screen in front of you;
- irritation at your travel partner;
- fear of holding up the line;
- embarrassment about not understanding;
- a strong urge to explain yourself;
- the feeling that there is no time.

These sensations do not mean you are failing. They mean your body has detected a problem. Use that signal.

When you feel pressure, say one of the safest sentences in travel:

> “I need a minute.”

Then take the minute.

### The four tools scammers use in the moment

Most travel scams use one or more of four tools: urgency, authority, distraction, and isolation.

#### 1. Urgency

Urgency says, “Now or else.”

- “The last taxi is leaving.”
- “Your reservation will be canceled.”
- “You must pay the fine immediately.”
- “Your account will be locked.”
- “Your family member needs help right now.”

Urgency is powerful because travel is full of real deadlines. Flights do close. Trains do leave. Hotels do have check-in rules. That is why urgency alone does not prove a scam. But urgency plus a demand for money, secrecy, personal information, or unusual action is a warning.

Your response:

> “If it is important, I need to verify it.”

#### 2. Authority

Authority says, “Do this because I am in charge.”

- fake police;
- fake station staff;
- fake hotel employees;
- fake airline agents;
- fake bank security calls;
- fake immigration or customs officials;
- unofficial “guides” who claim only they understand the rules.

Real authority has procedures. Real authority can usually be verified. Real authority does not need you to follow a stranger into an alley to pay a cash fine.

Your response:

> “I will handle this at the official desk.”

#### 3. Distraction

Distraction says, “Look over here.”

- a spill on your coat;
- a dropped wallet;
- a person bumping you;
- a loud argument nearby;
- someone asking for directions while another person reaches into your bag;
- a child crowding close while you are at a kiosk;
- a stranger offering to take your photo while another watches your purse.

Distraction scams often happen in groups. One person engages your eyes and emotions. Another handles the theft.

Your response:

> Stop moving. Secure your bag. Create space. Do not accept cleaning help from strangers. Move toward staff, a shop counter, or your hotel.

#### 4. Isolation

Isolation says, “Do not ask anyone else.”

- “Do not tell your daughter; she will worry.”
- “Do not call the hotel; they are part of the problem.”
- “Come with me where it is quieter.”
- “This special price is only for you.”
- “If you tell anyone, your grandson will be in more trouble.”

Isolation is one of the clearest warning signs. A legitimate process can survive a second opinion.

Your response:

> “I do not make decisions in private with strangers.”

### Concrete scene: the midnight hotel call

You are asleep in a hotel room after a long travel day. The room phone rings.

A calm voice says, “Good evening, this is the front desk. I’m sorry to bother you. Our payment system went down and lost several credit card authorizations. We need to re-enter your card to keep the room active.”

You are half awake. You do not want a billing problem. The voice knows your room number. The request seems plausible.

This is a classic pressure setup: fatigue, authority, urgency, and convenience. The solution is simple.

Say:

> “I’ll come to the desk in the morning.”

Hang up. If worried, call the front desk using the hotel phone button or go downstairs if safe. Do not read your card number to an incoming caller.

Notice that you do not need to accuse anyone. You only need to move the interaction to a channel you choose.

### The “maybe it is real” problem

The hardest scams are not ridiculous. They are plausible.

Maybe the hotel really did have a payment problem. Maybe the taxi stand really did move. Maybe the museum really is closed. Maybe the police really do need to see ID. Maybe your bank really did flag your card.

Safety does not require you to decide instantly whether the claim is real. Safety requires a verification habit.

Ask:

1. Did this contact begin through a channel I trust?
2. Is the person asking for money, documents, passwords, codes, or secrecy?
3. Are they rushing me?
4. Can I verify through an official source I choose?
5. What happens if I wait five minutes?

Scammers often make waiting feel impossible. That feeling is evidence.

### Red flags: pressure language

> **Red Flags**
>
> Listen for phrases like:
>
> - “You must do this now.”
> - “Do not hang up.”
> - “Do not tell anyone.”
> - “This is your only chance.”
> - “The police will come if you do not pay.”
> - “I am trying to help you, but you need to trust me.”
> - “Your family will be angry if they find out.”
> - “There is no time to check.”
> - “Give me the code that was just sent to your phone.”
> - “Cash only, right now.”

### The pause plan

A pause is not hesitation. It is a practiced maneuver.

Use this three-step pause plan:

1. **Name the pressure.** “This feels rushed.”
2. **Create distance.** Step back, hang up, close the door, or move toward a public counter.
3. **Verify independently.** Use a number, website, app, desk, or person you already trust.

For travel partners, agree on a phrase before the trip. It should sound ordinary but mean, “We need to stop and regroup.”

Examples:

- “Let’s check the map.”
- “I need my blue folder.”
- “Let’s take a water break.”
- “We promised to compare first.”

When one person says the phrase, both people stop the transaction. No debate in front of the stranger. You can discuss it privately after stepping away.

### Scripts: buying time without conflict

> **Scripts**
>
> **General pause:** “I need a minute to think.”
>
> **Payment demand:** “I do not pay until I verify.”
>
> **Phone pressure:** “I’m hanging up and calling back through the official number.”
>
> **Street approach:** “No, thank you. I’m continuing on.”
>
> **Authority pressure:** “I will go with you to the official desk, not anywhere else.”
>
> **Family emergency:** “I need to confirm with our family code.”
>
> **Travel partner intervention:** “Let’s take a water break.”

### When politeness keeps you in the trap

Many older travelers were raised to answer questions, respect officials, and avoid making a scene. These are fine habits in ordinary life. In a scam moment, however, a stranger may use conversation as a leash.

They ask where you are from. They ask whether this is your first visit. They ask where you are staying. They compliment your necklace. They tell you about their cousin in your hometown. They ask whether you remember them from the hotel breakfast.

Each answer gives them more material.

You are allowed to stop answering.

You can say:

> “I don’t share travel details.”

Or simply:

> “No, thank you.”

Then keep walking.

A person who means well may think you are reserved. That is acceptable. A person who means harm may be deprived of an opening. That is better.

### The role of fatigue, pain, and hunger

Travel days can lower anyone’s defenses. Long flights, time zone changes, dehydration, pain, blood sugar changes, medication schedules, hearing difficulty in noisy places, and poor sleep all affect decision-making.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention advises travelers with chronic illnesses to plan ahead, consider how travel may affect their condition, and prepare for health needs during the trip [^cdc-chronic-illness]. For scam prevention, this matters because physical stress can make pressure harder to resist.

If you know that fatigue or pain affects you, build your itinerary with protection:

- Avoid major financial decisions on arrival day.
- Pre-book transportation from the airport through a known provider when possible.
- Keep snacks and water accessible.
- Carry essential medications in your carry-on bag, not checked bags.
- Schedule rest after long travel segments.
- Use larger-print copies of key addresses and phone numbers.
- Ask your doctor or pharmacist how time zone changes may affect medication timing.

A rested traveler is not immune to scams. But a rested traveler has more room to pause.

### Adult children: do not weaponize fear

If you are an adult child reading this, you may feel anxious. You may want to send your parent a long list of warnings. You may be tempted to say, “This is why you should not go.”

Be careful. Fear can backfire.

If your parent feels judged, they may stop telling you about problems. If they feel monitored, they may resist reasonable planning. If they feel treated like a child, they may reject advice they would otherwise accept.

Try partnership instead:

- “I’m excited for you. Can we make an emergency plan so none of us panic?”
- “If anyone calls me claiming to be with you, what should I do to verify?”
- “What number should I use if your phone is lost?”
- “Would you like me to keep a passport copy in case you need it?”
- “Let’s choose a family code word for emergencies.”

The tone is not, “You cannot handle this.”

The tone is, “Scammers use confusion, so let’s remove confusion.”

### Checklist: your pressure interruption card

> **Checklist: Write this on a small card**
>
> **When I feel rushed:**
>
> - Stop.
> - Step back.
> - Secure bag and phone.
> - Say, “I need a minute.”
> - Do not pay, sign, scan, or share codes.
> - Verify through my own source.
> - Call my travel partner or family contact if needed.
>
> **My refusal line:** __________________________
>
> **My family verification number:** __________________________
>
> **My hotel front desk number:** __________________________

### The second rule of Part I

> **Pressure is not proof that something is important. Pressure is a reason to verify.**

This rule protects your dignity. You do not need to argue with a stranger about whether they are honest. You only need to refuse the rushed channel.

## Chapter 3: Your Travel Safety Setup Before You Leave Home

### The quiet work that saves the trip

The best scam defense often happens before the trip begins.

It happens at the kitchen table with a folder, a phone charger, a printed itinerary, and a cup of coffee. It happens when you make copies of your passport, enroll in a traveler program, check the advisory page, review your health coverage, and decide which credit card goes where.

This work is not glamorous. But it can save days of stress.

Preparation does not prevent every problem. It gives you fewer decisions to make under pressure.

### Quick take: set up your defenses at home

> **Quick Take**
>
> Before you leave:
>
> - Check official travel information for your destination [^state-dept-travel-advisories].
> - Enroll in STEP for international trips when appropriate [^step].
> - Make passport copies and store them separately [^state-dept-checklist].
> - Understand that Medicare usually does not cover health care outside the United States [^medicare-outside-u-s].
> - Check whether your medications are legal and properly documented at your destination [^state-dept-age-65].
> - Plan for chronic conditions, mobility needs, prescriptions, and medical records [^cdc-chronic-illness].
> - Create a communication and emergency plan with family.

### The travel safety folder

Create a travel safety folder before every significant trip. It can be a paper folder, a digital folder, or both. Many travelers prefer both because phones can be lost and paper can be misplaced.

Your folder should include:

- passport identification page copy;
- visa or entry authorization copy, if applicable;
- driver’s license copy, if you will drive;
- travel insurance policy and emergency number, if purchased;
- health insurance card copy;
- Medicare card copy, if relevant, with a note about limits abroad;
- prescriptions and medication list;
- doctor contact information;
- emergency contacts;
- hotel addresses and phone numbers;
- airline and cruise information;
- tour company contacts;
- embassy or consulate information for destination countries;
- credit card issuer phone numbers;
- bank contact information;
- family verification plan;
- copies of mobility device documentation if relevant.

The State Department’s travel checklist recommends making copies of important documents, including your passport, and leaving a copy with someone at home while carrying another separately from the original [^state-dept-checklist].

A copy is not a magic shield. It will not replace the passport at a border. But if the passport is lost or stolen, it can help you report the loss.

### Paper copies without creating a new risk

Copies help only if stored wisely. Do not keep every document together in one obvious envelope labeled “PASSPORT AND CREDIT CARDS.” If that folder disappears, the thief has a useful package.

A better system:

- Keep your actual passport in the safest practical location for the day.
- Keep one paper copy separate from the passport, such as in a different bag or hidden pocket.
- Leave one copy with a trusted person at home.
- Store a digital copy in a secure place you can access if your phone is lost, such as an encrypted cloud account, if you are comfortable using one.
- Avoid sending passport images through unsecured messages unless necessary.

If you are traveling with a partner, each person should know where their own documents are. One person can help the other, but no adult should be completely dependent on another person’s memory.

### STEP: a useful official channel

For international travel, U.S. citizens and nationals can consider enrolling in the Smart Traveler Enrollment Program, known as STEP. STEP allows travelers to receive information from the U.S. embassy about safety conditions in the destination and helps the embassy contact them in an emergency [^step].

STEP is a communication tool. If there is a natural disaster, civil unrest, security issue, or other emergency affecting your area, enrollment can make it easier to receive official information. For scam prevention, official channels reduce reliance on rumor.

### Travel advisories and country information

Before you go, check the State Department’s travel advisories and country information pages for each destination [^state-dept-travel-advisories]. These pages can include safety and security information, entry and exit requirements, local laws, health notes, and contact information for U.S. embassies and consulates.

Do not treat a travel advisory as a fortune-telling device. It cannot tell you exactly what will happen on your trip. Treat it as a planning tool.

Look for:

- areas to avoid;
- common crime concerns;
- transportation cautions;
- demonstration or civil unrest notes;
- entry requirements;
- local laws affecting medications, photography, alcohol, driving, or public behavior;
- emergency contact information;
- special considerations for older travelers or travelers with disabilities.

Country-specific details change. Emergency numbers, medication rules, visa rules, and local laws can change. That is why this book gives general habits and points you to official sources rather than listing rules that may become outdated.

### Health coverage: do not assume Medicare travels with you

Many Americans are surprised to learn that Medicare usually does not cover health care when you travel outside the United States, with limited exceptions [^medicare-outside-u-s].

This fact matters for both health and scam prevention.

If you become ill abroad and do not know your coverage, you may be vulnerable to panic decisions. You may accept the first expensive transport offer, share payment information with an unverified caller, or believe a stranger who says, “Your insurance requires cash now.”

Before you leave, ask:

- Does my health insurance cover care outside the United States?
- Does it pay directly, or must I pay and request reimbursement?
- Do I need travel medical insurance?
- Do I need medical evacuation coverage?
- What number do I call before or after receiving care?
- What documents should I keep for claims?

If you buy travel insurance, read the emergency contact procedure. Put the number in your phone and on paper. Share it with your travel partner and family contact. A scammer thrives when you do not know the process.

### Medications: legal at home does not mean legal everywhere

Medication planning deserves special attention. The State Department advises travelers to check whether medications are legal in the destination country and to travel with prescriptions and proper documentation [^state-dept-age-65]. Rules can vary, especially for controlled substances, sleep aids, pain medications, stimulants, and certain over-the-counter products.

Before travel:

- Make a complete medication list with generic and brand names.
- Keep medications in original labeled containers when possible.
- Carry prescriptions or a doctor’s note when appropriate.
- Check destination rules through official sources, especially for controlled medications.
- Pack medications in carry-on luggage, not checked luggage, for flights.
- Bring enough for the trip plus a small buffer if allowed and medically appropriate.
- Ask your clinician about time zone changes, storage temperature, and missed doses.

Medication uncertainty can become a scam opening. A fake official may claim your medicine is illegal and demand a cash fine. A stranger may offer to “replace” lost medication. An unverified online seller may promise quick delivery to your hotel.

If there is a medication problem abroad, use official channels: your doctor, travel insurer, embassy or consulate guidance, licensed pharmacies, and local medical providers recommended by reliable sources.

### Chronic illness and realistic planning

The CDC’s Yellow Book guidance for travelers with chronic illnesses emphasizes planning before travel, including assessing fitness for travel, preparing health documents, arranging medications, and considering destination medical resources [^cdc-chronic-illness].

This planning is not about limiting your life. It is about making the trip fit your body.

Consider:

- How far can you comfortably walk on uneven ground?
- What happens if an elevator is broken?
- Do you need refrigeration for medication?
- What is your plan if a device charger fails?
- How will you manage oxygen, CPAP, hearing aids, insulin, mobility devices, or other equipment?
- Does your itinerary include rest days?
- Will heat, altitude, humidity, or pollution affect your condition?
- Do you have a summary of your medical conditions and allergies?

Scammers notice distress. A traveler struggling with luggage, shortness of breath, confusion at a station, or pain in a long line may welcome help from the wrong person. The answer is not to refuse all help. The answer is to arrange better help in advance and know where to get official assistance.

### Money setup: separate, limit, and simplify

A good money setup assumes that something may go wrong. A card may be declined. An ATM may eat a card. A wallet may be stolen. A bank may flag a transaction. You may misplace a pouch.

Use separation:

- Carry one primary card for the day.
- Keep a backup card in a separate secure place.
- Carry limited daily cash.
- Keep emergency cash separate from spending cash.
- Avoid displaying large amounts of money in public.
- Use ATMs in secure locations, such as inside banks or monitored areas, when possible.
- Shield your PIN.
- Decline help at ATMs and payment machines.

Before you leave, call or check your bank’s travel procedures if needed. Some institutions no longer require travel notices, but you should know how they handle fraud alerts. Make sure you can receive alerts securely while abroad. If your phone plan will not work, set up another method before departure.

### Phone setup: your phone is a key

Your phone is not just a phone. It may be your map, translator, boarding pass, bank key, photo album, medical note, rideshare tool, email account, and connection to family.

Protect it accordingly.

Before travel:

- Use a strong passcode, not only face or fingerprint unlock.
- Turn on device tracking if you use it and understand how to access it.
- Back up important data.
- Update the operating system and critical apps.
- Remove apps or documents you do not need.
- Store emergency contacts in a visible or accessible way.
- Write down key numbers on paper in case the phone is gone.
- Consider a privacy screen if you will use the phone in crowded places.
- Use caution with public Wi-Fi, unknown QR codes, and shared charging stations.

A common travel mistake is to rely on the phone for every backup. If the phone is stolen, the backup disappears with it. Paper still has value.

### Passwords, codes, and banking alerts

Never share one-time codes sent to your phone or email with someone who contacts you unexpectedly. A caller may say they are from the bank, airline, hotel, or government and need the code to “verify” you. In many scams, that code is the key that lets them into your account.

Your rule:

> **Codes are for me to enter, not for me to read aloud.**

If you receive a fraud alert, do not click a link in a suspicious message. Open the bank app yourself or call the number on the back of your card. If the card is physically lost, use the issuer’s official number from your records.

### Luggage and document routines

Create a routine for where things live. Routines prevent panic.

Examples:

- Passport lives in the neck pouch or hotel safe, depending on the day’s needs.
- Phone goes in the zipped front pocket, never on a cafe table edge.
- Wallet goes in the inside jacket pocket or crossbody bag.
- Backup card stays in the suitcase lining or separate pouch.
- Medication stays in the carry-on, not the checked bag.
- Hotel address card stays in the day bag.

Do not create a system so complicated that you cannot follow it when tired. Simple beats clever.

### The arrival-day rule

Arrival day is high risk because you are tired and unfamiliar with the setting. Make arrival day boring on purpose.

Before you depart, decide:

- How will you get from the airport, port, or station to lodging?
- What will you do if the driver says the hotel is closed?
- What is the official address in local language if applicable?
- What is the hotel phone number?
- What is your backup transportation plan?
- How much local cash do you need before reaching the hotel?

Consider prearranged transportation through your hotel, tour operator, or a reputable provider, especially if you arrive late, have mobility concerns, or are carrying medical equipment. This is not always necessary, but it can reduce pressure.

Once you arrive, resist major changes proposed by strangers:

- “Your hotel burned down.”
- “That neighborhood is dangerous; I know a better place.”
- “The road is closed; the fare is double.”
- “The official taxi line is fake; come with me.”

Your response:

> “I will call the hotel.”

### Red flags: setup gaps scammers love

> **Red Flags**
>
> Before travel, watch for these weak spots:
>
> - No passport copy anywhere.
> - Only one credit card and no backup plan.
> - Adult children do not know the itinerary.
> - Traveler does not know whether health insurance works abroad.
> - Medications are packed in checked luggage only.
> - No paper record of important phone numbers.
> - Phone has no passcode or weak passcode.
> - Family has no verification plan for emergency calls.
> - Traveler plans to “figure out” airport transportation after a long overnight flight.

### Scripts: setting boundaries before the trip

> **Scripts**
>
> **To a bank:** “I’ll be traveling internationally. What is the safest way to respond if I receive a fraud alert?”
>
> **To a doctor or pharmacist:** “Can you help me prepare a medication list for travel, including generic names?”
>
> **To an adult child:** “I want you informed, not in charge. Let’s make a plan so neither of us panics.”
>
> **To a parent:** “I respect your independence. Can I keep a copy of your itinerary in case you ask for help?”
>
> **To a travel partner:** “If either of us says ‘water break,’ we stop the transaction and step away.”

### Checklist: before-you-leave safety setup

> **Checklist: Travel defense setup**
>
> **Official information**
> - [ ] Check State Department travel advisory and country information [^state-dept-travel-advisories].
> - [ ] Review entry, exit, and local law information.
> - [ ] Enroll in STEP if appropriate [^step].
>
> **Documents**
> - [ ] Passport valid for destination requirements.
> - [ ] Passport copy carried separately [^state-dept-checklist].
> - [ ] Copy left with trusted person.
> - [ ] Visas or entry authorizations copied.
>
> **Health**
> - [ ] Confirm health coverage abroad; remember Medicare usually does not cover foreign care [^medicare-outside-u-s].
> - [ ] Consider travel medical or evacuation coverage.
> - [ ] Prepare medication list and documentation.

> - [ ] Plan chronic illness needs [^cdc-chronic-illness].

### The third rule of Part I

> **Every important item needs a backup, and every urgent message needs verification.**

Preparation gives you calm choices. Calm choices are the enemy of scams.

## Chapter 4: The Family Planning Chapter

### Independence and connection can travel together

Family travel safety conversations can go wrong quickly.

An adult child says, “Mom, I read about scams. You have to be careful.”

Mom hears, “You think I am helpless.”

A father says, “I do not need anyone tracking me.”

His daughter hears, “If something happens, I will have no way to help.”

Both sides may be acting from love. Both sides may feel disrespected.

This chapter is about building a family plan that protects independence. The plan should not turn an older traveler into a child or an adult child into a supervisor. It should make the predictable parts easy so that everyone has more freedom.

### Quick take: a good family plan is not control

> **Quick Take**
>
> A good family travel plan:
>
> - supports the traveler’s independence;
> - gives adult children enough information to help if asked;
> - creates a verification system for emergency calls and messages;
> - avoids constant monitoring unless the traveler wants it;
> - includes health, document, money, and communication backups;
> - sets expectations before stress begins.

### The family meeting at the kitchen table

Hold one planning conversation before the trip. Do not wait until the night before departure, when everyone is tired and the suitcase is still open.

The tone should be practical:

> “Let’s assume nothing bad happens. But if something annoying happens—a lost phone, a missed train, a strange call—we will already know what to do.”

Bring:

- itinerary;
- lodging information;
- flight or cruise details;
- insurance information;
- medication list if the traveler is comfortable sharing it;
- emergency contacts;
- passport copy plan;
- phone plan;
- bank contact procedures;
- family code word.

If privacy is important, decide what information is shared and what is only available in an emergency. For example, a traveler may not want adult children seeing every medical detail but may agree to seal a medication list in an envelope labeled “Open only if I ask or if I cannot speak for myself.”

Respect matters. A plan built on respect is more likely to be used.

### The emergency code word

One of the simplest family defenses is a code word or verification question.

This is especially useful for impostor emergency scams. A caller may claim to be a grandchild, police officer, doctor, lawyer, hotel employee, or tour guide. They may say a loved one has been injured, arrested, robbed, or stranded. They may demand secrecy: “Do not tell Mom.” They may ask for wire transfers, gift cards, cryptocurrency, cash pickup, or card numbers.

The family rule:

> **No emergency money moves without verification through a known channel.**

Choose a code word that is easy for family to remember but hard for outsiders to guess. Do not use a pet name or birthplace that appears on social media. You can also use a verification question with an answer not posted publicly.

Examples:

- Code word: “Blue teapot.”
- Verification question: “What did we call the old station wagon?”
- Family phrase: “Aunt June’s pancakes.”

If a caller cannot verify, hang up and call the person directly using the number you already have. If you cannot reach them, call another trusted contact, the hotel, tour company, cruise line, or local authorities through official numbers.

### Scripts: emergency call responses

> **Scripts**
>
> **If someone claims to be a grandchild:** “I’m sorry you’re upset. What is the family code word?”
>
> **If someone says secrecy is required:** “Our family does not handle emergencies in secret.”
>
> **If someone demands money now:** “I will verify first. I do not send money during an incoming call.”
>
> **If the voice sounds real but feels wrong:** “I’m hanging up and calling you back.”
>
> **If a caller claims to be police or a hospital:** “Give me your name and department. I will call the official number.”

### What adult children should know

Adult children do not need to know every museum reservation or restaurant plan. But they should have enough information to help if something goes wrong.

At minimum, a trusted person should know:

- destination countries and cities;
- travel dates;
- lodging names and phone numbers;
- flight, cruise, or tour company information;
- traveler’s phone number and backup contact method;
- passport copy location or copy itself if traveler agrees;
- travel insurance emergency number if any;
- health limitations that would matter in an emergency;
- medication allergies if traveler agrees;
- family verification plan;
- what to do if they receive a suspicious call.

The State Department encourages travelers, including older travelers, to consider emergency planning, health needs, and contact arrangements before travel [^state-dept-age-65]. Family planning is part of that preparation.

### What travelers should ask from adult children

Older travelers also need commitments from family.

Ask adult children to agree:

- not to panic if they receive a strange call;
- not to send money without verification;
- not to post your detailed itinerary publicly on social media;
- not to assume silence means disaster if you miss one casual check-in;
- to keep passport copies and personal information secure;
- to answer if you use the emergency contact method;
- to respect the difference between help and control.

A traveler might say:

> “If I call and say I need help, I need you calm. If someone else calls about me, I need you skeptical.”

That sentence alone can prevent expensive mistakes.

### Check-ins without surveillance

Some families like daily check-ins. Others find them burdensome. Choose a rhythm that fits the traveler and the trip.

Options:

- text after arrival in each new city;
- quick evening message every other day;
- shared itinerary app;
- email summary after major travel days;
- phone call on Sundays;
- no routine check-ins, but emergency contact plan in place.

Avoid creating a system where one missed text triggers panic. Travel includes dead batteries, bad reception, long dinners, time zone confusion, and naps.

A better plan:

- “If I miss one check-in, wait twelve hours and try again.”
- “If I miss two planned check-ins and you cannot reach the hotel, then contact the tour company or local emergency channels.”
- “If you receive a demand for money, verify immediately through our plan.”

The goal is calm escalation.

### The adult child’s anxiety checklist

If you are the adult child, ask yourself:

- Am I giving information or trying to control?
- Have I asked what kind of help my parent actually wants?
- Am I treating ordinary travel risk as a reason they should not go?
- Have I shared practical tools instead of frightening stories?
- Do I know what to do if I receive a scam call?
- Can I stay calm long enough to verify?

Your parent’s travel may make you nervous. That does not mean the trip is wrong. It means you need a plan for your own response.

### The traveler’s pride checklist

If you are the traveler, ask yourself:

- Am I refusing a useful plan because I feel judged?
- Have I told my family how to help without taking over?
- Would I rather be slightly inconvenienced now or stranded without document copies later?
- Do I have a way to ask for help without embarrassment?
- Have I agreed not to send money or share codes under pressure?

Independence is not the same as secrecy. You can be independent and still have backup.

### Concrete scene: the grandson call

A woman in Ohio receives a call at 6:30 a.m. The voice says, “Grandma?”

It sounds like her grandson, but the line is muffled.

“Ethan?” she says.

“Yes, it’s me. Please don’t tell Mom. I’m in trouble in Spain. I was in a car with friends and there was an accident. The lawyer says I need money today or I’ll be held.”

A second voice comes on, serious and professional. “Ma’am, your grandson is frightened. We need to resolve this quietly.”

This is emotional pressure at full strength: love, fear, secrecy, urgency, and authority.

The family defense interrupts it.

She says, “What is the family code word?”

The caller hesitates. “Grandma, please, there’s no time.”

She hangs up. She calls Ethan’s phone. No answer. She calls Ethan’s mother. Ethan is asleep in his apartment.

The plan did not require her to be cold-hearted. It allowed her to protect him by refusing the fake emergency.

### Money rules for families

Family money rules should be explicit before travel.

Consider adopting these:

1. No wire transfers, gift cards, cryptocurrency, or cash pickups based only on an incoming call or message.
2. No emergency payment without speaking directly to the traveler through a known number or verifying through the code word and a second channel.
3. No sharing banking passwords, one-time codes, or card numbers with callers.
4. If real emergency funds are needed, use a method discussed in advance.
5. If a traveler is truly unreachable, contact lodging, tour operators, cruise staff, airlines, embassy or consulate resources, or local emergency services through official numbers.

These rules protect both generations. Scammers often aim at the person at home because that person is frightened and cannot see the traveler.

### Documents and privacy

Who should hold passport copies?

The answer depends on trust and family dynamics. A trusted adult child, sibling, friend, attorney, or travel companion can hold copies. The important point is that someone reliable can access them if needed.

If privacy is a concern, use sealed envelopes, password-protected digital storage, or a written instruction sheet that says when the information may be used.

For example:

> “My passport copy and insurance details are in this envelope. Please open only if I ask for help, if I am missing and cannot be reached, or if a medical emergency prevents me from speaking.”

That statement preserves dignity while enabling help.

### Health information boundaries

Some older travelers do not want adult children involved in medical details. That preference deserves respect. At the same time, certain information can be crucial if the traveler cannot speak.

A compromise:

- Share allergies and emergency contacts.
- Share the name of the primary doctor.
- Share insurance or travel medical policy numbers.
- Keep a full medication list sealed or digitally locked for emergency use.
- Carry a medical summary while traveling.

CDC guidance emphasizes planning for chronic illness and carrying relevant health information for travel [^cdc-chronic-illness]. The family plan can support that without turning private medical history into family gossip.

### Planning for lost phone, lost wallet, or lost passport

Families should discuss three common problems.

#### Lost phone

Before travel, decide:

- Can the traveler access email from another device?
- Does the traveler know key passwords or use a password manager they can access?
- Are important phone numbers written on paper?
- Who can help suspend service or track the phone?
- How will two-factor authentication work if the phone is gone?

#### Lost wallet

Decide:

- Which cards are being carried?
- Where are issuer phone numbers written?
- Is there a backup card separate from the wallet?
- Can a family member help identify which cards to cancel?
- Is emergency cash available?

#### Lost passport

Decide:

- Where is the passport copy?
- Who at home has a copy?
- Where is the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate information stored?
- How will the traveler prove identity and continue the trip?

The State Department advises travelers to make passport copies and keep copies separate from the original [^state-dept-checklist]. This is exactly the kind of simple preparation that turns a crisis into a process.

### Red flags: family-targeted scams

> **Red Flags**
>
> Be skeptical if a caller or message:
>
> - claims a loved one is in trouble but says not to tell anyone;
> - asks for money through gift cards, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or courier pickup;
> - refuses to let you call back through an official number;
> - says the traveler’s phone is broken but cannot answer verification questions;
> - creates panic with crying, background noise, or legal threats;
> - claims to be a lawyer, police officer, doctor, or embassy official but pressures immediate payment;
> - asks for one-time codes or account passwords;
> - tells you normal family verification will make things worse.

### Scripts: adult children speaking with parents

> **Scripts**
>
> **Opening the topic:** “I’m not trying to limit your trip. I want us to have a plan so scammers can’t rush either of us.”
>
> **Asking for itinerary:** “Could you send me the hotel names and dates? I’ll only use them if you need help.”
>
> **Discussing code word:** “If anyone calls me claiming you’re in trouble, I want a way to verify before I act.”
>
> **Talking about documents:** “Would you like me to keep a passport copy, or would you rather choose someone else?”
>
> **Respecting boundaries:** “Tell me what information you want private, and what I should have for emergencies.”

### Scripts: parents speaking with adult children

> **Scripts**
>
> **Setting tone:** “I appreciate your concern. I need help with backup, not permission.”
>
> **Sharing plans:** “Here is my itinerary. If I change hotels, I’ll send an update.”
>
> **Preventing panic:** “If I miss a casual text, don’t assume disaster. Follow our check-in plan.”
>
> **Emergency money:** “Do not send money to anyone claiming to be with me unless we verify through our code and known numbers.”
>
> **Privacy:** “I’ll share what you need for emergencies, but I want my medical details handled respectfully.”

### The family travel agreement

A short written agreement can prevent confusion. It does not need to be legalistic. It can be a one-page note.

Include:

- traveler name;
- trip dates;
- destinations;
- primary contact method;
- backup contact method;
- check-in rhythm;
- emergency code word;
- trusted contacts;
- document copy location;
- insurance emergency number;
- instructions for suspicious calls;
- permission boundaries.

Sample wording:

> “If anyone contacts family claiming I am in trouble, do not send money or share personal information until you verify through our code word and at least one known contact method. If I cannot be reached, contact my hotel, tour company, cruise line, or appropriate official resources using numbers from my itinerary or official websites.”

### Checklist: family planning before departure

> **Checklist: Family travel plan**
>
> **Communication**
> - [ ] We chose a check-in rhythm.
> - [ ] We agreed what counts as a missed check-in concern.
> - [ ] We have primary and backup contact methods.
>
> **Verification**
> - [ ] We chose a code word or verification question.
> - [ ] We agreed not to send emergency money without verification.
> - [ ] We know not to share one-time codes or passwords.
>
> **Documents**
> - [ ] Passport copy stored separately by traveler.
> - [ ] Passport copy held by trusted person or otherwise accessible.
> - [ ] Insurance and emergency numbers recorded.
>
> **Health**
> - [ ] Traveler has medication list and relevant health information.
> - [ ] Family knows what health information is available in an emergency.
> - [ ] Coverage abroad has been checked, including Medicare limits [^medicare-outside-u-s].
>
> **Respect**
> - [ ] Adult children understand the traveler is in charge of the trip.
> - [ ] Traveler understands family needs enough information to help.
> - [ ] Everyone agrees the plan is for backup, not control.

### When family relationships are complicated

Not every traveler has a close adult child. Not every family member is trustworthy. Some people are widowed, divorced, estranged, private, or simply prefer friends. The “family plan” can be made with anyone reliable.

Your trusted contact might be:

- a sibling;
- a niece or nephew;
- a close friend;
- a neighbor;
- a faith community contact;
- an attorney;
- a professional travel advisor;
- a tour leader for part of the trip.

Choose someone who is calm, reachable, and respectful of your wishes. Do not choose the person most likely to panic or take over.

### The fourth rule of Part I

> **A family plan should make it easier to say no to strangers and easier to ask trusted people for help.**

That is the balance. We do not want fear. We want connection. We do not want control. We want backup. We do not want shame. We want a shared language for pressure, verification, and calm action.

### Part I closing: the defense system in four rules

Part I has given you the foundation for the rest of this book. Specific scams, payment tools, criminal scripts, and travel rules will change. The defense system remains steady.

Remember the four rules:

1. **If someone is rushing you, your answer is no until you have time to verify.**
2. **Pressure is not proof that something is important. Pressure is a reason to verify.**
3. **Every important item needs a backup, and every urgent message needs verification.**
4. **A family plan should make it easier to say no to strangers and easier to ask trusted people for help.**

You do not need to become suspicious of the world to travel safely. You need habits strong enough to survive fatigue, confusion, kindness, and fear.

Carry copies. Check official sources. Plan for health needs. Understand your insurance. Protect your medications. Share enough information with trusted people. Practice your scripts.

Then go enjoy the trip. A defense system does not make travel smaller. It keeps travel open.

# Part II — Airports, Arrival, and First-Day Vulnerability

Now we move from preparation to the first vulnerable hours of a trip. Arrival is when bags are heavy, signs are unfamiliar, and the body is asking for food, water, and rest. The chapters in this part are built for that moment.

## Chapter 5: Airport Help Scams and Baggage Distractions

Ellen had rehearsed the arrival in her mind. Land, follow signs to baggage claim, collect the blue suitcase with the yellow ribbon, then meet the driver the hotel had recommended. Simple enough.

But the flight landed late. Her knees were stiff from the aisle seat. The arrivals hall was bright, echoing, and full of people holding signs. Her phone battery was down to 11 percent. Just as she reached for the handle of her suitcase, a man with a plastic badge on a lanyard stepped close.

“Hotel car?” he asked. “Your driver left. I take you now.”

Before Ellen could answer, his hand was on her bag.

That is the moment to practice for. Not because Ellen is careless. Because airports are built out of noise, fatigue, movement, and urgency. A stranger who sounds certain can make a tired traveler feel as if the decision has already been made.

> **Quick Take**
> - Arrival areas create pressure: noise, luggage, language barriers, bathroom needs, phone battery worries, and the desire to be done.
> - A badge, vest, clipboard, or confident voice is not proof that someone is official.
> - Keep physical control of your bags until you verify the person, counter, vehicle, or company.
> - If you are unsure, move toward an airline counter, airport information desk, uniformed airport employee, or official taxi/rideshare area.
> - You do not owe speed to a stranger. “I need to verify this first” is a complete sentence.

### Why airports create scam openings

An airport arrival is one of the few times a traveler may be carrying everything important at once: passport, wallet, phone, medication, glasses, itinerary, and the first hotel address. You may also be managing a cane, walker, CPAP case, jacket, or a companion who walks faster or slower than you do.

Scammers and aggressive touts look for overload. They watch for travelers who pause under signs, fumble with phones, or seem too polite to say no. The approach is often friendly at first. “Madam, this way.” “Sir, I help with bags.” “Hotel shuttle changed.” “Taxi official.” “Your driver sent me.”

The trick is not always a dramatic theft. Sometimes it is a costly unauthorized ride. Sometimes it is a “porter” who demands a large tip after carrying a bag twenty feet. Sometimes one person distracts you while another slides away with a backpack. Sometimes a fake helper steers you out of the protected terminal into a place where it is harder to ask questions.

A good airport rule is this: **you choose the helper; the helper does not choose you.**

### Red flags in the arrivals hall

Be careful when:

- someone approaches you before you approach an official desk;
- a stranger reaches for your suitcase, wheelchair, phone, or passport without permission;
- you are told your booked driver, shuttle, or taxi option is canceled but no official source confirms it;
- the person rushes you away from the terminal, taxi stand, or information desk;
- the “employee” cannot point to a staffed counter, company sign, reservation record, or official pickup zone;
- payment is requested before you see the vehicle or before the service is clear;
- you are told not to ask airport staff, hotel staff, or police;
- a second person suddenly appears to “help” while the first keeps you talking.

A lanyard alone means very little. At many airports, legitimate staff wear uniforms and identification, but visitors, drivers, tour greeters, and unofficial touts may also wear badges or carry clipboards. The safer question is not “Does this person look official?” It is “Can I verify this through an official channel?”

### What to do in the first 30 seconds

When someone approaches and you feel pressured, do not debate. Reset the scene.

1. **Stop walking.** Plant your feet and take one breath.
2. **Put a hand on your bag handle.** Keep your small valuables bag on your body.
3. **Step sideways, not forward.** Move out of the flow of traffic but do not follow the person.
4. **Use one calm sentence.** “I need to verify this first.”
5. **Turn toward a staffed point.** Look for information, airline, police, customs exit staff, official taxi desk, or the rideshare pickup instructions in your app.
6. **Call the known number.** If it involves your hotel or tour company, use the number from your booking confirmation, not a number the stranger provides.

If you use hearing aids or the airport noise makes speech hard to understand, you can say, “Please write that down.” A legitimate employee can slow the conversation. A scammer usually wants momentum.

### Safe response: keep the bag, verify the story

Marisol, seventy-six, arrived in a city she had visited years earlier. A young man near baggage claim said, “Porter service. I bring bags to taxi.” He smiled and pointed toward the exit. Her first impulse was gratitude; the suitcase felt heavier than it had at home.

Then she remembered her rule: choose the helper. She kept her hand on the suitcase and said, “Thank you. I’m going to the information desk first.” He shrugged and moved on to another traveler.

At the desk, she learned that official porters wore numbered uniforms and worked from a marked area near the exit. She hired one there, paid the posted fee, and kept her passport pouch under her sweater.

The goal is not to refuse all help. Help can be necessary and welcome. The goal is to accept help from a verified source, on terms you understand.

### Script ladder: from polite to firm

Start soft if the situation allows. Move firmer if the person keeps pressing.

- **Polite:** “No, thank you. I have a plan.”
- **Verifying:** “I need to check with the airport desk before I go anywhere.”
- **Boundary:** “Please do not touch my bag.”
- **Repeat:** “No. I am not going with you.”
- **Public help:** “Officer, could you help me verify my transportation?”

You do not need to explain that you are tired, widowed, traveling alone, hard of hearing, or unfamiliar with the airport. Too much explanation gives a manipulator more material to work with. Short sentences are stronger.

### Baggage distractions

Baggage distractions often happen in pairs. One person asks a question, drops coins, spills coffee, points to something on your coat, or says your bag is leaking. While your attention moves, another person moves a suitcase, backpack, purse, or phone.

A common travel risk for older travelers is the “helpful lift.” Someone offers to lift a suitcase onto a cart, into a shuttle, or over a curb. The bag disappears, or the helper demands money to give it back. Another version involves a stranger offering to watch your bag while you use the restroom. Do not accept. If you must separate from luggage, use a staffed baggage service, airline counter, hotel desk, or travel companion you know.

Keep the irreplaceable items on your body: passport, one payment card, essential medication, phone, glasses, and a printed hotel address. A rolling suitcase full of clothing is inconvenient to lose. A passport and medication kit can derail the trip.

Consider a simple “arrival uniform”: crossbody bag zipped in front; passport pouch under clothing; phone in a zipped pocket; suitcase handle in hand.

### Recovery steps if something goes wrong

First, get to a staffed, public place. The middle of the arrivals hall is not where you solve the whole problem. Go to an airline counter, airport information desk, airport police office, official taxi desk, or your hotel if you can safely reach it.

Then take the next steps in order:

1. **Protect yourself and your companion.** Sit down if you feel shaky. Ask staff for help.
2. **Cancel or lock payment cards.** Use your bank’s app or the number on the back of another card. If your phone is gone, ask the airline desk, hotel desk, or a trusted companion for help reaching the bank.
3. **Report stolen passport or documents.** For U.S. citizens abroad, the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate can help replace a lost or stolen passport and provide information for crime victims. It cannot investigate like local police, replace all expenses, or guarantee reimbursement. [^state-dept-lost-passport-crime]
4. **File a local report if needed.** Insurance, airlines, embassies, and banks may ask for a police or airport security report.
5. **Document details while fresh.** Location, time, description, vehicle plate, badge wording, and what was taken.
6. **Tell your hotel or tour operator.** They may recognize a pattern and help with safer transportation.

If the loss includes a phone, use another device to change passwords for email, banking, and travel apps. If you prepared a printed emergency sheet, this is when it earns its place.

### Supporter note

If you are helping an older traveler prepare, do not make the airport plan a test of independence. Make it a fatigue plan. Print the arrival details in large type: flight number, terminal, hotel address, verified phone number, official transport plan, and “If confused, go to information desk.” Include a small phrase card if language may be an issue.

A short role-play can help: “What will you say if someone says the shuttle is canceled?” The answer does not need to be perfect. It just needs to be practiced.

### Chapter takeaway

At the airport, the safest traveler is not the fastest traveler. Keep your bag. Slow the conversation. Verify through a staffed or official source. Accept help you choose, not help that corners you.

## Chapter 6: Taxi, Rideshare, and Shuttle Scams

The taxi line looked long, and Thomas was tired enough to feel it behind his eyes. His suitcase wheel had started squeaking on the airport floor. Outside the sliding doors, drivers held signs and phones and waved people toward the curb.

A man near the exit lifted one hand. “Taxi? Same price. No wait.”

The car looked clean. The driver smiled. The trunk was already open. Thomas almost handed over his bag before he realized he had verified nothing: not whether this was an authorized airport taxi, not the fare, not the meter, not the route, not even whether the man was a taxi driver at all.

Transportation is one of the first trust decisions of a trip. Most drivers are simply working. The goal is not to fear every ride; it is to know what must match before you and your luggage are inside a moving vehicle.

> **Quick Take**
> - Use official taxi stands, hotel-arranged cars, licensed shuttle counters, or app-confirmed rideshare pickups.
> - For rideshare, match the license plate, car make/model, driver name/photo, and destination before entering. [^uber-lyft-safety]
> - Confirm fare rules before the car moves: fixed fare, meter, card payment, tolls, airport fees, and luggage fees where applicable.
> - Keep documents, medication, phone, and one payment card with you, not in the trunk.
> - If a ride feels wrong, aim for a safe exit in a public place rather than winning an argument.

### How transportation scams work

Transportation scams rely on the same feeling: “You need to decide right now.” The setting may be an airport curb, train station, cruise port, hotel entrance, or crowded cafe district.

Common patterns include:

- an unofficial driver approaching inside arrivals and claiming to be “same as taxi”;
- a fake rideshare driver calling out “Uber?” or “Lyft?” until someone responds;
- a driver who guesses your name after overhearing it;
- a taxi with a “broken” meter in a place where meters are normal;
- a long route that quietly increases the fare;
- a cash-only demand after card payment was promised;
- surprise charges for bags, airport access, tolls, night service, or extra passengers;
- a shuttle that claims your hotel transfer was canceled;
- a luggage-hostage moment, where the driver will not unload bags until more cash is paid.

Older travelers can be pushed harder because a heavy bag, sore hip, rainstorm, or long walk back to the terminal makes it harder to say, “No, I’ll start over.”

### What to do in the first 30 seconds

Before you enter any taxi, rideshare, or shuttle, pause at the curb and do this:

1. **Keep your small bag on your body.** Passport, phone, medication, glasses, and one card stay with you.
2. **Verify before loading luggage.** Once a suitcase is in the trunk, the driver has leverage.
3. **Check the ride source.** Did you request this through an app, official stand, hotel desk, licensed counter, or known company?
4. **Match details.** For rideshare, compare plate, car, driver, and destination. For taxis, look for official markings, license, meter or fare chart, and the correct queue.
5. **Confirm the fare rule out loud.** “Is this fixed fare or metered? Can I pay by card?”
6. **If pressured, step back.** “I’m not ready. I need to verify first.”

Do not let embarrassment rush you. It is normal to check. Legitimate drivers see travelers verify details all day.

### Rideshare: match, then enter

Rideshare pickup zones can be confusing. Airports may require a designated garage level or app area. Hotels may have several entrances. In the confusion, fake drivers look for people staring at phones.

Do not ask, “Are you here for Margaret?” A dishonest person can say yes. Better options are:

- silently match the license plate in the app;
- compare the car make and model;
- compare the driver name and photo;
- ask, “What name is on the ride?” if you need a verbal check;
- confirm that the destination shown in the app is yours.

If the plate is different, do not get in because the driver says, “I changed cars,” “the app is wrong,” or “get in quickly.” Cancel through the app if needed and request again from a safer spot. If you are using rideshare in the U.S., app safety tools may include trip sharing, emergency assistance links, PIN verification in some markets, and in-app reporting. Availability varies, so learn the app before the trip. [^uber-lyft-safety]

A quiet micro-rule helps: **door closed only after details match.** Keep the door open and your feet outside until you are satisfied.

### Taxis: local rules matter

Taxi systems vary widely. Some cities use meters. Some airports use fixed-fare vouchers. Some require prepayment at an official counter. Some have color-coded licensed taxis. Some add tolls or airport surcharges that are legitimate if disclosed.

Before you travel, look up the official airport transportation page or ask your hotel what the normal process is. At the curb, ask simple questions before bags go in:

- “Is this the official taxi line?”
- “Is the fare fixed or metered?”
- “About how much to this address?”
- “Can I pay by card?”
- “Please turn on the meter,” where meters are standard.
- “May I have a receipt?”

If the answers keep changing, step away. If the driver says the meter is broken, the card machine is broken, and the posted fare is wrong, that is too many broken things for one ride.

### Shuttles and private transfers

Hotel shuttles and private transfers are comforting because they sound arranged. That comfort is what impostors borrow.

Priya landed after visiting her grandchildren and saw a man holding a sign with the first three letters of her hotel’s name. “Shuttle moved,” he said. “Come now.” She almost followed, then noticed he did not know her surname. She called the hotel from her confirmation email. The real shuttle was outside Door 6 with her full name on its list.

For hotel shuttles, ask in advance: pickup location, shuttle markings, whether a reservation is needed, and whether the driver will know your name. For private transfers, carry the company name, driver name if provided, vehicle description, and a phone number from the original booking.

### Safe response if the ride changes

Sometimes the ride begins normally and then changes. The driver takes a strange route, demands a different payment method, becomes angry, or says your hotel is closed and offers another one. Your priority is safety, not proving the map is right.

Use calm instructions:

- “Please stop at this hotel entrance.”
- “Please let me out at that open cafe.”
- “I need to end the ride here.”
- “I will handle payment through the app.”
- “Please unload my bag now.”

If you are in an app ride, use in-app safety or support tools if available. If you feel in danger, contact local emergency services when it is safe to do so. In the U.S., 911 is the emergency number; abroad, emergency numbers vary, so note them before travel.

Avoid threats inside a moving vehicle. Do not grab the steering wheel, shout insults, or announce that you will “ruin” the driver. Get to a public place first.

### Script ladder: transportation boundaries

- **Polite:** “No, thank you. I’m using the official line.”
- **Verifying:** “I need to check the plate and driver details before I get in.”
- **Fare clarity:** “Before we leave, is this metered or fixed fare? Can I pay by card?”
- **Boundary:** “Please take my bag out of the trunk. I’m not taking this ride.”
- **Exit request:** “Please stop at that hotel/cafe. I am getting out here.”
- **Public help:** “Can you call airport security? I need help with a driver dispute.”

Practice the bag sentence. Many travelers find it harder to demand their luggage than to decline a ride. Say it plainly: “Please remove my suitcase now.”

### Recovery steps after a bad ride

Once you are safe:

1. **Write down the details.** Driver name, company, plate number, taxi medallion or license, pickup point, route, amount paid, and time.
2. **Save receipts and screenshots.** Do not edit them; store copies.
3. **Report through the app or taxi authority.** Rideshare companies handle complaints through the app. Taxi complaints may go to an airport, city taxi commission, hotel concierge, or local consumer office.
4. **Dispute improper card charges.** Contact your card issuer promptly. In the U.S., card protections depend on the type of transaction and timing, so report quickly and keep documentation. [^cfpb-card-dispute]
5. **Report theft or threats locally.** If luggage, documents, or money were taken, make a police or airport security report.
6. **Warn your lodging.** Hotels may help future guests avoid the same curb or impostor shuttle.

If you paid cash under pressure, recovery may be difficult. Still document it. A pattern of reports can help airports, hotels, and local authorities respond.

### Supporter note

If a parent or older friend is traveling, help set up the transportation plan before departure. Save the hotel address in the rideshare app, print it in local language if relevant, and mark the official taxi or rideshare pickup location. If apps are stressful, consider arranging a hotel car and printing the verification details.

Support without shaming. “Call me from the curb if anything feels off” is better than “Don’t get scammed.” A calm backup person helps them pause.

### Chapter takeaway

Do not climb into uncertainty. Verify the ride before the bag goes in, confirm the fare before the wheels move, and exit in public if the situation changes. A good driver can wait thirty seconds while you check.

## Chapter 7: The First 24 Hours: Jet Lag, Cash, and Confusion

Many scams do not need a clever script. They need a tired traveler.

On her first evening in Lisbon, Ruth planned to unpack, wash her face, and eat soup near the hotel. Instead, the room was not ready, her phone would not connect to Wi-Fi, and she had slept only two hours on the plane. A cheerful man in the lobby overheard her asking about an ATM.

“Bank machine outside is bad,” he said. “I show you better exchange. Very good rate.”

Ruth was not greedy. She was hungry, embarrassed, and tired of solving problems. That is when arrival-day mistakes happen: too much cash from a street ATM, a fake hotel Wi-Fi login, a confusing cafe bill, or a “new friend” who wants to move somewhere quieter.

The first 24 hours deserve a safety plan.

> **Quick Take**
> - Treat arrival day as a low-decision day, not a sightseeing challenge.
> - Do not make major purchases, complicated banking decisions, or private side trips when tired.
> - Use your hotel or other staffed lodging as a verification base.
> - Eat, hydrate, rest, and reset before solving anything expensive or confusing.
> - “Tomorrow” is a safety tool. Scammers press for today.

### The first-24-hours rule

For the first 24 hours, avoid decisions that are expensive, rushed, private, or hard to undo.

That means no jewelry, rug, art, electronics, or timeshare purchases. No unofficial tours that leave immediately. No street currency exchange unless verified. No complicated banking at an outdoor ATM while juggling luggage. No signing forms you do not understand. No handing over your passport unless the requirement is legitimate under local rules.

This may sound cautious, but it is not meant to shrink your trip. It protects the trip. A quiet first night can save the next ten days.

A useful phrase is: **“I don’t buy on arrival day.”** You can say it to a salesperson, a friendly stranger, or yourself.

### What to do in the first 30 seconds

When you feel foggy, rushed, or cornered on the first day, use a reset routine:

1. **Stop adding decisions.** Do not agree to the ride, tour, purchase, exchange, or “quick stop” yet.
2. **Name the problem.** “I am tired. I need to verify this later.”
3. **Move to a staffed place.** Hotel desk, bank branch, museum counter, restaurant host stand, or official visitor information.
4. **Use the known contact.** Call the hotel, tour operator, bank, or card issuer using a saved number or official app.
5. **Delay money.** If payment is not urgent, wait until after food and rest.
6. **Tell one person.** Message a trusted contact: “Arrived. Tired. I’m postponing decisions until morning.”

Jet lag can feel like poor judgment because it temporarily is. You may be smart, experienced, and still operating at half speed. Build the plan for the half-speed version of yourself.

### Build a deliberately boring first-day plan

A strong arrival plan is not glamorous:

1. Reach lodging safely.
2. Check in or leave bags with the official desk.
3. Put passport, backup card, and extra cash in a secure place.
4. Eat something simple.
5. Confirm tomorrow’s plan.
6. Charge the phone.
7. Sleep.

If your room is not ready, do not wander the city with all valuables because you feel awkward. Ask the front desk to store luggage. Keep irreplaceable items on your body. Sit in the lobby or a nearby cafe recommended by the hotel.

Book the first night for ease: 24-hour front desk if possible, elevator if needed, simple food nearby, and clear transportation from the airport or station. A remote inn may be wonderful on night three. On night one, boring can be beautiful.

### Cash, cards, and ATMs on day one

Arrival cash decisions are fertile ground for mistakes. You may not yet know the currency, normal prices, bank locations, or whether a machine is legitimate. A stranger who “helps” at an ATM may be watching your PIN, switching cards, or steering you to a machine with poor terms.

Safer practices:

- use a bank ATM inside or attached to a bank when possible;
- avoid outdoor ATMs late at night or in crowded tourist corridors;
- cover your PIN with your hand;
- decline help from strangers at the machine;
- take only the amount you reasonably need before you can reassess;
- separate cards so one lost wallet does not end the trip;
- keep emergency cash in a separate place.

If a machine keeps your card, do not accept help from a bystander who says they can retrieve it. Go into the bank if it is open, or call the card issuer using the number in your banking app or on another card. If the card is gone or suspiciously retained, lock it if your bank allows and monitor activity.

For U.S. travelers, bank and card protections vary by debit versus credit card and by how quickly you report a problem. The practical advice is simple: report unauthorized transactions promptly and keep records. [^ftc-lost-cards]

### Wi-Fi, phones, and fake login pages

The first day is also when travelers rush onto Wi-Fi. You want to message family, download maps, and check the hotel booking. In that hurry, a fake network name can look close enough: “GrandHotel_Free” instead of the hotel’s actual network, or a cafe login page that asks for email credentials, card numbers, or app passwords.

Ask the front desk or cafe staff for the exact Wi-Fi network name and password. Be cautious with public Wi-Fi pages that request more than basic access information. Avoid banking or entering sensitive passwords on public networks unless you are using protections you understand, such as cellular service or a reputable VPN. Update your phone and travel apps before leaving home.

If a pop-up says your device is infected and you must call a number, do not call. Close the page. If worried, ask a trusted tech helper later. Scare screens can lead to support scams.

### Confusion scams: “closed,” “moved,” and “come with me”

A first-day traveler is vulnerable to confident misinformation.

“The museum is closed.”

“Your hotel is in a bad area; I know a better one.”

“That cafe overcharged you; come to my cousin’s place.”

“The ticket office moved.”

Some statements may be true. Many are not. Verify without following. Check the official website, call the venue, ask the hotel desk, or ask a uniformed employee. Do not walk away with the person who benefits from your confusion.

At a cafe, if a bill looks wrong, ask the server to explain it before paying. If there is a cover charge, service charge, bread charge, or tourist menu confusion, stay calm. Not every surprise is a scam. But do not let a stranger outside the cafe “solve” the bill by taking you to an ATM.

### Script ladder: arrival-day delays

- **To a seller:** “I don’t make purchases on arrival day. I’ll come back tomorrow if I’m interested.”
- **To a stranger offering help:** “Thank you, but I’m checking with my hotel first.”
- **At an ATM:** “No help, thank you. Please step back.”
- **At a cafe or shop:** “Please show me the itemized bill.”
- **For Wi-Fi:** “What is the exact network name?”
- **To yourself:** “Food, water, charge phone, then decide.”

The self-script matters. Scams often enter through self-pressure: “I should be able to handle this,” “I don’t want to seem rude,” “I already wasted the afternoon.” Replace that with: “I am protecting tomorrow.”

### Recovery steps if the first day goes sideways

If you made a purchase, shared information, used a suspicious ATM, or followed someone you now distrust, pause and triage.

1. **Get to safety.** Return to your lodging, a staffed business, or a public official location.
2. **Secure payment methods.** Lock or cancel cards if needed. Review recent transactions.
3. **Change passwords if you entered them on a suspicious Wi-Fi page.** Start with email, banking, and travel accounts. Use a trusted network.
4. **Contact your bank or card issuer promptly.** Ask how to dispute unauthorized charges and what documentation they need.
5. **Report identity theft if personal information was misused.** For U.S. travelers, IdentityTheft.gov is the Federal Trade Commission’s recovery planning site; outside the U.S., use your local consumer protection or police reporting channels. [^ftc-identitytheft-gov]
6. **Report passport theft or crime.** U.S. citizens abroad can contact the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate for passport replacement help and victim assistance information; local police handle local crime reports. [^state-dept-help-abroad]
7. **Tell a trusted person.** Shame keeps scams alive. A calm supporter can help you sort facts.

If you overpaid but are safe, it may be best to document and move on. Chasing a small amount while exhausted can create a larger risk.

### Supporter note

Before the trip, help the traveler create a “first 24 hours” card: lodging address, front desk phone, emergency contact, tomorrow’s first activity, bank contact, and a reminder: “No major purchases today.” If you are the person at home, expect a tired call. Keep your voice steady. Ask, “Are you safe? Are you sitting down? What needs to happen in the next ten minutes?”

Avoid scolding after a mistake. The traveler already feels foolish. Your job is to help them recover, not to prove you warned them.

### Chapter takeaway

Arrival day is for landing, not proving yourself. Eat, hydrate, charge the phone, verify through staffed sources, and postpone expensive decisions. Tomorrow’s rested self can say yes if yes still makes sense.

# Part III — Money, Cards, Phones, and Public Places

Once you have arrived, the next risk zone is daily transactions: cash, cards, phones, streets, transit, and official-looking pressure. You do not need to memorize every scam. You need a few habits that work when a moment starts to feel rushed.

## Chapter 8: ATM and Card Scams

The ATM in the train station looks ordinary enough: bank logo, glowing screen, card slot, keypad, and a small line of people shifting their luggage behind you. Your taxi driver is waiting outside. Your tour group meets in twenty minutes. You only need a little cash for the market.

That is the moment criminals like best. Not because you are careless, but because you are busy, a little tired, and standing in public with your wallet open. ATM and card scams work by turning a normal travel errand into a rushed performance. Someone watches your PIN. A device reads your card. A “helpful” stranger interrupts. A payment terminal offers to charge you in U.S. dollars with an exchange rate you did not choose. A waiter walks away with your card for “just a minute.”

Most card problems can be reduced with three habits: choose safer machines, control the moment of payment, and check your accounts quickly. You do not need to become suspicious of every cashier. You do need to treat your card like your passport: useful, valuable, and not something to hand over casually.

> **Quick Take**
> - Use ATMs inside or attached to a bank when possible, and inspect the machine before inserting your card.
> - Cover your PIN with your other hand every time, even if no one seems to be watching.
> - Decline help from strangers at machines and payment terminals.
> - When a card terminal offers your home currency, choose local currency instead.
> - Keep your card in sight at restaurants and shops, and review statements promptly.

### The scam mechanics: how the money gets taken

Card scams usually happen in one of five ways.

**Skimming** means a criminal captures card information with a device attached to, or hidden inside, an ATM, fuel pump, ticket kiosk, or payment terminal. A tiny camera or a person nearby may capture your PIN. Sometimes the fake part is obvious if you look closely: a bulky card slot, a loose keypad, a plastic lip that does not match the machine. Sometimes it is not.

**Shimming** is a thinner version aimed at chip cards. The device sits inside the reader and steals data when the card is inserted. Chip cards are safer than magnetic-stripe cards, but no payment method is magic.

**Shoulder surfing** is simple watching. A person behind you, beside you, or pretending to use a phone can see your PIN, then steal the card or use captured card data.

**Card trapping** uses a device or trick that keeps your card inside the machine. A stranger says, “That happened to me yesterday—enter your PIN twice,” or “Press cancel and wait.” While you are distracted or walk away for help, the criminal retrieves the card.

**Dynamic currency conversion**, often called DCC, is not necessarily a scam in the criminal sense. It is a card feature offered by some merchants, ATMs, and payment processors that lets you see a charge in your home currency. The problem is that the offered exchange rate and fees may be worse than your bank or card network would provide. In many travel situations, the better choice is usually local currency, but verify the screen carefully. Some machines phrase the choice in a confusing way, and the local-currency option may be the one that says something like “continue without conversion.”

### Scene: the crowded ATM

Picture Margaret, seventy-four, arriving in Lisbon after an overnight flight. She finds an ATM just outside baggage claim. The screen is in English, which feels reassuring. As she inserts her card, a man in a neat jacket steps close and says, “This one charges too much. Use the button on the left.” He reaches toward the keypad.

Margaret feels two pressures at once. First, she does not want to seem rude. Second, she wants the transaction finished because people are waiting. Those are classic senior pressure points: politeness, fear of holding up the line, unfamiliar technology, and the desire not to make a scene.

Her safest move is not to debate him. It is to end the transaction.

She covers the keypad, presses cancel, takes her card, and says, “No thank you. I don’t accept help at ATMs.” Then she walks to a staffed bank branch inside the terminal.

That one sentence protects her money and her confidence.

### Before you use an ATM

Use a machine in a bank lobby, airport banking area, hotel lobby, or other well-lit controlled space when you can. Outdoor machines on quiet streets, standalone convenience-store machines, and machines tucked into nightlife areas may be more exposed to tampering or distraction.

Before inserting your card:

- Look at the card slot. Does it seem crooked, bulky, taped, cracked, or oddly colored?
- Lightly wiggle the reader. Do not force anything; you are only checking for loose attachments.
- Look above and around the keypad for tiny holes, plastic strips, or anything aimed at your fingers.
- Check whether the keypad feels raised or unusually spongy.
- Notice who is nearby. If someone is too close, let them go first or step away.
- Have your card ready before you approach so you are not searching through your wallet in public.

Always cover your PIN with your other hand, even if no one seems to be watching. Covering the keypad is not paranoia. It is seat-belt behavior.

### What to do in the first 30 seconds

If something feels wrong at an ATM or card terminal, the first 30 seconds matter because you may still have your card, your cash, and your choices.

1. **Stop touching new buttons.** Do not keep trying because a stranger says to.
2. **Cover the keypad.** If your PIN is exposed, the risk increases.
3. **Press cancel once.** Wait for the card to return.
4. **Take card, cash, and receipt immediately.** Put them away before talking.
5. **Step back from the machine.** Create space.
6. **Refuse all help from strangers.** Staff can help from behind a counter, not by reaching over your shoulder.
7. **If the card does not return, call the issuer from your banking app or the number on another card record.** Do not use a phone number posted on a suspicious sticker near the machine.

If you feel unsafe, leave the cash. Money can be replaced more easily than personal safety.

### Script ladder: from polite to firm

Many travelers, especially older travelers, are targeted because they were raised to be courteous. Keep courtesy, but add boundaries.

**Level 1: Polite**  
“No thank you. I’m all set.”

**Level 2: Clear**  
“I do not accept help at ATMs.”

**Level 3: Firm**  
“Please step back. I need privacy.”

**Level 4: Exit**  
“I’m canceling this transaction now.” Then leave.

For a cashier or restaurant situation:

**Level 1:** “Please run the card here where I can see it.”  
**Level 2:** “I don’t let my card leave the table.”  
**Level 3:** “If the terminal cannot come here, I’ll pay another way.”

You do not need to accuse anyone. A boundary is enough.

### DCC: the dollar amount that may cost more

At a cafe in Rome, the server presents a handheld terminal. It asks: “Pay 82.40 EUR or 91.73 USD?” The U.S. dollar amount feels comforting because you know what it means. But that familiar number may include an unfavorable exchange rate or markup.

The usual travel rule is: choose the local currency and let your card issuer or network do the conversion. In this example, that usually means choosing euros. But read the screen carefully. Some ATMs use confusing wording such as “Accept conversion” versus “Decline conversion.” Declining the machine’s conversion often still completes the withdrawal in local currency. If you are unsure, cancel and use another machine or ask your bank before travel how its cards display foreign transactions.

Accuracy caveat: local currency is usually the better choice, but not every card, country, or terminal is identical. Verify the total, fees, and wording before you approve.

### Red flags

Watch for these warnings:

- A stranger approaches while you are using the machine.
- The card slot, keypad, or cash dispenser looks loose or mismatched.
- The machine is in a dim, isolated, or unusually crowded spot.
- The ATM displays multiple fee screens you do not understand.
- A terminal defaults to U.S. dollars without asking.
- A merchant insists on taking your card out of sight.
- A cashier says the first transaction “failed” but will not provide a void receipt.
- Someone asks you to re-enter your PIN after a problem.
- A posted help number is on a sticker, not part of official bank signage.

### Recovery steps if your card is compromised

If your card is lost, trapped, skimmed, or charged incorrectly, act quickly but calmly.

1. **Lock or freeze the card** in your banking app if available.
2. **Call the card issuer** using the number in the app, on the back of the card, or from the issuer’s official website. If your physical card is gone, use a saved number from your travel documents.
3. **Review recent transactions** for small test charges as well as large ones.
4. **Change your PIN** if it may have been seen or entered on a suspicious device.
5. **Save evidence:** receipts, ATM location, time, screenshots, merchant name, and photos of posted prices if safe.
6. **Ask your issuer about emergency replacement cards or digital wallet options.**
7. **File reports when appropriate** with your bank, card network, travel insurer, tour operator, local police, the FTC’s ReportFraud system for scams, IdentityTheft.gov for identity theft, or the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center for internet-enabled fraud.

Card disputes are important, but they are not guaranteed. Credit cards, debit cards, ATM withdrawals, mobile payments, wire transfers, and cash have different protections, deadlines, and documentation requirements. Your issuer may investigate timing, receipts, chip use, PIN use, merchant records, and local rules. Strong documentation improves your chances, but it does not promise reimbursement.

### Chapter takeaway

At every ATM or card terminal, remember: **one hand covers, one mind chooses.** Cover your PIN with your hand. Use your mind—not the line behind you, not a stranger, not a flashing screen—to choose the currency, the amount, and whether to continue.

A safe transaction is not the fastest transaction. It is the one you understand before you approve it.

## Chapter 9: Currency Exchange and Overcharge Tricks

Money feels different when you travel. The bills are new colors. Coins pile up in your pocket. A taxi fare has more zeros than you expected. A restaurant bill includes a line you do not recognize. You may know, in a general way, that ten euros is not ten dollars, or that 10,000 yen is not a fortune, but in the moment your brain hesitates.

Scammers love that hesitation. So do dishonest merchants, aggressive touts, and opportunists who are not running a grand criminal enterprise but are happy to overcharge a tired visitor. Currency exchange and overcharge tricks depend on confusion, embarrassment, and momentum. They work especially well on older travelers who do not want to appear difficult, who may be managing eyesight or hearing challenges, or who remember a time when arguing over a bill felt impolite.

The goal is not to become a suspicious person who distrusts every waiter, driver, and shopkeeper. Most people you meet will be honest. The goal is to make prices visible before you agree and to keep money simple enough that you can notice a problem.

> **Quick Take**
> - Ask for the total price in writing before you commit to a purchase, ride, or exchange.
> - Treat “no commission” without a clearly posted exchange rate as a warning, not a deal.
> - Hold and count bills yourself; do not let cash leave your hand prematurely.
> - Clarify the unit before saying yes: total or per person, per bag, per hour, per photo.
> - Use bank ATMs rather than street exchangers when practical, and compare the final amount you receive.

### A micro-scene: the “no commission” window

Paul and Denise, both in their late seventies, arrive with U.S. dollars and see a bright exchange booth near the square: “NO COMMISSION.” The clerk smiles, taps a calculator, and slides bills through the tray. The stack looks thick. They step away pleased—until their grandson later checks the rate and realizes the booth used a poor exchange rate that cost them far more than a normal fee would have.

“No commission” did not mean “best price.” It only meant the charge was hidden in the rate.

That is one of the central mechanics of travel money scams: the fee is moved, renamed, or buried.

### Common mechanics

Currency and overcharge tricks often follow patterns:

- **Bad exchange rates behind friendly signs.** “No fee” or “no commission” may hide a wide spread between the buying and selling rate.
- **Short-changing.** A clerk counts quickly, uses unfamiliar bills, or distracts you with conversation while returning too little.
- **Bill switching.** You hand over a large bill; the person claims you gave a smaller one.
- **Damaged-bill claims.** After accepting your money, the person says the bill is torn, old, or invalid and demands another.
- **Calculator tricks.** A displayed amount includes an extra zero, a decimal in the wrong place, or a conversion that favors the seller.
- **Menu switching.** A posted menu shows one price; a different menu appears after you eat.
- **Unclear service charges.** Cover charges, bread charges, table fees, resort fees, luggage fees, or “special holiday” charges appear only at the end.
- **Per-person surprises.** “Twenty” meant twenty per person, per bag, per hour, or per photo.
- **The rushed taxi or rideshare alternative.** A driver quotes one amount, then claims traffic, luggage, tolls, or night rates changed the price.
- **Friendly add-ons.** A guide, performer, or vendor offers “just try it,” then demands payment after you accept.

Not every mismatch is fraud. Travel involves language barriers, real local taxes, and customs you may not know. But a fair business can explain the price before you buy.

### What to do in the first 30 seconds

When a money situation starts to feel off, use the first 30 seconds to slow the transaction without escalating it.

1. **Keep the bill, menu, or item in view.** Do not hand over more money while confused.
2. **Ask for the total in writing.** Point to paper, phone, calculator, or receipt.
3. **Clarify the unit.** “Total or per person?” “Per bag?” “Per hour?”
4. **Separate your cash.** Hold only the amount you intend to pay; keep larger bills out of sight.
5. **Use your phone calculator if appropriate.** Type the amount yourself.
6. **If the person becomes pushy, step toward a public counter, hotel desk, or official staff member.**
7. **If you feel unsafe, pay the smallest reasonable amount and leave.** Your pride is not worth a confrontation.

The first 30 seconds are not for winning an argument. They are for preventing a larger loss.

### The price-before-service rule

Use these safer habits:

- “What is the total price?”
- “Is that per person or total?”
- “Are taxes, service, tolls, luggage, or tips included?”
- “Can you write it down?”
- “May I see the menu with the prices?”
- “Can I pay by card, and will it be charged in local currency?”

If the answer is vague, smile and leave. A clear price is not a luxury. It is part of consent.

### Script ladder: money conversations without drama

Use short sentences. Long explanations invite debate.

**At a market stall**  
Level 1: “How much, total?”  
Level 2: “Please write the total price.”  
Level 3: “No written price, no purchase.”

**In a taxi or private car**  
Level 1: “Please start the meter.”  
Level 2: “What is the total fare including bags and tolls?”  
Level 3: “I will get another ride if the price is not clear.”

**At a restaurant or cafe**  
Level 1: “Could you show me where that charge is on the menu?”  
Level 2: “We did not order this item.”  
Level 3: “Please bring the manager and an itemized receipt.”

**At an exchange counter**  
Level 1: “How much local currency will I receive for this amount?”  
Level 2: “Please show the rate and all fees before I hand over cash.”  
Level 3: “No thank you. I am not exchanging today.”

Practice the sentences before travel. It is easier to speak firmly when the words are already in your mouth.

### Senior-specific pressure points

Older travelers can face particular pressures around money: small print, unfamiliar accents, politeness training, fatigue, and tour-group time pressure. Carry reading glasses, ask for prices in writing, and decide ahead of time who in your party handles money questions. If a payment screen is unclear, cancel and start over. You can be kind and still ask for an itemized receipt.

### Red flags

Be cautious when you notice:

- “No commission” signs without a clearly posted exchange rate.
- A clerk who will not show how much you receive before taking your cash.
- A driver who refuses the meter or gives only a vague estimate.
- A guide who says, “Pay what you like,” then demands a fixed amount.
- A menu with no prices, or prices covered by stickers.
- A bill that includes items you did not order.
- A card terminal turned toward the cashier so you cannot see the amount.
- A seller who becomes angry when you ask for a receipt.
- Pressure to pay in U.S. dollars when the local currency is normal.
- A QR code payment page that does not match the business name.

That last point belongs in this chapter because money scams increasingly use phones. A fake QR sticker on a table, parking meter, or ticket sign may send you to a look-alike payment page. If you are about to enter card details after scanning a code, check the web address, business name, and amount. When in doubt, use the official app, typed website, staffed counter, or card terminal.

### Keep cash simple

Before leaving your hotel each morning, build a “day wallet.” Put in small bills, one card, transit pass, and perhaps a modest emergency amount. Keep larger bills, backup cards, and extra cash elsewhere, preferably in a money belt, hotel safe, or locked luggage depending on the situation.

When paying cash, say the bill amount as you hand it over: “Here is fifty.” That simple narration helps prevent bill-switching. Place money on the counter rather than into a moving hand when possible, and wait for change before putting away your wallet.

Use ATMs in secure locations rather than street exchangers when practical. If you do exchange cash, compare the final amount you will receive, not just the advertised fee. A booth with a fee but a better rate may beat a “no commission” booth.

### Card overcharges and DCC

Card payments create their own traps. A merchant may select U.S. dollars on the terminal before handing it to you. A restaurant may add a tip line in a country where service is already included. A shop may run a transaction twice and say the first failed.

Check the amount and currency before tapping or inserting your card. For dynamic currency conversion, the practical rule is usually to choose local currency, but verify the screen. DCC wording can be confusing, and not every terminal displays it the same way. If a charge is in U.S. dollars when you expected local currency, ask to cancel and rerun it before you sign or approve.

If you later dispute a card charge, remember the caveat: disputes are not guaranteed. Banks and card issuers evaluate evidence. Keep receipts, photos of posted prices, screenshots, booking confirmations, and names.

### Recovery steps after an overcharge

If the amount is small and the setting feels tense, leaving may be the wisest choice. If the amount is significant:

1. **Get an itemized receipt.** If they refuse, photograph posted prices or menus if safe.
2. **Write down details immediately:** time, place, names, taxi number, license plate, table number, or booking reference.
3. **Contact the platform** if it involved a rideshare, booking site, tour marketplace, or delivery app.
4. **Ask your hotel or tour leader for help** with local customs and translation.
5. **Contact your card issuer** to ask about a dispute or chargeback. Provide documents, but do not assume the outcome.
6. **Report patterns** to local consumer agencies, tourism offices, or police when practical.
7. **Warn your travel companions** so the same person or place does not target someone else.

### Chapter takeaway

The memorable rule for this chapter is: **See the total before you say yes.**

Not the estimate. Not the friendly wave. Not “we settle later.” The total. Written, displayed, or clearly confirmed. When the price is visible, you can decide. When it is hidden, someone else is deciding for you.

## Chapter 10: Phone, Wi-Fi, QR Code, and Booking Scams

Your phone is your map, camera, wallet, boarding pass, translator, medication list, rideshare tool, hotel key, family connection, and emergency lifeline. For many older travelers, it is also the bridge between independence and anxiety: “As long as I have my phone, I can find my way back.”

That makes the phone a target. Criminals do not always need to steal the device. Sometimes they only need you to connect to the wrong Wi-Fi, scan the wrong QR code, click the wrong booking message, or give payment information to a caller pretending to be the front desk.

Digital travel scams arrive dressed as convenience: free airport Wi-Fi, a QR menu at a cafe, a booking text, a payment warning, a parking code, or a late-night room phone call.

The defense is not to avoid technology. Technology helps travelers, especially older travelers. The defense is to use official paths, protect logins, and treat urgent digital requests as something to check separately.

> **Quick Take**
> - Confirm the exact Wi-Fi network name with staff before connecting.
> - Never enter banking, email, or cloud-storage passwords on a Wi-Fi pop-up page.
> - Treat QR codes as untrusted: check the web address before entering payment or login information.
> - Never give card details on an incoming room phone call; hang up and call the front desk yourself.
> - Use the official app, typed website, or saved phone number — not the link in an urgent message.

### A micro-scene: the hotel Wi-Fi trap

Ellen, eighty-one, arrives at a small hotel after a long train ride. At check-in the clerk says, “The Wi-Fi is RoseGarden-Guest.” In her room, Ellen sees several networks: RoseGarden-Guest, Rose_Garden_Free, Hotel Guest 5G, and Free Wi-Fi Secure. She chooses Free Wi-Fi Secure because it has the strongest signal. A page asks her to “confirm” her email password to activate the connection.

That is the danger moment. The fake network does not need to break into her phone. It only needs to persuade her to type a password into a page the criminal controls.

Ellen’s safer move is to stop, disconnect, and call the desk: “Could you repeat the exact Wi-Fi name?” If the login page asks for email, banking, or cloud-storage passwords, she does not proceed.

### Public Wi-Fi: useful, but not private

Public Wi-Fi in airports, hotels, cafes, libraries, cruise terminals, and train stations can be legitimate and helpful. But public networks are shared spaces. A fake network may imitate the real name, and a real network may still be less secure than your home network.

Safer habits: confirm the exact Wi-Fi name with staff or official signage; avoid vague names such as “Free Airport Wi-Fi” if the airport lists a different official name; do not enter banking, email, or medical passwords through a Wi-Fi pop-up page; use cellular data for banking and sensitive accounts when possible; keep your phone and apps updated before travel; turn off auto-join for public networks you no longer need; and use a strong passcode, not only face or fingerprint unlock. A reputable VPN can help if you already know how to use it, but it should not make you reckless.

Accuracy caveat: secure websites and modern apps provide important protection, but public Wi-Fi still deserves caution. The safest route for high-value tasks—banking, card disputes, password changes—is usually cellular data or a trusted private network.

### QR codes: convenient squares with invisible destinations

QR codes are everywhere: restaurant menus, museum guides, parking meters, luggage storage, scooter rentals, event tickets, and payment pages. Most are legitimate. The risk is that a QR code hides the destination until after you scan it. A scammer can place a sticker over a real code, print a fake sign, or send a code in a message.

At a cafe, a couple scans a table code to view the menu. The site asks them to create an account and enter a card before showing prices. At a parking meter, a traveler scans a sticker and pays $18 to a page that looks official but is not connected to the city. At an airport, a “lost luggage claim” QR code leads to a form asking for passport and card details.

Before you pay or log in from a QR code:

- Look for stickers placed over original signs.
- Check the web address after scanning and before entering information.
- Be suspicious of misspellings, extra hyphens, odd endings, or a business name that does not match.
- Use the official app or type the website yourself when money is involved.
- Do not enter banking passwords from a QR link.
- If a restaurant QR menu demands card details before you order, ask for a paper menu or staff help.

QR codes are doors. Look at the address before you walk through.

### Booking and phishing scams

Travel phishing messages use urgency because urgency works. The message may say:

- “Your reservation will be canceled unless you verify your card.”
- “Your room is not guaranteed.”
- “We could not process your payment.”
- “Your flight refund is ready.”
- “Your tour meeting point changed—click here.”
- “Security issue: confirm your identity now.”

The message may include your real hotel name, dates, or booking number, especially if a third-party system, email account, or property messaging tool has been compromised. That makes it feel legitimate.

Do not click the link just because the details are correct. Open the booking app yourself, type the official website, or call the hotel using the number from your original confirmation—not the number in the urgent message. If the message came through a booking platform, check inside the platform account after opening the app directly.

### The hotel room phone scam

This scam is old because it still works. You are asleep or getting ready for bed. The room phone rings. The caller says, “This is the front desk. Our system lost your card. Could you read the number again so we can keep your room active?”

A tired traveler may comply because the call seems internal. But anyone who can reach room phones—or who tricks the hotel switchboard—may sound official.

Your rule: never give card details on an incoming room call. Hang up. Call the front desk using the official button on the hotel phone, the number printed on your key packet, or walk downstairs.

Script: “I don’t give payment information on incoming calls. I’ll contact the desk directly.”

### What to do in the first 30 seconds

When a digital request surprises you, the first 30 seconds can prevent a stolen password or card number.

1. **Stop before tapping.** Do not click, scan again, or keep typing.
2. **Look at the source.** Who sent it? Does the address, number, or network name exactly match the official one?
3. **Leave the message or page.** Do not use its link to “fix” the problem.
4. **Open a separate route.** Use the official app, typed website, saved phone number, or front desk.
5. **If you already entered a password, change it from a trusted connection.** Start with email, banking, and booking accounts.
6. **If you entered card details, lock the card and call the issuer.**
7. **Tell your travel companion.** A second set of eyes can catch panic decisions.

The key is separation. The message creates the emergency; you use a separate channel to check whether the emergency is real.

### Script ladder for digital pressure

**Suspicious hotel or booking message:** “I will check this in the app.” / “I don’t use links from payment messages.” / “Please send any request through the official booking platform.”

**Front desk phone request:** “I’ll call the desk back.” / “I don’t provide card numbers on incoming calls.” / “I’m hanging up now and coming downstairs.”

**QR payment page:** “I’m checking the web address.” / “I’ll use the official app instead.” / “I’m not entering my card on this page.”

### Red flags

Be alert when you see:

- A Wi-Fi login page asking for email, bank, or cloud-storage passwords.
- Multiple similar network names at the same hotel or airport.
- A QR code sticker placed over another code.
- A payment page with a strange web address or misspelled business name.
- A booking message demanding immediate payment outside the platform.
- A hotel caller asking for card details after you are already checked in.
- A text or email that threatens cancellation within minutes.
- A request to download an unfamiliar app to receive a refund or ticket.
- A rideshare driver messaging outside the app for payment or pickup changes.
- A public charging station or borrowed cable offered by a stranger when you can use your own power bank.

### Recovery steps if you clicked, scanned, or paid

Do not waste time feeling embarrassed. Digital scams are designed to look normal. Take the next steps in order:

1. Disconnect from suspicious Wi-Fi and switch to cellular data or a trusted network.
2. Change exposed passwords from a safe connection, starting with email because it resets other accounts.
3. Enable multi-factor authentication on important accounts.
4. Lock or replace affected cards, review transactions, and contact your issuer about disputes or fraud claims. Reimbursement is not guaranteed.
5. Save screenshots, URLs, phone numbers, emails, QR locations, receipts, and booking messages.
6. Report the problem inside the official hotel, airline, rideshare, rental, or ticketing platform.
7. Warn the venue if a cafe table, parking meter, or museum sign has a fake QR sticker.

If your email account was compromised, assume the criminal may see travel confirmations and password reset messages. Change that password first, then review forwarding rules and logged-in devices if you know how, or ask a trusted helper.

### Travel phone checklist

Before leaving home:

- [ ] Update devices and key apps.
- [ ] Set a strong passcode.
- [ ] Turn on device-finder features.
- [ ] Save hotel addresses and emergency contacts offline.
- [ ] Bring a power bank.
- [ ] Install official airline, hotel, bank, and rideshare apps.
- [ ] Turn on card alerts.

On the road:

- [ ] Confirm Wi-Fi names before connecting.
- [ ] Use cellular data for sensitive logins when possible.
- [ ] Treat QR payment codes as untrusted until checked.
- [ ] Avoid urgent booking links sent by text or email.
- [ ] Ask official staff, not random bystanders, when technology fails.

### Chapter takeaway

The memorable rule for this chapter is: **Use the official path.** Choose the official app, typed website, saved phone number, front desk in person, card issuer number from the banking app, or rideshare communication inside the rideshare app.

Scammers try to pull you onto their path—a look-alike Wi-Fi network, QR sticker, payment link, or incoming call. Step back onto the official path, and the scam usually loses its power.

## Chapter 11: Pickpocket Teams and Distraction Setups

The train doors open, and everyone moves at once. A young man drops a handful of coins near your feet. Someone behind you bumps your shoulder. A woman at your left asks, “Is this the stop for the cathedral?” For three seconds, your attention goes everywhere except the zipper on your bag.

Pickpocketing is not always one sneaky hand in one pocket. In tourist areas, transit hubs, markets, festivals, hotel lobbies, and busy sidewalks, theft may be a small performance. One person distracts, one blocks, one steals, and one carries the item away. The team may never look like a team.

> **Quick Take**
> - Treat sudden commotion as a cue to protect your space.
> - Keep daily cash separate from your passport, backup card, and emergency money.
> - Do not hang bags on chairs or place phones on cafe tables.
> - Boarding, exiting, paying, photographing, and sitting down are high-attention moments.
> - If theft happens, stop further loss before chasing.

### How distraction theft works

A pickpocket team needs three things: access, cover, and escape. Access means your wallet, phone, passport, or bag is reachable. Cover means your attention is elsewhere or the thief’s hand is hidden by a jacket, map, clipboard, shopping bag, stroller, or crowd. Escape means the stolen item can be passed to another person or carried into a crowd before you understand what happened.

Common setups include someone spilling sauce or birdlike “droppings” on your coat and offering to clean it; a person dropping coins, papers, or a phone; a crowd pressing at a train or bus door; someone stopping suddenly at the top of an escalator; a staged argument; a stranger asking for directions while standing too close; a child or adult holding a petition board against your body; and a bag lifted from the back of a cafe chair.

The goal is not to make you afraid of crowds. The goal is to make you protective during transitions. Transitions are moments when your brain is already busy: finding the platform, stepping over a curb, reading a menu, paying a driver, lifting luggage, answering a question, or taking a photograph.

### Senior-specific pressure points

Older travelers often carry items that matter: medications, reading glasses, hearing aid batteries, a printed itinerary, insurance cards, a passport, and sometimes a spouse’s documents too. You may also be managing balance, fatigue, limited hand strength, or the natural caution of moving through unfamiliar streets. A thief does not need you to be careless. The thief needs one crowded moment when your attention is divided.

There is also a social pressure point. Many people, especially those who pride themselves on being courteous, automatically respond when someone asks for help. A thief can use that reflex. You can remain helpful while creating distance: “I’m sorry, I can’t help,” said while moving your bag to the front and taking a step away, is both polite and protective.

### Build a travel carry system

Use three layers.

1. **On-body essentials:** passport when you must carry it, backup card, emergency cash, medication doses you cannot miss, and perhaps a paper with hotel address and emergency contacts. Keep these in a money belt, neck pouch worn under clothing, interior zipped pocket, or other secure place that is not opened in public.
2. **Daily wallet:** small cash and one card for the day. This is what you use at ticket windows, cafes, and shops.
3. **Bag or luggage:** replaceable items such as a sweater, water bottle, guidebook, umbrella, and snacks.

Never keep every important item in one bag. If you travel with a spouse or friend, divide backups. One person should not carry both passports, all cards, all cash, and both phones unless there is no alternative. If one person is more mobile, that person can handle tickets, but the other can still carry a sealed backup card or emergency cash.

Before leaving the hotel, do a thirty-second “touch check”: phone, wallet, room key, glasses, medication, passport if needed. Do the same before leaving a taxi, rideshare, cafe, restroom, train seat, or security checkpoint.

### Red flags and protective habits

Watch for these cues:

- Someone gets unusually close when there is room not to.
- A person insists on helping clean your clothing or bag.
- A stranger points urgently at your shoe, coat, tire, backpack, or spilled item.
- A group creates a bottleneck at a doorway, escalator, ticket machine, or turnstile.
- Your bag is behind you, open, or hanging from a chair.
- Your phone is lying on a table beside a map, menu, or cup.
- A person holds a coat, newspaper, sign, or map over your belongings.

Simple habits defeat many setups:

- Wear crossbody bags in front in crowds.
- Zip compartments after every use.
- Put a hand over the zipper when boarding or exiting.
- Keep wallets out of back pockets.
- Do not place your phone on a cafe table; keep it in a zipped pocket or hold it.
- Keep bags on your lap or between your feet where you can see them.
- Loop a bag strap around your leg only if doing so will not create a tripping hazard.
- At restaurants, avoid hanging a purse or day bag on the back of a chair.

### Micro-scene: the cafe table

A widower traveling with his sister sits at an outdoor cafe. He places his phone beside his coffee so he can check the walking route. A man approaches with a laminated card asking for donations. The card covers the phone for a moment.

His sister has read about this. She gently moves the phone into his hand and says to the man, “No thank you.” The man leaves quickly.

No accusation is needed. The important part is recognizing the cover. Anything that blocks your view of your phone or wallet is a cue to move it.

### Script ladder: close-contact distraction

1. **Create space:** “Excuse me, I need room.”
2. **Decline help:** “No thank you. I’ll handle it.”
3. **Protect belongings:** “Please do not touch my bag.”
4. **Call attention:** “Security, could you help me?” or “Staff, I need assistance.”
5. **Exit the cluster:** Move to a wall, counter, staffed booth, hotel lobby, or open area.

If someone spills something on you, step away first. Do not let several strangers crowd around to wipe your jacket. Say, “I’ll clean it myself,” and move your bag to the front. If someone drops items at your feet, you do not have to bend down. Stepping aside is enough.

### If your wallet, passport, or phone is stolen

Do not chase into traffic, alleys, train tunnels, or crowds. Physical safety comes first. Most stolen items are not worth a fall, injury, or confrontation with a team.

Move somewhere safe: a hotel lobby, staffed cafe counter, police station, transit office, bank, museum security desk, or well-lit shop. Then contain the loss.

1. **Cards:** Lock or cancel cards through your bank app, card issuer website, or the phone numbers on your backup list. If you use mobile payments, remove the stolen device from trusted devices if possible.
2. **Phone:** Use Find My iPhone, Find My Device, or your carrier to lock, mark lost, or wipe the phone if needed. Change passwords for email, banking, and travel accounts if the phone was unlocked or accessible.
3. **Passport:** Contact the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate if you are a U.S. citizen abroad and your passport is lost or stolen. They can guide you through replacement procedures and local reporting needs.
4. **Police report:** Ask whether a local police report is needed for insurance, card disputes, passport replacement, or travel insurance claims.
5. **Medications and medical items:** If medication, glasses, hearing aids, or medical documents were stolen, contact your travel insurer, doctor, pharmacy, tour leader, cruise desk, or embassy or consulate for help finding replacement options.
6. **Support:** Tell a travel companion, adult child, trusted friend, hotel manager, or tour leader. Stress makes decisions harder; a second calm person helps.

Write down what happened: time, place, transit line or street, description of the setup, what was taken, and what steps you completed. This record is useful when your mind is tired.

### Chapter takeaway

Pickpocket teams do not win because travelers are foolish. They win when normal attention is overloaded. Your job is not to be tense all day. Your job is to create a few automatic habits: separate valuables, zip and touch-check, protect transitions, keep phones off tables, and move away from commotion before solving it.

If something is stolen, the trip is not ruined unless you are left alone with panic. Stop further loss, replace what matters, ask for help, and continue when you are ready. A prepared traveler can lose a wallet and keep their confidence.

## Chapter 12: Fake Officials and Authority Pressure

A man in a dark jacket steps in front of you near the train station. He shows a badge quickly, too quickly for you to read. “Police,” he says. “There is counterfeit money in this area. We must inspect your wallet.” His partner stands slightly behind you. The street is busy, but the conversation suddenly feels private.

Authority changes behavior. A person who would ignore a pushy street vendor may obey someone claiming to be police, customs, transit inspection, hotel security, immigration, or health control. Scammers know this. They use badges, uniforms, official-looking lanyards, radios, clipboards, and confident voices to make travelers comply before thinking.

> **Quick Take**
> - A uniform, badge, vest, or confident tone is not enough by itself.
> - Procedures vary by country and city; physical safety comes first.
> - Do not physically resist real or armed officials.
> - When safe, ask for identification, a written citation or receipt, and an official payment channel.
> - Do not hand over your wallet for “inspection” or pay cash to unofficial channels if you can safely avoid it.

### The essential safety caveat

This chapter is about reducing scam risk, not arguing with legitimate law enforcement. Procedures vary widely. In some places, real transit inspectors collect fines on the spot. In others, they issue written citations. In some countries, police may have authority to require identification. In a tense situation, especially with armed officials, your physical safety is more important than winning a debate about procedure.

Do not run. Do not shove. Do not grab back documents from a real or armed official. Do not escalate if you feel unsafe. Use calm words, public visibility, and official channels when you can. If you believe the person is fake but the situation feels dangerous, comply enough to stay safe, move toward witnesses if possible, and seek help afterward from your hotel, tour leader, local authorities, embassy or consulate, or emergency number.

The safer goal is not defiance. The safer goal is verification.

### Common fake-official scripts

Fake officials often rely on urgency and embarrassment. They may say:

- “Your passport has a problem.”
- “You used counterfeit money.”
- “You broke a transit rule.”
- “There are drugs in this area; we must inspect your wallet.”
- “Your visa is invalid.”
- “You photographed a restricted building.”
- “Your taxi or rideshare driver reported you.”
- “Pay the fine now or you will be arrested.”
- “The embassy cannot help you.”
- “Do not call anyone. This must be handled immediately.”

A scammer may use a partner who pretends to be another tourist and pays quickly. That staged “proof” pressures you to do the same. Another version begins with a friendly stranger who asks to see your money or passport, then a fake officer appears and accuses both of you of wrongdoing. The friendly stranger may disappear, leaving you frightened and eager to pay.

### Senior-specific pressure points

Older travelers may be especially vulnerable to authority pressure because many were taught to respect officials, avoid public conflict, and keep travel documents in perfect order. If you are already tired, worried about missing a train, concerned about a spouse’s mobility, or anxious about medication timing, an official-sounding threat can feel overwhelming.

Scammers may also use age-related assumptions against you: speaking too fast, crowding your personal space, implying you misunderstood a rule, or separating you from a younger companion “for your own good.” None of this means you cannot handle the situation. It means you are allowed to slow it down.

A useful internal phrase is: **Respectful does not mean rushed.**

### Red flags

Be cautious when:

- The person will not show identification long enough for you to read it.
- They refuse to move to a staffed office, station, ticket counter, hotel front desk, or other official location.
- They demand cash immediately and discourage a receipt.
- They ask to inspect your wallet, count your money, handle your cards, or see your PIN.
- They tell you not to call your hotel, tour leader, cruise desk, embassy or consulate, family, or local emergency number.
- They threaten arrest for asking basic questions.
- They isolate you from your companion or lead you to an alley, parked car, or unmarked office.
- They pressure you to pay through gift cards, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, a personal mobile account, or an unofficial card reader.

Green flags include clear identification, patience with reasonable verification, visible affiliation with a real checkpoint or office, written paperwork, official receipts, posted rules, and payment through a recognized government, transit, or court channel. Still, green flags do not require you to be careless. Keep control of your wallet and ask questions calmly.

### Safe verification

Stand where other people can see you if possible. Keep your voice even. Avoid insults such as “You are fake.” Instead, ask for process.

**Script ladder: fake official or authority pressure**

1. **Cooperative pause:** “I want to cooperate. Please speak slowly.”
2. **Identification:** “Please show your identification again so I can read it.”
3. **Official location:** “I will discuss this at the police station/transit office/hotel front desk.”
4. **Written process:** “Please write the citation, amount, office, and payment instructions.”
5. **Receipt:** “If there is a fine, I need an official receipt.”
6. **Call for help, when safe and lawful:** “I want to cooperate, and I need to verify the official payment process before I pay.” If there is immediate danger, call the local emergency number; for routine verification, use your hotel, tour leader, embassy or consulate, transit office, police station, or another official channel when you can do so safely.
7. **Safety exit:** If the situation feels unsafe, stop debating. Do not physically resist or obstruct a verified official. Focus on getting to a public, staffed place and seeking help afterward.

If you must show a passport, hold it yourself when possible and show the photo page without surrendering it, unless a verified official legally requires possession. Carrying a photocopy or digital scan may help in some routine situations, but it does not replace the legal requirements of the country you are visiting. Know local ID rules before you travel.

Do not hand over your wallet for inspection if you can safely avoid it. If someone claims counterfeit money is involved, offer to resolve it at a bank, police station, hotel front desk, or official office. If someone claims you violated a transit rule, ask for the written citation and official payment method. If someone claims your passport is invalid, ask to go to the nearest official immigration or police office and contact your embassy or consulate when safe. If a verified official gives a lawful order, do not turn verification into confrontation; prioritize safety and get help afterward.

### Micro-scene: the transit fine

A grandmother traveling with her adult son exits a tram. A man in a vest says her ticket was not validated and she must pay cash immediately. He has a badge, but no machine, no receipt book, and no other inspectors nearby.

She does not argue about the rule. She says, “I want to cooperate. Please write the citation and show me where to pay officially.” Her son adds, “We will step to the station office.”

If the man is legitimate, an official process should appear: a supervisor, a posted rule, a ticket office, a receipt, or a recognized payment channel. If he is not, the request for process may end the performance. Either way, she stays respectful, visible, and unhurried.

### If you paid, surrendered documents, or feel threatened

First, get safe. Go to your hotel, a police station, a staffed transit office, a consulate or embassy, a bank, a reputable shop, or another public place with staff. If you are in immediate danger, call the local emergency number.

Then document the event while details are fresh: time, location, descriptions, badge numbers if visible, vehicle markings, what was said, amount paid, payment method, witnesses, and any receipts or papers. Take photos only if it is safe and legal.

If you paid by card, contact the card issuer and explain that you were pressured by someone claiming official authority. Ask whether the charge can be disputed or the card should be replaced. If you paid cash, recovery may be difficult, but reporting still helps create a record for insurance or authorities. If your passport was taken or copied under suspicious circumstances, contact your embassy or consulate for guidance. If your phone was handled, check for missing cards, changed settings, unfamiliar apps, and account alerts.

Tell your hotel manager, tour leader, cruise security desk, or rideshare platform if the incident involved their property or services. If you are traveling alone, call a trusted person. Authority scams can leave capable people shaken. You deserve backup.

### Chapter takeaway

Real authority can be verified through real process. Scammers prefer speed, isolation, cash, secrecy, and shame. You do not need to challenge every uniform. You need to slow the moment, stay in public view, protect your wallet and documents, ask for written procedure, and choose safety over pride.

The strongest sentence is calm: “I want to cooperate through the official process.” It shows respect without surrendering your judgment.

### Part III confidence reset

You have now covered the main public-space pressure points: money, phones, friendly strangers, crowded movement, and authority claims. Most travel days will not include any of these problems. When something does feel off, the habits are small: keep essentials separated, slow payment moments down, and move unusual requests back through a channel you chose.

# Part IV — Hotels, Cruises, Tours, and Restaurants

After transit and street scams, the next layer is the places that feel safe: hotels, restaurants, ships, tours, and shops. Most of these places are exactly what they seem. The risk is that scammers borrow familiar routines—a front-desk call, a menu, a group excursion, a “recommended” store—and use them to rush a decision.

## Chapter 13: Hotels and Room Safety Scams

A hotel should feel like a safe landing place. After a long day of airports, taxis, walking, or sightseeing, the room becomes your quiet base. That is exactly why hotel-related scams can be so effective. Travelers are tired. They want the problem solved quickly. A caller says there is a front desk issue. A stranger in the hallway says he is maintenance. A message claims your reservation will be canceled unless you update your card. A person who looks helpful offers to carry your bag, call a taxi, or fix your room key.

Most hotel employees are not the problem. The risk is that scammers imitate hotel employees or exploit normal hotel routines. Your goal is not to treat the hotel like an enemy. Your goal is to use the hotel’s official channels and keep control of your room, identity, and money.

> **Quick Take**
> - Never read your card number to an incoming room phone call; hang up and call the front desk yourself.
> - Verify booking and payment messages by opening the official app or website, not the link in the message.
> - Confirm a person at your door is hotel staff by calling the front desk before you open it.
> - Use only hotel-arranged or clearly licensed taxis from the lobby, not unsolicited drivers outside.
> - Review the bill at checkout for resort, parking, destination, or currency-conversion fees.

### The fake front desk call

One of the oldest hotel scams is also one of the simplest. You check in. Later, usually when you are tired or asleep, the room phone rings. The caller says, “This is the front desk. We had a problem with your credit card. Could you please read the number again?” The voice sounds polite and ordinary. The request seems reasonable. You may not want to get dressed and walk downstairs.

Do not give the card number over that call.

Hang up and call the front desk using the number printed on the hotel phone, your reservation confirmation, or the hotel’s official website. Better yet, walk to the desk if you can do so safely. If the problem is real, the front desk can handle it in person.

### Script: the room phone payment call

> “I don’t give payment details on incoming calls. I’ll come to the front desk or call the official hotel number. Thank you.”

If the caller argues, that is useful information. Real hotel staff should understand caution. A scammer often pushes harder.

### Fake booking messages and phishing links

The FTC warns that phishing scams try to get personal or financial information by pretending to be a trusted company [^ftc-phishing]. In travel, this may look like an email or text from a hotel, vacation rental platform, booking site, airline, or cruise line. The message may say your reservation is incomplete, your room will be canceled, your payment failed, or you need to verify your identity.

Warning signs include:

- A link that does not match the hotel or booking company’s real website.
- Urgent language: “Act now,” “final notice,” “room canceled in two hours.”
- Requests for full card numbers, passwords, passport scans, or one-time codes.
- Poor spelling, odd formatting, or a sender address that looks almost right but not quite.
- A request to pay by wire transfer, gift card, cryptocurrency, or a payment app outside the booking system.

A senior traveler may be especially vulnerable because the message arrives when there is already a real reservation. It is easy to think, “They know I’m staying there, so this must be legitimate.” Unfortunately, reservation information can sometimes be exposed through data breaches, forwarded emails, shared devices, or compromised accounts.

### Safer response

Open a new browser window and type the hotel or booking site address yourself. Or use the official app you already installed. Do not use the link in the message. If you call, use a number from the official website or your original booking confirmation—not the number inside the suspicious message.

### Script: verifying a hotel message

> “I received a message about my reservation, but I do not click payment links. Can you confirm whether my booking is in good standing?”

Write down the name of the employee, the date, and what they said. If you are traveling with family, forward suspicious messages to a trusted person before acting.

### Room key and hallway scams

Hotels create a temporary community of strangers. Hallways, elevators, lobbies, breakfast rooms, and parking areas can feel semi-private, but they are public spaces. A scammer may watch for someone who appears alone, tired, or uncertain.

Common approaches include:

- “I’m locked out. Could you let me into this room?”
- “I’m maintenance. We need to check your air conditioner.”
- “I’m with housekeeping. Can I come in for a quick inspection?”
- “I’m hotel security. There has been a problem with your account.”
- “Your car has been hit. Come with me to the parking lot.”

Some may be legitimate. The safety rule is simple: verify before opening the door or leaving with a stranger.

If someone says they are hotel staff, keep the chain or latch engaged and call the front desk. If someone says there is an emergency involving your car, bag, or account, call the front desk or hotel security yourself. If you feel threatened, call local emergency services and alert the hotel.

### Script: person at the door

> “Please wait while I confirm with the front desk.”

Then close and lock the door. Do not apologize for being careful.

### The “helpful” luggage and taxi helper

A person near the entrance offers to help with your bags or arrange a taxi. Sometimes this is a legitimate bellhop, doorman, or licensed taxi dispatcher. Sometimes it is not. The difference may be hard to see after a long flight.

Before handing over luggage, look for hotel uniform, name tag, official stand, or confirmation from the front desk. If you need a taxi, ask the hotel to call one or direct you to the official taxi queue. In some cities, unlicensed drivers target tourists outside hotels, charging inflated fares or taking travelers to commission-based shops.

### Script: transportation from a hotel

> “Please arrange a licensed taxi or official car service through the hotel. I need the approximate fare and the driver’s name or car number.”

If someone tries to rush you away from the lobby, stop. A reputable driver does not need secrecy.

### Safe use of hotel Wi-Fi and shared devices

Hotel Wi-Fi is convenient, but it is not a private room. Scammers may create look-alike networks such as “Hotel_Guest_Free” when the real network is “HotelGuest.” A fake network can capture information or direct you to phishing pages.

At check-in, ask for the exact Wi-Fi network name and login method. Be cautious if a network asks for unusual information such as your full credit card number, Social Security number, or email password. Avoid banking or sensitive account changes on public Wi-Fi unless you are using a secure connection and know what you are doing. If possible, use your phone’s cellular data for financial tasks.

Avoid logging into email, bank, or medical portals on lobby computers. If you must use a shared computer, never save passwords, always log out, and assume information may not be private.

### Checklist: hotel digital safety

- Confirm the official Wi-Fi network name with the front desk.
- Do not enter payment information because a pop-up demands it.
- Use cellular data for banking when possible.
- Keep your phone and tablet locked with a passcode.
- Do not leave devices charging unattended in public areas.
- Do not share one-time security codes with callers or texters.

### Room safes, documents, and copies

A room safe is useful, but it is not magic. Use it for passport, backup cards, extra cash, and copies of important documents when you do not need them. Carry only what you need for the day. In some places, you may be required to carry identification; in others, a copy may be enough for ordinary sightseeing. Rules differ by country, so check official guidance before travel.

Keep a paper copy of your passport information page separate from the passport itself. Keep a digital copy in a secure place you can access if your phone is lost. Give a copy of your itinerary and passport information to a trusted person at home. The State Department recommends knowing how to contact the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate if you are abroad and need help.

### Room charges and checkout surprises

Not every unfair charge is a scam. Hotels may have resort fees, minibar charges, parking fees, destination fees, late checkout fees, or currency conversion costs. The problem is that some charges appear only at checkout, when travelers are in a hurry.

Before booking, look for total price, taxes, mandatory fees, cancellation deadlines, and deposit rules. At check-in, ask:

> “Are there any mandatory fees not already shown on my confirmation?”

At checkout, review the bill line by line. If you see a charge you do not recognize, ask calmly.

### Script: disputing a hotel charge

> “I don’t recognize this charge. Please show me what it is for. If it was added in error, please remove it before I check out.”

If the hotel will not correct it, keep the receipt, take a photo of the bill, write down names, and contact the booking platform or card issuer. A credit card often gives better dispute options than cash, debit, wire transfer, or payment apps.

### Caregiver note: helping without taking over

Adult children and caregivers can help older travelers by setting up safeguards before the trip:

- Print hotel confirmations with official phone numbers.
- Save hotel contacts in the traveler’s phone.
- Review what a phishing message may look like.
- Agree on a “pause and call me” rule for unexpected payment requests.
- Set spending alerts on credit cards if the traveler agrees.
- Make sure the traveler knows not to share one-time codes.

The goal is independence with backup. A senior traveler should not feel scolded for asking, “Does this message look real?” That question is a strength.

### Recovery steps if a hotel scam happens

If you gave payment information to a fake caller or phishing site:

1. Contact the card issuer immediately using the number on the back of the card or in the official app.
2. Ask about canceling the card, blocking charges, and disputing fraud.
3. Change passwords if you entered them on a suspicious site.
4. Turn on account alerts if available.
5. Report phishing or fraud to the relevant company and to appropriate authorities such as the FTC if you are in the United States.
6. If your passport or ID was copied or stolen abroad, contact the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate for guidance.
7. Tell a trusted person. Shame helps scammers; disclosure helps recovery.

### Chapter takeaway

Hotels are safer when you make one habit automatic: verify through the official front desk, official website, or official app before giving money, documents, or access to your room. A real employee can wait. A scammer needs you to hurry.

## Chapter 14: Cruises, Group Tours, and Shopping Pressure

Cruises and group tours can be wonderful for older travelers. They reduce planning, provide structure, and offer companionship. You can see several places without packing every morning. You can enjoy meals, lectures, excursions, and social events with people who share your interests.

That same structure can create pressure. You may be far from the ship, dependent on the group schedule, uncertain about local prices, and surrounded by people who are buying.

The pressure often sounds ordinary:

- A guide says the jewelry is a “once in a lifetime” value.
- A shop owner says the rug can be shipped home if you decide in the next ten minutes.
- A fellow traveler says everyone on the tour is investing in a vacation club.
- A salesperson says the cruise line “guarantees” the store.

Sometimes the deal is real enough but overpriced. Sometimes the promises are exaggerated. Sometimes the product never arrives.

The FTC advises travelers to be skeptical of travel deals that require quick payment, unusual payment methods, or promises that sound too good to be true [^ftc-avoid-travel-scams]. On cruises and tours, the most important skill is giving yourself permission to slow down, even when the group is moving fast.

> **Quick Take**
> - A “recommended” store is a recommendation, not a guarantee — read the fine print before buying.
> - Take all major purchases home with you mentally; sleep on contracts longer than 24 hours.
> - Get full details in writing: legal name, address, return policy, shipping, and total cost.
> - Pay by credit card for major purchases; cash, wire, gift cards, and apps offer little recourse.
> - Hospitality (free tea, demonstrations, transportation) is often part of the sales process — you may always leave.

### The psychology of organized travel

Group travel creates trust quickly. You eat with the same people. You listen to the same guide. You wear the same badge or carry the same tour sticker. You may feel that recommended stops have been carefully vetted. Often they have been chosen for convenience, safety, or commission arrangements. That does not mean they are dishonest. It does mean the recommendation may not be neutral.

Shopping pressure works because of four feelings:

- **Scarcity:** “This is the only place to buy it.”
- **Authority:** “Your guide recommends us.”
- **Belonging:** “Everyone on the tour is buying.”
- **Fatigue:** “I’m tired and want this decision done.”

A strong traveler recognizes the feeling and pauses.

### Script: buying time in a shop

> “I never make a large purchase while I’m on a timed tour. Please write the details down, and I’ll consider it later.”

If they refuse to write it down, that tells you something.

### Cruise port shopping and “guaranteed” stores

Cruise ports often have recommended shopping maps. Stores may advertise guarantees, appraisals, duty-free pricing, or special cruise passenger discounts. Some cruise lines or port shopping programs may offer limited help if something goes wrong, but travelers should not assume every purchase is protected. Read the fine print before buying expensive jewelry, watches, art, rugs, electronics, or collectibles.

Ask these questions before a major purchase:

- What exactly is the item made of?
- Is there an independent appraisal, or only the seller’s appraisal?
- What is the return policy in writing?
- Who pays return shipping?
- What happens if the item is not as described?
- Is the warranty valid in my home country?
- Is this price in local currency or U.S. dollars?
- Are taxes, duties, shipping, insurance, or customs fees included?
- Does my credit card offer purchase protection or dispute rights?

Do not rely on verbal promises. Get everything in writing, including the store’s full legal name, address, phone number, email, item description, and refund terms.

### The art auction and collectible trap

Cruise art auctions can be entertaining. Free champagne, lively auctioneers, and framed works create excitement. The risk is not that every art purchase is fake. The risk is that travelers may misunderstand value, scarcity, shipping fees, framing costs, or resale potential.

If you like a piece of art and can afford it as decoration, that is different from buying it as an investment. Be cautious of claims such as:

- “This will only increase in value.”
- “You can easily resell it.”
- “This appraisal proves the bargain.”
- “There are only a few left, and this is your last chance.”

### Script: art and collectibles

> “I buy art only because I want to live with it, not as an investment. I’m going to wait before deciding.”

A legitimate seller may be disappointed. A high-pressure seller may become aggressive. Do not reward aggression with your card.

### Shore excursions and unofficial operators

Cruise passengers often have a limited time in port. Scammers know that missing the ship is a terrifying thought. Some unofficial tour operators may be perfectly reputable; others may overpromise, overcrowd vehicles, skip promised stops, or demand extra fees mid-tour.

Before booking a shore excursion outside the cruise line:

- Read recent reviews from multiple sources.
- Confirm whether the operator guarantees return to ship on time.
- Know the ship’s all-aboard time and keep it in writing.
- Carry the port agent information provided by the ship.
- Do not surrender your passport unless clearly required by law or ship procedure.
- Pay in a way that offers a record.
- Avoid operators who approach aggressively at the pier and refuse written terms.

If you are mobility-limited, ask specific questions: Are there stairs? Is the van high? Are restrooms available? How far is the walk? A vague “no problem” is not enough.

### Group tour “factory visits” and demonstration sales

Many tours include stops at carpet workshops, leather stores, perfume factories, tea houses, pottery demonstrations, or jewelry centers. These can be educational and enjoyable. They can also become sales rooms.

A common pattern is hospitality first: tea, a chair, a demonstration, friendly conversation. Then the tone changes. A salesperson brings out higher-priced items. A manager appears. The door may not be locked, but the social pressure feels like a wall.

Seniors may feel rude leaving after accepting tea. Remember: hospitality is often part of the sales process. You are allowed to leave.

### Script: leaving a high-pressure demonstration

> “Thank you for the demonstration. I’m not buying today. I’m returning to the bus now.”

Repeat it without explaining. Explanations invite debate.

### Vacation clubs, timeshares, and “free” travel presentations

Some cruise ports, resorts, and tourist areas advertise free meals, gifts, tours, or discounted excursions in exchange for attending a presentation. The presentation may involve a timeshare, vacation club, travel membership, or resale program. These arrangements can be complicated, expensive, and difficult to cancel.

Warning signs include:

- You are told the offer is good only today.
- You are discouraged from reading the contract slowly.
- You are told not to discuss it with family or a financial advisor.
- The salesperson focuses on monthly payment rather than total cost.
- Promises are made verbally but not included in writing.
- You are asked to finance the purchase immediately.

Do not sign a major travel contract while tired, hungry, or on vacation excitement. If you are interested, take the documents home and review them. If the company says that is impossible, walk away.

### Fellow traveler scams

Not all pressure comes from salespeople. Sometimes a fellow traveler becomes unusually friendly and then introduces an opportunity: a private guide, investment, charity, medical fundraiser, jewelry deal, or “friend’s shop.” This overlaps with the romance and companionship scams discussed later, but it can begin in a cruise dining room or tour bus.

Be friendly without becoming financially entangled. Do not lend money, co-sign, carry packages, move funds, or receive deposits for someone you just met. Do not let a new acquaintance use your phone, email, room, or credit card.

### Script: money request from a fellow traveler

> “I’m sorry you’re dealing with that, but I don’t lend money or handle financial matters while traveling. Please contact the cruise staff, tour director, or your family.”

### Checklist: before buying on a cruise or tour

Use this checklist for any purchase over an amount you would be upset to lose.

- Have I had at least one hour away from the salesperson?
- Do I understand the total cost in my home currency?
- Is the return policy in writing?
- Do I know shipping, customs, duty, insurance, and delivery terms?
- Is the item described in detail on the receipt?
- Have I checked independent prices?
- Am I buying because I want it, not because I feel trapped?
- Would I still buy it if my adult child or trusted friend were standing beside me?
- Am I paying by credit card rather than cash, wire, gift card, or payment app?

### Caregiver note: planning for pressure points

Before a cruise or tour, talk about shopping pressure in a respectful way. Do not say, “Don’t get tricked.” Say:

> “These trips sometimes include high-pressure shopping stops. Let’s agree on a purchase limit. Anything over that amount gets a 24-hour pause.”

Help the traveler set a daily spending plan. If appropriate, arrange card alerts. Encourage them to text a photo of any major item, receipt, or contract before purchasing. If the traveler values independence, frame this as a travel habit, not supervision.

### Recovery steps after a pressured purchase

If you bought something and now regret it:

1. Read the receipt and return policy immediately.
2. Contact the seller in writing as soon as possible.
3. Contact the cruise shopping program or tour company if the store was recommended.
4. Save all documents, photos, packaging, appraisals, and messages.
5. Contact your credit card issuer promptly if the item was misrepresented or never delivered.
6. If you signed a timeshare or vacation club contract, look for cancellation rights and deadlines, which may be short and vary by jurisdiction. Consider getting qualified legal advice; do not rely on the salesperson’s explanation.
7. Report suspected fraud to consumer protection authorities when appropriate.

### Chapter takeaway

Cruises and group tours are enjoyable when you remember that convenience is not the same as neutrality. A recommended shop can still be expensive. A friendly group can still create pressure. Your best protection is a written record, a cooling-off pause, and the confidence to say, “Not today.”

## Chapter 15: Restaurants, Bars, Cafes, and Menus

Food is one of travel’s pleasures. A bowl of soup in a small cafe, fresh fruit at a market, a pastry near a museum, dinner with a view—these moments become part of the story you bring home. Scams around food and drink are especially upsetting because they invade a place of relaxation. They can also be subtle. A menu has no prices. A server brings items you did not order. A card disappears behind the counter. A stranger at a bar becomes very friendly. A QR code leads to a fake payment page.

This chapter is not meant to make you afraid of restaurants. It is meant to help you enjoy them with eyes open.

> **Quick Take**
> - Confirm prices before ordering; do not order anything labeled “market price” without an estimate.
> - Ask whether unordered bread, water, or appetizers carry a charge before eating them.
> - Keep your card in sight during payment; check the amount and currency before approving.
> - Do not follow a stranger to an unfamiliar bar or club, even when invited charmingly.
> - Keep bags closed and attached to you; do not place phones on the cafe table edge.

### The menu without prices

In tourist areas, some restaurants display menus outside with attractive photos but unclear prices. Others provide a menu in one language with lower prices and another with higher prices. Some list seafood, specials, or drinks by weight, bottle, or “market price” without explaining the cost. The surprise comes when the bill arrives.

Before ordering, look for prices. If a dish is listed as market price, ask the approximate cost. If the answer is vague, choose something else. If a server suggests a special, ask:

> “What is the price for that dish?”

This is not rude. It is normal.

### Script: unclear menu pricing

> “Before I order, could you please show me the price on the menu or write it down?”

If the restaurant will not give a price, leave politely.

### The “free” appetizer, bread, water, or limoncello

In some countries, bread, olives, water, table service, or small appetizers may have a charge. In others, they are complimentary. The problem is when a server presents something as free or automatic and charges an inflated amount.

When something arrives that you did not order, ask:

> “Is this complimentary, or is there a charge?”

If there is a charge and you do not want it, say:

> “No thank you. Please remove it.”

Do this before eating or drinking it. Customs vary, and not every charge is dishonest. The key is clarity.

### Card handling and payment safety

In many places, servers bring a portable card terminal to the table. In others, you may pay at a counter. Avoid letting your card disappear from sight when possible. Skimming devices and double charges can happen anywhere, though most restaurants are honest.

Check the amount on the terminal before tapping or inserting your card. Watch the currency. Sometimes a machine offers “dynamic currency conversion,” letting you pay in U.S. dollars rather than local currency. This may come with an unfavorable exchange rate. Often, choosing local currency is better, but check your card’s terms and fees.

Keep receipts until charges post correctly. Use card alerts when traveling.

### Script: card terminal

> “Please bring the card machine to the table, or I’ll come with you to the register.”

If that is not possible, consider paying cash for small meals, but be aware that cash offers little recourse if overcharged.

### QR code menu and payment scams

QR codes became common in restaurants. A real QR code may open the menu. A fake sticker placed over the real code may lead to a phishing site, fake payment page, or app download.

Before scanning:

- Look for tampering, stickers, or a code that seems placed oddly.
- Ask for a paper menu if available.
- If the code asks you to create an account, enter a card, or download an app just to view a menu, pause.
- Use the restaurant’s official website if you can verify it.

Do not enter bank passwords, email passwords, or one-time codes from a menu link. A menu does not need that information.

### Inflated bills and unordered items

A common restaurant scam is the bill with extra drinks, larger portions, service charges, or dishes you did not order. Sometimes it is a simple mistake. Sometimes tourists are targeted because they are unlikely to argue in another language.

Review the bill before paying. If there is a service charge, ask whether it is mandatory and whether tip is included. Customs differ widely. Do not assume your home tipping rules apply everywhere.

### Script: bill correction

> “I’m sorry, we did not order this item. Please correct the bill.”

If language is a barrier, point to the item and shake your head calmly. Use a translation app if needed. Stay polite, but do not be rushed.

### The bar friend and the enormous tab

Some travelers are drawn into bars or clubs by a friendly stranger, flirtatious companion, or promoter. The person suggests “just one drink.” The menu is unclear. Drinks arrive for the table. When the bill comes, it is shocking. Security may pressure the traveler to pay.

This can affect seniors too, especially widowed or solo travelers who are enjoying attention and conversation. It can happen to men and women. The scammer may be charming, sympathetic, or romantic.

Protect yourself with simple rules:

- Choose the venue yourself or from a trusted recommendation.
- Check prices before ordering.
- Do not allow strangers to order for you.
- Keep your drink in sight.
- Leave if you feel steered, isolated, or pressured.
- If threatened, prioritize safety and seek help from hotel staff, local police, or the nearest embassy or consulate if abroad and the situation is serious.

### Script: invitation to an unknown bar

> “Thank you, but I already have plans. I don’t go to unfamiliar bars with people I just met.”

### Food delivery and fake restaurant listings

Travelers increasingly use delivery apps or online searches. Fake restaurant listings can copy photos and menus from real restaurants, take payment, and never deliver. Other scams involve calls after an order claiming the payment failed and asking for card details.

Use reputable apps, check reviews, and avoid paying outside the platform. If someone calls about your order, do not give full card details. Open the official app and check there.

### Distraction theft in cafes

Cafes are common places for bag theft. A person asks for directions while another takes a purse. A map is placed over a phone on the table. A child or adult spills something on you and offers to help clean it while an accomplice reaches for your bag. A chair-back purse disappears in seconds.

Keep your bag closed and attached to you or between your feet with a strap around your leg. Do not leave a phone on the table edge. Keep wallets in front pockets or secured bags. If someone creates a commotion, put a hand on your belongings before engaging.

### Checklist: safer dining

- Look at prices before ordering.
- Ask whether unordered items are complimentary.
- Keep your card in sight when possible.
- Check the amount and currency before approving payment.
- Review the bill for extra items.
- Avoid unfamiliar bars with strangers who approach you.
- Keep bags and phones secured.
- Use official apps or verified restaurant websites for delivery.
- Save receipts until charges are correct.

### Caregiver note: dining confidence

Some older travelers avoid speaking up because they do not want to appear difficult. Practice a few phrases before the trip:

- “What is the price?”
- “No thank you.”
- “Please remove this.”
- “I need a receipt.”
- “I did not order this.”

If memory or vision is a concern, encourage the traveler to photograph menus, bills, and receipts. A photo can help later if a charge is disputed.

### Recovery steps after a restaurant or bar scam

1. Move to a safe public place.
2. Keep the receipt and take photos of menus or posted prices if safe.
3. Contact your card issuer if you were overcharged or charged twice.
4. Report serious threats, theft, or assault to local authorities.
5. If abroad and you are a victim of crime, the U.S. Department of State says consular officers can help you contact local police, medical services, family, or other resources, although they cannot act as your attorney or pay bills for you [^state-dept-victims-crime].
6. Tell your hotel or tour director; they may know local patterns and can help you avoid further risk.

### Chapter takeaway

A good meal should not require blind trust. Ask prices, keep control of payment, secure your belongings, and be cautious when friendliness comes with a destination you did not choose.


# Part V — Friendly Strangers, New Friends, and Relationship Pressure

Some travel scams begin with a person, not a payment screen. This part covers the friendly stranger, the sudden companion, and the new relationship that becomes pressure. The aim is not to distrust kindness. The aim is to notice when kindness asks you to give up privacy, money, documents, boundaries, or judgment.

## Chapter 16: The Friendly Stranger Pattern

A woman in a blue scarf steps beside you outside the museum and smiles as if you have met before. “Beautiful jacket,” she says. “You are from the U.S.? My aunt lives in Chicago.” Before you can decide whether to answer, she is pointing toward a side street. “The main entrance is closed today. Come, I show you the better way.”

Nothing about this moment is automatically criminal. Travel is richer because strangers can be kind. A local may point out the right bus, a waiter may warn you about a long line, and another traveler may offer to take your photo. The friendly stranger scam works because it borrows the shape of ordinary kindness. The goal is not to make you cold or suspicious. The goal is to help you recognize when friendliness has turned into a script.

> **Quick Take**
> - A “free” gift, bracelet, flower, prayer card, or souvenir may become a demand for money.
> - Friendly pressure is still pressure.
> - You can be polite without stopping, explaining, donating, or following.
> - Do not follow a new acquaintance away from public areas.
> - Keep your hands, bag, phone, and attention under your own control.

### How the pattern works

The friendly stranger pattern usually has four stages.

First comes the warm opening: a compliment, a shared hometown, a joke, a question, or an offer of help. Second comes the small obligation: “Hold this,” “Sign here,” “Let me show you,” “Just look,” or “Take this gift.” Third comes emotional pressure: disappointment, flattery, religious language, urgency, embarrassment, or a claim that you have already agreed. Fourth comes the payoff: cash, a card payment, a donation, a commission at a shop, or a distraction while someone else reaches for your belongings.

Common versions include bracelets tied onto wrists, flowers pressed into hands, fake monks asking for donations, petition boards used to hide a thief’s hands, “found ring” offers, art students leading visitors to overpriced galleries, helpful guides who become paid guides after the fact, and strangers who insist your destination is closed so they can redirect you to a taxi, store, or tour.

The mechanics are simple. Once an item touches your hand or body, many people feel they owe something. Once you stop walking, the scammer controls more of the conversation. Once you explain, you create openings for debate. Once you follow, you may leave the safe flow of other travelers, cameras, staff, and exits.

### Why it can be harder for older travelers

Many older adults were raised to value courtesy, patience, and not making a scene. Those are strengths. Scammers exploit them by making refusal feel rude. They may also notice if you are managing a cane, hearing aids, bifocals, medication timing, fatigue, or a spouse who needs assistance. None of this means seniors are weak or gullible. It means a practiced manipulator may choose moments when anyone’s attention is divided.

A couple leaving a cafe after lunch may be checking the map, looking for a restroom, and calculating whether the next museum has elevators. A solo traveler may be trying not to appear lost. A grandparent traveling with family may be watching a child and a purse at the same time. Scam artists look for these normal human moments.

A useful sentence to keep in mind is: **I can be kind without participating.**

### Red flags

The following signs do not prove a scam, but they tell you to slow down and protect your space:

- The person touches you, ties something onto you, or places an object in your hand.
- The person walks with you after you have not invited them to do so.
- The offer of help turns into “Come this way.”
- The person says the official entrance, ticket office, hotel, ATM, taxi stand, or bus stop is closed, broken, unsafe, or “only for locals.”
- They want you to sign a clipboard while standing close to your bag.
- They ask where you are staying, whether you are alone, how long you will be in town, or whether this is your first visit.
- They react dramatically to a calm no: hurt feelings, anger, religious guilt, or “You promised.”
- A second person suddenly appears to agree, translate, or hurry you.

Green flags look different. A genuine local gives brief directions and continues with their day. A staff member helps from an official desk. A museum volunteer wears clear identification and does not pressure you to leave the public area. A gift is clearly from a business, tour, or event and does not become a surprise fee.

### Safe exits that preserve dignity

You do not need a perfect speech. You need a short one. Long explanations invite argument.

**Script ladder: friendly stranger**

1. **Soft no:** “No thank you. I am not stopping.”
2. **Repeat:** “No thank you. I said I am not stopping.”
3. **Boundary:** “Please step back.”
4. **Public help:** “I need assistance here, please.”
5. **Exit:** Move toward a store, hotel lobby, ticket window, cafe counter, security guard, family group, or other public place.

Use your body before your words. Keep walking if you already know you do not want the interaction. Put your hand over your bag zipper. Keep your phone in your pocket or crossbody bag unless you are using it. If someone blocks you, angle around rather than pushing through. If you are with a companion, agree in advance that either person can say, “We’re leaving now,” and the other will not debate.

If something is placed on you, do not panic. If it is safe, remove it and put it down on a nearby surface rather than handing over cash. Say, “I did not agree to buy this.” Then leave. If the person becomes aggressive, prioritize distance and help over proving a point.

### Micro-scene: the bracelet

At a busy square, a man approaches a retired teacher and says, “For good luck, madam.” He loops a thread bracelet toward her wrist. She smiles, steps back, and keeps her hands close to her body.

“No thank you. I am not stopping.”

“It is free. Free gift.”

“No thank you.” She turns toward the museum entrance where a uniformed attendant is checking tickets. Her friend, who has practiced this with her, says, “We’re going inside now.” They do not scold the man. They do not explain that they are on a fixed income. They do not discuss whether the bracelet is pretty. They leave the performance before the price appears.

That is the victory: not confrontation, but exit.

### If you already paid or were distracted

First, be kind to yourself. These scams work because they are rehearsed. A small loss is not a character flaw; it is a travel problem to contain.

Move to a safe place and check your essentials: phone, passport, wallet, cards, hotel key, medications, glasses, and mobility aids. If a card or wallet is missing, lock or cancel cards immediately through the bank app or the number on your backup list. If your phone is missing, use a companion’s phone, hotel computer, or official store to lock the device and protect email and banking accounts. If you were led to a shop and pressured into a large purchase, keep receipts, take photos of the storefront if safe, and contact your card issuer promptly to ask about dispute options.

Tell one trusted person what happened. Shame helps scammers; support helps travelers recover. Write down the location, time, description, and any names or payment details while memory is fresh. If the loss involves theft, threats, passport loss, or a large amount of money, ask your hotel, tour leader, cruise desk, or local police about reporting. U.S. citizens abroad can also contact the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate for guidance when documents are lost or a crime has occurred.

### Chapter takeaway

You do not need to judge every stranger’s heart. You only need to notice behavior. Kind people allow you to say no. Safe helpers do not demand secrecy, speed, cash, or control of your body and belongings. Your best travel manners are simple: smile if you wish, decline clearly, keep moving, and choose official channels when you need help.

A good rule for the day bag and the heart is the same: keep what matters under your own control.

Travel is social. You may meet kind strangers, generous locals, helpful staff, and other travelers who become real friends. This part is not here to make you colder. It is here to protect the line between connection and access.

## Chapter 17: Romance, Companionship, and New Friend Scams While Traveling

Romance scams are not limited to dating apps or online messages. They can begin in airports, hotels, cruise ships, group tours, religious pilgrimages, volunteer trips, language classes, cafes, or social events. The FTC and FBI both warn that romance scammers build emotional trust and then ask for money, gifts, transfers, account access, or help moving funds [^ftc-romance-scams] [^fbi-romance-scams]. Travel can accelerate the process because people are away from their usual routines and may feel more open to new experiences.

A scammer may be younger or older, local or foreign, polished or humble. The story changes, but the pattern is familiar: intense attention, rapid emotional closeness, isolation from outside advice, and a financial request.

> **Quick Take**
> - A new traveling friendship that asks for money, account access, or secrecy is a scam pattern, not a relationship.
> - Do not lend funds, share banking, ship packages, or carry items for someone you recently met.
> - Real emergencies have official channels — police, embassies, hospitals, banks. Private favors rarely belong.
> - If a new acquaintance asks you to keep things secret from family, talk to family before sending anything.
> - Physical safety first: meet in public, arrange your own transportation, do not share your room number.

### Why travel increases vulnerability

Travel can make even careful people more trusting. You may be:

- Away from family and friends who would normally give perspective.
- Feeling lonely, adventurous, or newly independent.
- Navigating unfamiliar language and customs.
- Grateful to someone who helps you.
- Flattered by attention after a difficult season of life.
- More willing to make exceptions because “this trip is special.”

None of this means you are foolish. It means you are human. Scammers are skilled at reading needs and offering exactly what seems missing.

### The fast friendship pattern

A person you just met becomes unusually attached. They want to spend all their time with you. They tell you personal stories quickly. They say they feel a rare connection. They may discourage you from including others.

Common phrases include:

- “I feel like I’ve known you forever.”
- “Age is just a number.”
- “You understand me better than anyone.”
- “Please don’t tell your family yet; they won’t understand.”
- “I’m embarrassed to ask, but I have no one else.”

The words may feel wonderful. Enjoy kindness, but keep boundaries.

### Boundary rule

Do not give or lend money, share account access, move funds, buy gift cards, ship packages, co-sign anything, or provide passport/identity documents to a person you recently met while traveling.

### The crisis request

After emotional trust comes urgency. The person may claim:

- A wallet was stolen.
- A child or parent needs medical care.
- A business deal is stuck.
- A visa, passport, or permit fee must be paid.
- They need a temporary loan until the bank opens.
- They were robbed and cannot tell police.
- They need your bank account to receive funds.
- They want you to buy crypto, gift cards, or wire money.

The FTC warns that requests for gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or payment apps are strong scam signals because these payments are hard to reverse [^ftc-romance-scams]. A real emergency should be handled through official channels: police, embassy or consulate, bank, travel insurer, family, tour director, hotel management, or local emergency services.

### Script: refusing a crisis money request

> “I’m sorry this happened. I can help you contact the police, your embassy, your family, or the hotel, but I cannot give money or use my accounts.”

If the person rejects official help and insists only you can solve it, be very cautious.

### The “carry this for me” request

Never carry packages, medicine, envelopes, jewelry, cash, or documents for someone you met while traveling. You may be told it is a gift, family item, charity donation, or business sample. You may be told it will save fees or help a sick relative. If the item contains illegal substances, undeclared goods, or stolen property, you could face serious consequences.

### Script: package request

> “I can’t carry anything through customs or security for another person. Please arrange shipping through an official service.”

No exception is worth the risk.

### The investment or business opportunity

A charming new friend may introduce a “safe” investment: foreign property, precious metals, crypto, art, import/export, a restaurant, or a charity. They may show screenshots of profits or introduce you to a supposed expert. Romance and investment fraud often overlap. The emotional relationship makes the financial pitch feel trustworthy.

Warning signs:

- Guaranteed returns.
- Pressure to act before you go home.
- Instructions to keep it secret.
- A website or app that only they recommended.
- Difficulty withdrawing a small test amount.
- Requests for taxes or fees before withdrawal.

Do not invest through a person you met on a trip. If genuinely interested, wait until home and consult independent professionals.

### The caregiver or helper who becomes controlling

Some seniors travel with paid companions, informal helpers, distant relatives, or new acquaintances. A helper may gradually take control: holding the passport, managing cash, answering questions for the traveler, isolating them from others, or pressuring them to change plans.

This may be a scam, exploitation, or an unsafe dependency. If you feel you cannot say no, cannot access your documents, or cannot call family privately, seek help from hotel management, tour staff, local authorities, or the nearest embassy or consulate if abroad.

Caregivers at home should watch for sudden changes during travel:

- The traveler stops calling privately.
- A new person answers messages.
- Money requests become frequent.
- The traveler seems confused, fearful, or secretive.
- Plans change to include someone unknown.

### Online romance while traveling

A traveler may use dating apps or social media abroad. Scammers may claim to be nearby but repeatedly avoid meeting, or they may meet briefly and then continue the relationship online. The same rules apply: do not send money, do not share financial information, and do not provide intimate photos that could be used for blackmail.

If someone threatens to share private images unless you pay, do not keep paying. Preserve evidence, block when safe, report to the platform, and seek help from law enforcement or trusted support. If you are abroad and feel unsafe, contact local authorities or the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate.

### Meeting safely

If you choose to meet someone new:

- Meet in a public place you choose.
- Tell a trusted person where you are going.
- Arrange your own transportation.
- Keep control of your drink and belongings.
- Do not share your hotel room number.
- Do not leave your passport, wallet, or phone unattended.
- Leave if the person pressures you to change locations.

### Script: keeping the meeting public

> “I’m enjoying the conversation, but I only meet in public places and I arrange my own ride.”

A respectful person will accept that.

### Checklist: is this connection becoming unsafe?

Ask yourself:

- Is the relationship moving unusually fast?
- Have I been asked to keep secrets from family or friends?
- Has money, banking, crypto, gift cards, shipping, or documents come up?
- Does the person have repeated emergencies?
- Do they resist official solutions?
- Do they make me feel guilty for setting boundaries?
- Would I be embarrassed to tell a trusted person the full story?

If you answer yes to any, pause. Talk to someone outside the relationship.

### Caregiver note: respond without shame

If an older adult tells you about a travel romance or new friend, do not begin with ridicule or panic. Shame drives people back to the scammer. Try:

> “I’m glad you told me. You deserve kindness and companionship. Let’s just make sure no one is pressuring you for money or secrecy.”

If money has already been sent, focus on immediate steps: contact the bank or card issuer, stop further payments, preserve messages, and report. Emotional recovery may take longer than financial recovery. The person may grieve the relationship even after learning it was fraudulent.

### Recovery steps after romance or companionship fraud

1. Stop sending money or information.
2. Do not warn the scammer before securing accounts; they may intensify pressure.
3. Contact banks, card issuers, and payment platforms immediately.
4. Change passwords and enable stronger account security.
5. Preserve messages, receipts, usernames, phone numbers, photos, and transaction records.
6. Report to the dating app, social platform, FTC, FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center if appropriate, and local law enforcement.
7. If abroad and threatened or victimized, contact local authorities and the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate.
8. Tell a trusted person and consider counseling or victim support.

### Chapter takeaway

Travel friendship is one of life’s joys. The test is not whether someone seems kind. The test is whether they respect boundaries. Real affection does not require secrecy, money, account access, or rushed decisions.


# Part VI — Health, Documents, Money, and Emergency Recovery

Some travel problems are real emergencies, not scams. The goal in this part is to separate the real problem from the person trying to exploit it. Safety comes first. Paperwork comes second. Shame does not help either one.

## Chapter 18: Medical, Pharmacy, and Emergency Assistance Scams

Illness away from home is frightening. Even a minor problem—a rash, stomach illness, lost medication, dental pain, a fall—can feel overwhelming in an unfamiliar place. Scammers exploit that fear. They may pose as doctors, ambulance staff, pharmacy workers, insurance representatives, or helpful strangers. They may sell counterfeit medicine, demand cash before providing information, or steer travelers to overpriced clinics.

Most medical professionals are legitimate and compassionate. Your task is to prepare enough that you can find real help without relying on the loudest person in the room.

> **Quick Take**
> - Prepare before the trip: medication list, insurance limits, evacuation coverage, and emergency contacts.
> - Use licensed channels for medicine — clinics, pharmacies, insurer assistance lines, embassies — not market stalls or strangers.
> - For non-urgent care, ask for cost estimates and itemized bills before treatment.
> - In a true emergency, call the local emergency number first and verify charges later.
> - Never share insurance, passport, or card details with an unexpected incoming caller.

### Before the trip: medical preparation

Preparation reduces panic. Before traveling, especially internationally, consider these steps:

- Talk with your health care professional about whether you are fit for the trip and whether any vaccines, preventive medicines, or precautions are recommended.
- Bring enough prescription medicine for the trip plus extra in case of delays, when allowed.
- Keep medicines in original labeled containers when possible.
- Carry a medication list with generic and brand names, doses, allergies, major conditions, and emergency contacts.
- Check whether your medicines are legal and available in your destination country. Rules differ.
- Ask your insurer what is covered away from home.
- Understand that Medicare coverage outside the U.S. is generally limited, with only certain exceptions.
- Consider travel medical insurance and medical evacuation coverage, especially for international trips, cruises, remote areas, or complex health conditions.

The State Department and CDC both provide travel health and insurance guidance, but your personal situation matters [^state-dept-medicine-health] [^cdc-travel-insurance]. Verify details before departure.

### Fake pharmacy and counterfeit medicine risks

In some destinations, pharmacies are reliable and highly regulated. In others, counterfeit or substandard medicine is a known risk. A traveler who loses medication may be desperate, and that is when bad sellers appear.

Warning signs:

- Medicine sold loose without packaging or labeling.
- A seller who does not ask about prescription requirements.
- Prices that seem too good to be true.
- Packaging with misspellings, broken seals, or unfamiliar markings.
- Pressure to buy quickly or in large quantities.
- Offers from market stalls, hotel strangers, taxi drivers, or social media contacts.

If you need medicine abroad, use licensed medical channels first: a licensed doctor, licensed pharmacy, cruise medical center, travel insurer assistance line, or embassy or consulate medical-services list. Hotel or tour staff may help you locate official services, but they should not advise you on medication choice, substitutions, dosage, or safety. A U.S. embassy or consulate cannot provide medical care, but it may help you find medical services.

### Script: seeking a pharmacy safely

> “I need a licensed pharmacy or medical clinic. Please help me find an official location, not a private seller.”

### The inflated clinic or unnecessary treatment scam

Some clinics near tourist areas may be legitimate but expensive. Others may recommend unnecessary tests, demand large upfront payments, or refuse to provide clear bills. Medical pricing varies widely by country, and high cost alone does not prove fraud. The concern is lack of transparency and pressure.

When possible, ask:

- What is the doctor’s name and qualification?
- What is the proposed treatment?
- Is it urgent, or can it wait?
- What will it cost?
- Can I have an itemized bill?
- Will you provide records in writing?
- Can I contact my insurer or assistance company before treatment?

In a true emergency, get care first. Do not delay lifesaving treatment because of paperwork. But for non-urgent care, a pause can prevent exploitation.

### Script: non-urgent treatment estimate

> “I want to understand the cost and the reason for each test. Please provide an estimate and let me contact my insurance assistance line.”

A reputable provider should not object to reasonable questions when the situation is not immediately life-threatening.

### Fake ambulance and emergency transport

In some tourist areas, a “private ambulance” or driver may appear after a fall or illness and insist on taking the traveler to a particular clinic. The clinic may be expensive or connected to the driver. Again, not every private ambulance is fraudulent; emergency systems differ by country. Do not delay emergency care to investigate a possible scam. For chest pain, stroke signs, severe breathing trouble, major injury, loss of consciousness, or rapidly worsening symptoms, call the local emergency number first.

If the situation is not immediately life-threatening and the traveler is conscious, ask local emergency services, hotel staff, cruise staff, or tour leaders for help arranging appropriate transport through official channels.

If you are with a companion, one person should stay with the traveler while another verifies the destination and contacts the insurer or assistance line.

### Insurance impersonation and medical phishing

After a travel medical incident, scammers may call or email pretending to be an insurer, clinic, government office, or assistance company. They may ask for passport numbers, insurance IDs, credit card numbers, medical records, or one-time codes.

Use the FTC phishing rule: do not trust unexpected messages simply because they use familiar names [^ftc-phishing]. Contact the insurer or assistance company using the number on your policy documents or official app. Do not click links in unexpected emails or texts.

### Script: insurance verification

> “I don’t provide personal or payment information on incoming calls. I’ll call the number on my policy card and ask for this case.”

### Medical evacuation confusion

Medical evacuation can be extremely expensive. Some travelers assume any travel insurance includes evacuation to the hospital or country of their choice. Policies differ. Some cover transport to the nearest appropriate facility, not necessarily home. Some require pre-authorization when possible. Some exclude pre-existing conditions unless requirements are met.

This is not a scam by itself, but misunderstanding coverage can become a crisis. Before travel, read the policy and ask:

- What medical expenses are covered?
- Is emergency evacuation covered?
- Who decides where I am transported?
- Are pre-existing conditions covered?
- Do I need to call before receiving non-emergency care?
- Are cruises, adventure activities, or remote areas covered?
- What documents are needed for a claim?

### Cruise ship medical bills

Cruise ships have medical facilities, but care is usually not free. Charges may be added to your shipboard account. Serious cases may require evacuation or care in a port country. Before cruising, ask your insurer about cruise coverage and medical evacuation. Medicare generally has limited coverage outside the U.S., though certain shipboard or nearby U.S. situations may be exceptions; verify current rules with Medicare and your plan [^medicare-outside-u-s].

If treated onboard, ask for itemized records before disembarking. If you are too ill, a companion should request copies.

### The “helpful stranger” at the hospital

A person at a hospital or clinic may offer to translate, speed paperwork, contact family, or arrange payment. Some are legitimate staff or volunteers. Others may demand fees, steal information, or steer you to private services.

Ask for identification. Use official hospital interpreters when available. If abroad, contact your travel insurer assistance line, tour company, hotel, or embassy or consulate for help finding reliable support.

### Script: accepting help carefully

> “Thank you. Are you employed by the hospital? Please show me identification, and I will confirm at the desk.”

### Checklist: medical safety abroad

Carry or store securely:

- Passport copy and insurance information.
- Medication list with generic names.
- Allergy and condition list.
- Emergency contacts.
- Travel insurance assistance number.
- Doctor contact information at home.
- Copies of prescriptions when appropriate.
- A small amount of local currency for transportation, while keeping most funds secure.

During a medical problem:

- Use official emergency numbers when needed.
- Contact your insurer or assistance company when possible.
- Ask for itemized bills and medical records.
- Do not buy medicine from informal sellers.
- Do not provide personal information to unexpected callers.
- Tell a trusted person what is happening.

### Caregiver note: health planning for senior travelers

Caregivers can help by creating a one-page medical travel sheet. Keep it clear, large-print, and updated. Include:

- Full name and date of birth.
- Emergency contacts.
- Medicines and doses.
- Allergies.
- Major conditions.
- Doctor contact.
- Insurance and travel insurance numbers.
- Mobility needs.
- Communication needs, such as hearing aids or preferred language.

Do not overload the traveler with a thick folder they will not use. A simple sheet can save time and reduce confusion.

### Recovery steps after a medical scam or overcharge

1. Get to a safe place and make sure medical needs are addressed.
2. Save all bills, receipts, prescriptions, packaging, and records.
3. Contact your insurer or travel insurance company promptly.
4. Contact your card issuer if you suspect fraud or duplicate charges.
5. Report counterfeit medicine or dangerous treatment to local authorities where appropriate.
6. If abroad and you need help finding resources, contact the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate. The State Department’s Overseas Citizens Services can be reached from the U.S. or Canada at 888-407-4747 and from abroad at 202-501-4444 [^state-dept-help-abroad]. These numbers and procedures can change, so verify current information before and during travel.
7. Follow up with your regular doctor when you return home.

### Chapter takeaway

Medical fear makes people vulnerable. Preparation gives you choices. In a non-life-threatening situation, pause long enough to verify the provider, understand the cost, contact insurance, and use official help.

## Chapter 19: Lost Passport, Lost Wallet, and Real Emergency Recovery

Some travel days turn suddenly. A passport is missing from the hotel safe. A wallet is gone after a crowded train. A purse disappears from a cafe chair. A phone is left in a taxi. A traveler falls, becomes confused, or is separated from the group.

These are real emergencies, not just scams. But emergencies attract opportunists: fake police, fake document fixers, fake embassy helpers, fake money transfer requests, and strangers who offer rescue for a fee.

This chapter gives a calm recovery plan. It is written especially for the first hour, when panic can make a bad situation worse.

> **Quick Take**
> - Safety first, paperwork second — sit down in a public place before solving anything.
> - For a lost or stolen U.S. passport, contact the nearest embassy or consulate; never use unofficial “document fixers.”
> - Lock or cancel cards immediately through your bank app or saved issuer numbers.
> - A family verification phrase prevents secondary scams targeting relatives at home.
> - Document everything (times, names, report numbers, receipts) while details are fresh.

### First principle: safety before paperwork

If you are injured, threatened, or in immediate danger, move to safety and contact local emergency services. Documents can be replaced. Cards can be canceled. Your safety comes first.

If you are safe but shaken, sit down in a public place such as a hotel lobby, police station, airport information desk, bank, or staffed cafe. Take a breath. Do not let a stranger rush you into a car, office, or private room to “solve everything.”

### Script: stopping a fake helper

> “Thank you, but I’m going to handle this through the hotel, police, bank, or embassy.”

Repeat as needed.

### The first-hour emergency checklist

Use this order when a passport, wallet, or phone is lost or stolen:

1. **Get safe.** Leave crowds, dark streets, or isolated areas.
2. **Check carefully.** Look in inner pockets, under seats, hotel safe, bathroom, and bags before declaring items stolen.
3. **Tell your travel companion, tour director, or hotel.** Do not manage confusion alone.
4. **Secure money.** Cancel or freeze missing cards using official bank numbers or apps.
5. **Secure phone accounts.** Use device-finding tools if available; change key passwords if the phone is gone.
6. **Report theft when appropriate.** A police report may be needed for insurance, passport replacement, or card disputes.
7. **Contact the embassy or consulate for a lost or stolen U.S. passport abroad.** The State Department provides guidance and can issue a replacement passport, often limited-validity in urgent travel situations.
8. **Contact family or a trusted person.** Use a verified number, not one supplied by a stranger.
9. **Document everything.** Times, places, names, report numbers, receipts, and screenshots.

### Lost or stolen U.S. passport abroad

The U.S. Department of State advises Americans whose passport is lost or stolen abroad to contact the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate. You generally cannot travel internationally without a valid passport. The consular section can explain replacement steps. Requirements may include proof of identity, proof of U.S. citizenship if available, a passport photo, forms, fees, and a police report depending on circumstances and country.

Details can change by country and situation. Some embassies require appointments except emergencies. Some replacement passports issued quickly may be limited-validity and may need to be replaced again after returning home. If you receive a limited-validity emergency passport, ask the embassy or consulate and your airline whether it will be accepted for your remaining route, including any transit countries.

Before travel, write down or print:

- The address and phone number of the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate for each destination.
- State Department Overseas Citizens Services numbers: 888-407-4747 from the U.S. or Canada, and 202-501-4444 from abroad.
- A copy of your passport information page.
- A passport photo if you want extra preparedness.

Store this separately from your passport.

### Script: calling the embassy or consulate

> “I am a U.S. citizen traveling in [city/country]. My passport was lost or stolen. I am safe now. I need instructions for replacing it and whether I should file a police report first.”

If you are also a victim of crime, say so clearly.

### Fake passport fixers

After a passport loss, someone may say they can get you a replacement faster than the embassy, “for a fee.” They may claim to know an official, offer a fake stamp, or ask for passport photos and personal details. Do not use unofficial document fixers. A fake document can create serious legal trouble.

Use only the embassy, consulate, or official local authorities as directed. If a hotel or tour director helps, they should help you contact official offices, not replace them.

### Lost wallet: cards, cash, and identification

A lost wallet can feel like losing your entire identity. Start with financial protection.

- Use your bank or card issuer’s official app to freeze cards if possible.
- Call the number on the card issuer’s official website or stored travel documents.
- Ask about emergency card replacement or cash assistance.
- Review recent transactions.
- Keep notes of case numbers.
- If your driver’s license or national ID was stolen, learn how to report it when you return home.

If you have no money, contact your bank, card issuer, travel insurer, tour company, or family. The embassy may help you contact family or friends and explain options, but it generally cannot replace private travel insurance, act as a bank, or pay your bills.

### Script: bank call after theft

> “I’m traveling in [country]. My wallet was lost or stolen at approximately [time]. Please freeze or cancel these cards, review recent charges, and tell me my emergency replacement options.”

### Fake police and official-looking demands

In some places, scammers impersonate police officers. They may ask to inspect your wallet for counterfeit bills, demand an immediate fine, or request your passport and cash. Real police procedures vary by country, so this can be confusing.

If approached by someone claiming to be police and you are unsure:

- Stay polite.
- Ask to see identification.
- Ask to go to the nearest police station, hotel desk, or public official area.
- Do not hand over your wallet for a “check.”
- Do not pay cash fines on the street unless you are certain this is legitimate local procedure.

### Script: uncertain police encounter

> “I want to cooperate. Please take me to the police station or let me call my hotel/embassy to confirm the procedure.”

If you feel physically threatened, prioritize safety.

### Lost phone: the modern travel emergency

A phone may contain boarding passes, maps, bank apps, email, photos of documents, and contact numbers. Losing it is both inconvenient and risky.

Before travel:

- Use a strong passcode, not just face or fingerprint unlock.
- Turn on device location features if you are comfortable doing so.
- Back up important information.
- Write key phone numbers on paper.
- Do not store passwords in an unprotected note.
- Know how to contact your mobile provider.

After loss:

1. Use a trusted device to locate, lock, or erase the phone if available.
2. Contact your mobile provider to suspend service or protect the number.
3. Change passwords for email, banking, and travel accounts, starting with email.
4. Watch for texts or emails claiming your phone has been found and asking you to click a link. This can be phishing.
5. Report theft if needed for insurance.

### Script: suspicious “found phone” message

> “I will not click links. If my phone has been found, I will contact the police station, hotel, taxi company, or official lost-and-found directly.”

### Emergency money without becoming a target

If you need funds, use official and traceable methods. Contact your bank, card issuer, travel insurer, or family directly. Be careful with strangers offering currency exchange, private loans, or “instant embassy help.”

If family sends money, agree on a verification phrase before the trip. Scammers sometimes contact relatives pretending to be a traveler in trouble. A simple family rule helps:

> “If anyone asks for emergency money in my name, ask for our code phrase and call me back on my known number or contact my hotel.”

This protects both the traveler and the family.

### Victim of crime abroad

The State Department explains that consular officers can assist U.S. citizens who are victims of crime abroad. Help may include replacing a stolen passport, contacting family or friends, explaining local criminal justice processes, providing information about local attorneys or medical care, and helping communicate with local authorities. They cannot investigate crimes, act as your lawyer, serve as official interpreters in all situations, or pay expenses.

If you are robbed, assaulted, or threatened:

- Get to safety.
- Seek medical care if needed.
- Contact local police when appropriate.
- Contact the nearest U.S. embassy or consulate.
- Preserve evidence and records.
- Contact your travel insurer.
- Tell a trusted person.

Avoid making promises to private “helpers” who say they can recover stolen property for a fee. Recovery scams often follow theft.

### The recovery scam after the emergency

After a loss, you may receive messages from people claiming they found your passport, wallet, phone, or bag. They may demand shipping fees, customs fees, courier fees, or verification codes. Some may be honest finders; others are scammers.

Verify through official lost-and-found offices, police stations, hotels, airlines, cruise lines, or transportation companies. Do not send one-time codes. Do not provide bank logins. If paying shipping for a recovered item, use the official organization’s process.

### A calm plan for separated travelers

Older couples or friends may become separated in crowds. Before the day begins, agree on:

- A meeting place if separated.
- A time to return to the hotel or ship.
- Whether each person carries hotel information.
- Who has a phone and backup battery.
- What to do if one person becomes confused or ill.

Carry a hotel card or write the hotel name and address on paper. If you have memory concerns, consider a discreet emergency contact card.

### Script: asking official help when separated

> “I am separated from my travel companion. I am staying at [hotel]. Please help me call the hotel or contact local police/tour staff.”

Choose uniformed staff, information desks, hotel employees, or police stations rather than random passersby.

### Caregiver note: emergency folder and consent

For senior travelers, especially those with medical conditions or cognitive concerns, prepare an emergency folder. Include:

- Passport copy.
- Travel insurance policy.
- Medication list.
- Emergency contacts.
- Itinerary and hotel addresses.
- Copies of credit card emergency numbers, not full card numbers unless stored securely.
- Embassy/consulate contacts.
- Any relevant medical directives or consent documents, if appropriate and legally valid.

Do not assume documents accepted at home will work abroad. Laws and procedures vary. For legal documents, get qualified advice before travel.

Also discuss privacy. A senior traveler may want help but not want every detail shared with adult children. Agree in advance on when a caregiver should be contacted: hospitalization, lost passport, missing wallet, police report, confusion, missed check-in, or request for emergency money.

### After returning home

Recovery does not end when the plane lands. Take these steps:

1. Review bank and card statements for at least several weeks.
2. Replace any limited-validity emergency passport if required.
3. File insurance claims promptly with documentation.
4. Change passwords if devices or documents were lost.
5. Consider credit monitoring or fraud alerts if identity documents were stolen.
6. Follow up with doctors for any medical incident.
7. Report scams to appropriate agencies.
8. Write down what happened while memory is fresh.
9. Update your travel plan so the next trip is safer.

Do not spend weeks blaming yourself. A calm review is useful; shame is not.

### Final emergency checklist

Before leaving home:

- Passport valid and copied.
- Embassy/consulate contacts printed.
- Insurance reviewed.
- Medicines packed correctly.
- Bank travel notices or alerts set if needed.
- Emergency contacts on paper.
- Backup payment method stored separately.
- Phone secured and backed up.
- Family verification phrase agreed upon.

During an emergency:

- Get safe.
- Use official channels.
- Freeze cards.
- Contact embassy/consulate for lost passport abroad.
- Contact insurer when medical or theft claims may apply.
- Keep records.
- Avoid private fixers.
- Tell a trusted person.

### Chapter takeaway

A lost passport, wallet, or phone is frightening, but it is recoverable. Scammers want panic. Your plan is the opposite: safety first, official channels second, documentation third, and trusted help throughout.

# Conclusion — Travel with Confidence

The middle and later stages of a trip often contain the most ordinary pleasures: sleeping well, sharing meals, meeting people, buying souvenirs, taking tours, and handling small surprises. Scams hide inside ordinary moments. That is why the best protection is not fear, but rhythm.

Pause before paying. Verify before trusting a message. Keep control of your documents. Ask prices. Use official channels. Tell someone when something feels wrong. Let real professionals help with real emergencies, and do not let strangers turn urgency into secrecy.

For seniors, travel should remain open, dignified, and joyful. Caution is not a retreat from the world. It is a way to stay in it longer, with confidence.

# Appendix D — Scripts Library

Practice three lines before departure: one soft refusal, one firm refusal, and one exit line. In a stressful moment, short sentences work better than perfect sentences.

## Declining Street Pressure

- **Soft:** “No thank you.”
- **Firm:** “No thank you. I am not stopping.”
- **Exit:** “Please step back. I am leaving now.”

## Verifying a Driver

“I need to check the plate and driver details before I get in.”

## Handling an Unclear Price

“Please write the total price before we start. If the price is not clear, I will not continue.”

## Refusing ATM Help

“No thank you. I do not accept help at ATMs.”

## Hotel Phone Payment Request

“I do not give payment information over an incoming call. I will come to the front desk.”

## Fake Official Pressure

- **Soft:** “I want to cooperate. Please speak slowly.”
- **Firm:** “Please show identification and write down the citation information.”
- **Verification, when safe and lawful:** “I will handle this through an official office or payment channel.”
- **Safety note:** Do not physically resist a verified or armed official. If the situation feels dangerous, prioritize safety and seek help afterward.

## High-Pressure Shop

“I do not make expensive purchases today. I will take the information and decide later.”

## New Friend Money Request

“I am sorry you are dealing with that. I do not send money or carry packages for people I have just met.”

## Lost Passport at Hotel Desk

“My passport has been lost or stolen. I need help contacting local police if required and the nearest embassy or consulate.”

## Calling a Card Issuer

“I am traveling abroad and my card may be compromised. Please freeze the card, review recent transactions, and tell me what documentation you need.”
