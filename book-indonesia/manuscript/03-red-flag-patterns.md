# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The seventy-three scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: A stranger who approaches you first is almost certainly running a script

This is the single most important habit you can take to Indonesia. The instant a smiling local steps into your path uninvited at an airport arrivals hall, a temple gate, a Komodo-tour pier, or a rice-terrace viewpoint, the interaction that follows is a rehearsed script. You are not being greeted. You are being sorted.

Indonesia's scam ecosystem is unusually verbal, and the openers are almost always warm. At Bali's Ngurah Rai Airport (DPS), a man in a polo shirt flashes a phone screen that looks like Grab and says "Your car this way, special fixed price." At Jakarta's Kota Tua, a man on a becak (pedicab) offers a "free orientation tour" and routes through four commission shops. At Borobudur's outer gate, a man with a lanyard tells you "sunrise slot is sold out — I can get you in through Manohara Hotel for 850,000 rupiah" when the official ticket is 400,000 at the real booth inside. In Ubud on Jalan Raya, a driver in a sarong leans out of a minivan with "taxi, only 100,000 to Tegallalang" — the posted rate at the real parking stand is 50,000, and the Grab app shows 35,000. On Gili Trawangan, a porter intercepts your bag at the pier and carries it four minutes to your bungalow — then demands 500,000 rupiah.

None of these interactions start with you asking for help. That is the tell. Indonesians are famously warm, but they do not generally accost foreign tourists to volunteer Grab rides, temple-ticket shortcuts, or "free" bicycle tours.

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: when any stranger starts a conversation with you at an airport, a pier, or the entrance to a famous site, reply "*tidak, terima kasih*" (no, thank you — pronounced *tee-dak, teh-ree-mah kah-see*) and keep walking. Do not break stride. Do not engage. If the person follows you, repeat the phrase once, louder. Indonesian politeness conventions do not require you to listen to a sales pitch, and walking away is not rude.

*You will see this pattern in: the fake-Grab airport approach (Bali, Jakarta, Lombok, Labuan Bajo, Batam), the Borobudur sunrise fake-ticket gambit (Yogyakarta), the Kota Tua becak "free tour" (Jakarta), the Ubud taxi-mafia on Jalan Raya, the Gili pier porter shakedown, the Bromo Cemoro Lawang jeep-cartel approach, the Paltuding mandatory-guide intercept at Ijen, and almost every tout scene in the 73 scam entries that follow.*

## Pattern 2: "Grab is blocked here" or "meter is broken" means you are being overcharged

The second pattern is Grab and taxis. Indonesia has Grab and Gojek nationwide, and they are cheap, metered, and transparent — you see the price before you book, and you pay through the app. But a meaningful share of tourist rides begin with a driver refusing Grab and quoting a flat price — always 3× to 5× what the app would charge — or a licensed blue-cab driver whose meter runs unusually fast.

From Bali's Ngurah Rai Airport (DPS), the real Grab fare to Seminyak or Kuta is 70,000–120,000 rupiah. The flat-rate arrivals-hall quote is 350,000–500,000. From Jakarta's Soekarno-Hatta Airport (CGK) to the city, the real metered Blue Bird run is 180,000–220,000 with tolls; the "airport shark" quote runs 600,000. In Lombok, Praya Airport (LOP) drivers and Padang Bai fast-boat taxi blockades will tell you "Grab doesn't work here" — it works at the designated pickup zone, usually 100 meters from the arrivals exit. In Ubud and parts of Nusa Penida, there is a genuine local taxi-mafia blockade — Grab drivers will message you "can't pick up, sorry" — but the workaround is to walk 200 meters past the visible blockade line and re-book; drivers pick up outside the enforcement zone.

The specific tell is any sentence that ends with "special price" or "fixed rate" or "for you, my friend," or any claim that the Grab or Gojek app is not available when your phone plainly shows it is.

The defense is short and universal. Open Grab (or Gojek) and book the ride on the app; the price is shown up front, paid digitally or in cash against the app fare, and there is no meter-is-broken conversation. If you must take a street taxi, look for **Blue Bird** (the largest reputable chain, pale blue with "BLUE BIRD GROUP" in silver) and insist on the meter (*argo* in Bahasa). Watch out for copycat taxis — "Pusaka" or "Blue Pearl" painted almost identically — these are deliberate knockoffs. If a flat-rate hail insists "meter broken," get out at a well-lit corner and re-book on Grab.

*You will see this pattern in: the DPS fake-Grab taxi mafia (Bali), the CGK airport sharks (Jakarta), the Blue Bird impersonator and meter-tampering script, the Ubud taxi-mafia blockade, the Padang Bai fast-boat taxi blockade (Lombok), the Praya Airport LOP quote (Lombok), the YIA/JOG Yogyakarta airport run, the Banyuwangi airport and ferry run, and the Labuan Bajo (LBJ) and Batam (BTH) transfer overcharges.*

## Pattern 3: Prices that aren't posted will surprise you later

The third pattern is the unposted price. Indonesian restaurants, market stalls, scooter-rental shops, money changers, boat operators, photo stands, and village tour guides will sometimes — not always — quote you a price that is deliberately vague. "Special price." "Donation, up to you." "Local rate." Each of these phrases hides a later surprise.

This pattern accounts for a large share of the overcharging scams in this book. Money changers along Bali's Jalan Legian and Seminyak's Jalan Kayu Aya advertise "BEST RATE — 0% COMMISSION" boards that quote 30–50 rupiah above the real Bank Indonesia rate, then use sleight-of-hand to shortchange the physical count by 500,000–1,500,000 rupiah. Bromo jeep-tour operators quote "700,000 rupiah for sunrise tour" and quietly omit King Kong Hill viewpoint fees, Bromo crater horse rides, and return-leg parking fees that compound to another 600,000 at tour end. Komodo day-boat captains in Labuan Bajo sell a "500 US dollar park pass" when the real Komodo National Park fee is 500,000 rupiah (~$30). Gili snorkel-tour boats take groups out without life vests and then charge "equipment rental" separately. Seminyak beauty salons quote 200,000 for a pedicure and bill 900,000 after "add-on" nail art the customer never agreed to. Ijen sulfur-miner photo subjects appear friendly and then demand 100,000 rupiah per shutter click.

The defense is a single habit, repeated before every transaction: ask for the price in writing — on a piece of paper, on a napkin, or in the notes app on your phone — *before* anything happens. For money changers, use only **PT Central Kuta** or **Dirgahayu Valuta Prima** branded booths with a Bank Indonesia license number posted on the wall, count every rupiah note yourself in full view of the teller, and leave if the teller wants to hand the stack across the counter before you recount. For boat tours, confirm whether park fees are included and which park fees specifically. For salons, get a written itemized quote before sitting down. For photos involving humans (sulfur miners at Ijen, "traditional" market characters), agree a single total price before the first shutter click, in writing.

*You will see this pattern in: the Bali and Seminyak money-changer sleight-of-hand, the Bromo jeep-tour escalation, the Komodo National Park fee confusion, the Gili Trawangan snorkel-tour unlicensed-boat scam, the Seminyak beauty-salon add-on overcharge, the Seminyak tailor bait-and-switch, the Ijen sulfur-miner photo shakedown, and the Kota Tua becak "free tour" Jakarta pattern.*

## Pattern 4: The "pre-existing damage," the "missing spare key," the "scratched bike"

The fourth pattern is the damage claim. You rent something — a scooter, a surfboard, a diving set, a drone — and when you return it, the operator points at an imaginary or pre-existing scratch and demands millions of rupiah for "damage," or claims a "spare key" was never returned and withholds the security deposit.

Bali, Seminyak, Canggu, Nusa Penida, and Kuta Lombok are the epicenters of the scooter-scratch variant. The operator takes your passport as a deposit, points at a hairline scratch near the exhaust that was already there, and tells you it will cost 3,000,000 rupiah to fix — or you can buy "insurance" for 800,000 on the spot. The Nusa Penida variant adds a genuinely dangerous road network — the rental shops know the steep Broken Beach and Kelingking Beach descents will cause most tourists to tip over, and they have a standing 5-million-rupiah "injury scam" repair bill waiting. A related variant: the shop quietly keeps a copy of your scooter key, sends a confederate to "steal" the scooter, and then withholds your 2,000,000-rupiah "lost key" deposit. In Lombok's Kuta, the same script runs plus a "road police bribe" shakedown where an accomplice in a yellow vest stops you on the road and demands 300,000 rupiah to "avoid a real ticket."

The defense is photographic, and it is mandatory. Before you accept the rental, take six to ten date-stamped photos and a thirty-second video walkaround of the scooter from every angle: front, back, left, right, both sides of the seat, the specific areas where scratches typically appear, the odometer, the fuel gauge, and the ignition with both keys visible. Hand over an easily-replaceable paper photocopy of your passport identification page rather than the real passport; keep the real passport at the hotel safe, and offer a deposit in cash instead if pressed. If the operator refuses to accept a photocopy and insists on the real passport, walk to the next rental shop — in Canggu, Kuta, and Ubud there are dozens. If a damage claim is made at return, produce the photos on the spot. If the operator still refuses to return the passport or deposit, call **110** (police) or **+62 21 3838 899** (tourism hotline) immediately from the location and wait for an officer to arrive.

*You will see this pattern in: the Bali scooter "spare key theft" and pre-damage claim, the Nusa Penida scooter injury scam, the Kuta Lombok scooter rental scam and road-police bribe, and the Gili islands dive-shop certification-shortcut gear-refill scam.*

## Pattern 5: An "official" is an official only at an official office

The fifth pattern is the stranger in authority. Real Indonesian police — the national Polri in blue-gray uniforms, the Polisi Pariwisata (tourist police) in white shirts with "TOURIST POLICE" in English on the shoulder, and traffic police in sage-green vests — operate only from official stations and posted checkpoints. They do not conduct on-the-spot "fines" against foreigners for scooter-helmet infractions on a rural road. They do not demand "immigration fees" at the Komodo entrance. They do not demand cash payable on the spot.

The *faux policier* pattern runs across Indonesia in three forms. The first is the Kuta Lombok scooter "road police bribe" — an accomplice in a yellow traffic-police look-alike vest waves a tourist over and demands 300,000–500,000 rupiah to "avoid a real ticket." The real traffic police in Lombok do write tickets, but they do so with a printed form and a receipt that goes to the court (*tilang*). If there is no paper, there is no real fine. The second is the Bali scooter-helmet shakedown — a real police officer is present but asks for cash rather than writing the ticket; the defense is to politely say "*saya mau tilang*" ("I want the official ticket, please") and the officer will either write it (~150,000 rupiah fine, paid at the court) or wave you on. The third is the Komodo National Park "park ranger mandatory fee" — a man in a khaki vest approaches at Padar Island's viewpoint and demands a 500,000-rupiah "ranger fee" that is not part of the real park pass; asking to see the vest's ID and calling the park office ends it.

The defense is simple and universal. If anyone on the street or at a viewpoint identifies themselves as an official and wants to examine your wallet, your passport, your scooter papers, or your phone, refuse and walk into the nearest well-lit shop or hotel lobby. Say calmly "*saya akan ke kantor polisi*" (I will go to the police station — pronounced *sah-yah ah-kan keh kan-tor po-lee-see*). Call **110** from your phone and put the call on speaker if the person has not already left. The Ministry of Tourism hotline (**+62 21 3838 899**) is the most reliable English-speaking fallback. A real officer will have no problem with this. A fake one will leave immediately.

*You will see this pattern in: the Bali traffic police scooter bribe, the Kuta Lombok scooter road-police shakedown, the Komodo National Park "mandatory ranger" fee padding, the Pink Beach and Padar Island photo-spot fee shakedown, and the generic fake-official pattern at every major tourist pagoda, cruise pier, and airport.*

## Pattern 6: If you pay before you have the thing, you probably won't get the thing

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch by reading the fine print after you have committed — so this pattern is really about what to pin down before you click *Confirm* or hand over cash.

You book a "luxury Seminyak villa" through a direct-message Instagram account, pay full price by bank transfer, and the "villa" is a Photoshopped listing. You book a Komodo liveaboard that promised a "6-night luxury charter" through WhatsApp and step onto a 40-year-old boat with six times as many passengers as beds. You hand 3 million rupiah to a pushy street agent in Yogyakarta for a "Borobudur sunrise VIP ticket" that turns out to be a fake paper stub that gets rejected at the real gate. You agree to a "full-day Nusa Penida tour" that excluded the Kelingking Beach parking fee, the Broken Beach "mandatory guide" fee, and the Angel Billabong return-leg parking fee, each added at the stop. You book an Ubud yoga retreat through a glossy Instagram account and the "guru" turns out to be a fake certification and a 1,200-US-dollar non-refundable deposit that funds nothing.

All of these work because you committed before you knew the terms — and then the terms, the vendor, the venue, or the platform were not what you thought they were.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction you make in Indonesia. Book accommodation only through platforms that offer pay-through-platform guarantees and dispute resolution (Booking.com, Agoda, Airbnb, Expedia, Traveloka) — never by Instagram, WhatsApp, or direct wire transfer to a private account. When a host messages "pay by bank transfer for a 20% discount," recognize it as the off-platform fraud signal and refuse. Verify any property on Google Street View before you depart — a legitimate Indonesian villa has visible signage, and it appears on at least one established booking platform. Pay every rental, excursion, or hotel by credit card (Visa, Mastercard, American Express) rather than debit, cash, Wise, or bank transfer — only a credit card gives you a chargeback right, and you can reverse a fraudulent charge within thirty to sixty days. For temple sunrise passes, buy at the actual gate or through the official temple website — Borobudur's real sunrise slot is booked through *borobudurpark.com*. For liveaboards and dive courses, book only with operators listed on the Ministry of Tourism licensed-operator registry or PADI's pro-dive-shop finder.

### One more: the "commission stop" pattern

Indonesia has its own set of tour-adjacent traps worth knowing. Any guided tour that includes an unscheduled stop at a "batik gallery," "silver factory," "kopi luwak coffee plantation," "kris (dagger) workshop," or "traditional medicine village" is almost certainly routing you to a commission-paying shop. This is not an emergency — you can simply decline to buy — but budget twenty extra minutes into your tour for it, and never feel obligated to purchase. The kopi luwak plantation stop specifically often involves caged civet cats in animal-cruel conditions; declining is the compassionate choice.

*You will see this pattern in: the Yogyakarta Malioboro batik gallery commission, the Ubud kopi luwak plantation scam, the Nusa Penida full-day driver commission stops, the Bromo-Ijen combo sleep-deprivation tour, the fake Seminyak villa Instagram listing, the Ubud fake-guru retreat, and the Komodo liveaboard overpromising scam.*
