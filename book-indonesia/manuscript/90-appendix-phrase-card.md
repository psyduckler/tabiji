# Appendix A: Indonesian Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Indonesia. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, the **Bahasa Indonesia** (in standard Latin script), and a simple pronunciation guide in italics. Bahasa Indonesia is non-tonal and uses a regular Latin alphabet — the pronunciation is closer to how you would read Spanish or Italian than how you would read Vietnamese or Thai, and even a foreign accent carries the meaning clearly.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. Say the first three or four out loud a few times. You will feel much less awkward using them when the moment comes.

## A note on pronunciation

Bahasa Indonesia is a **non-tonal** language with very regular orthography. A few pointers that will get you 90 percent of the way:

- Vowels are clean and consistent — *a* is "ah," *e* is "eh" (or a short "uh" in closed syllables), *i* is "ee," *o* is "oh," *u* is "oo."
- *c* before a vowel is always a soft "ch" sound (as in "cheese"). *Baca* ("to read") is *BAH-cha*.
- *ng* at the start or middle of a word is the same sound as at the end of "singing" — a single nasal consonant. *Nggak* ("no, informal") is *ng-gak*.
- *ny* is the Spanish *ñ*, as in "canyon." *Uang* ("money") has both the *ng* and a glottal softness — *oo-ANG*.
- *r* is a light tap, not the guttural Spanish rolled *r*.
- Stress is consistent and light; most words are stressed on the second-to-last syllable.

## A note on politeness

Bahasa Indonesia has a **simpler politeness system** than Thai or Vietnamese. A few key markers:

- Add **"Pak"** (sir, for men) or **"Bu"** (ma'am, for women) after a phrase to a stranger older than you — *"Tidak, terima kasih, Pak"* sounds more respectful than *"Tidak, terima kasih"* alone.
- **"Selamat pagi"** (*seh-lah-mat PAH-gee*, good morning), **"Selamat siang"** (*see-ang*, good midday), **"Selamat sore"** (*soh-reh*, good afternoon), **"Selamat malam"** (*mah-lam*, good evening) are the basic greetings and work as friendly openers to soften any transaction.
- **"Terima kasih"** (thank you — pronounced *teh-REE-mah KAH-see*) always ends a transaction.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**  
Berapa harganya?  
*Beh-RAH-pah har-gah-nyah?*

**Please use the meter.**  
Tolong pakai argo.  
*Toh-lohng pah-kai AR-go.*

Say this the instant you get into a Blue Bird street taxi. The word *argo* is the standard Indonesian word for taxi meter. Alternatively, simply open Grab or Gojek on your phone and book the ride — Indonesian drivers universally understand what opening the app means.

**Can I see the menu, please?**  
Boleh saya lihat menunya?  
*Boh-leh sah-yah LEE-hat meh-NOO-nyah?*

Use this before you sit down at any restaurant. *Menu* is the same word in Indonesian.

**What is the price per kilogram? (for seafood, fruit, or items priced by weight)**  
Berapa harganya per kilo?  
*Beh-RAH-pah har-gah-nyah pair KEE-lo?*

Fish, prawns, and other items priced by weight should be confirmed in writing or on a phone calculator before cooking. A legitimate restaurant will weigh the whole item on a visible scale, quote a total in rupiah, and write it down. Demand this.

**Do you accept credit cards?**  
Apakah Anda menerima kartu kredit?  
*Ah-pah-kah AHN-dah meh-neh-REE-mah KAR-too KRAY-deet?*

If the answer is "ya" ("yes") but the card reader later turns out to be "broken," you are being scammed. See "Scam in progress" below.

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**  
Tidak, terima kasih.  
*Tee-dak, teh-REE-mah KAH-see.*

Said firmly and without slowing your pace. This is the single most useful phrase in this entire appendix. Indonesian people understand it as a complete refusal; you do not need to explain why.

**I'm not interested.**  
Saya tidak tertarik.  
*Sah-yah tee-dak tair-TAH-reek.*

Stronger than *tidak, terima kasih*. Use this when a tout, a batik-gallery runner, or a cidomo driver keeps talking after the first refusal.

**Please leave me alone.**  
Tolong tinggalkan saya sendiri.  
*Toh-lohng teeng-GAHL-kahn sah-yah sen-DEE-ree.*

Literally "please leave me alone." Gentle but clear. Use this for persistent touts.

**Don't touch me.**  
Jangan sentuh saya.  
*JAH-ngahn sen-TOOH sah-yah.*

Say this clearly and step back. The word *jangan* ("don't") is emphatic.

**Excuse me, please let me through.**  
Permisi, tolong beri saya jalan.  
*Pair-MEE-see, toh-lohng beh-ree sah-yah JAH-lahn.*

The *permisi* ("excuse me") softens it so the person blocking your path hears a polite request.

**I'm just looking, thank you.**  
Saya hanya melihat-lihat, terima kasih.  
*Sah-yah HAH-nyah meh-lee-hat-lee-hat, teh-REE-mah KAH-see.*

Use this firmly in any market stall, batik gallery, or silver shop when a vendor starts pressuring you to buy.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I'm calling the police.**  
Saya akan menelepon polisi.  
*Sah-yah AH-kahn meh-neh-leh-POHN poh-LEE-see.*

This is the firmest card in the deck. The police number is **110**, and the Ministry of Tourism hotline is **+62 21 3838 899** (English-speaking staff during business hours). Say the phrase, hold up your phone, and start dialing. Most scam operators back down the moment they hear *polisi*.

**I want the official ticket.** *(For scooter traffic-police bribe shakedowns — the magic phrase.)*  
Saya mau tilang.  
*Sah-yah mao TEE-lang.*

*Tilang* is the Bahasa word for the official traffic ticket form. If a "traffic officer" cannot produce the printed form, he is a confederate and will wave you on. If he does produce it, the fine is typically 150,000 rupiah payable at the court (not roadside cash).

**I want to go to the police station.**  
Saya mau ke kantor polisi.  
*Sah-yah mao keh KAN-tor poh-LEE-see.*

Use this at any "official" street confrontation or when a venue is padding a bill.

**I'll call my bank.**  
Saya akan menelepon bank saya.  
*Sah-yah AH-kahn meh-neh-leh-POHN bank sah-yah.*

Say this if a restaurant, taxi driver, or scooter-rental shop disputes a credit-card transaction. Calling your card issuer from the table signals that the charge is going to be disputed.

**I was scammed. Please help.**  
Saya ditipu. Tolong bantu saya.  
*Sah-yah dee-TEE-poo. Toh-lohng BAN-too sah-yah.*

The phrase *ditipu* is the specific Indonesian word for "was tricked / was cheated" — it communicates urgency more than any English translation. Use this when speaking to a tourist-police officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, a fellow traveler, or any bystander you want to enlist.

**Please give me an itemized receipt.**  
Tolong berikan saya kuitansi terperinci.  
*Toh-lohng beh-ree-kahn sah-yah kwee-tan-see tair-peh-reen-chee.*

Asking for *kuitansi terperinci* (a detailed receipt) forces the establishment to account for every item, and it is the most polite version of "I do not accept this charge."

**I didn't order this.**  
Saya tidak memesan ini.  
*Sah-yah tee-dak meh-meh-SAHN EE-nee.*

Say this calmly about any unrequested dish, bottle, or "service" that appears on your bill. In Indonesia you are not legally required to pay for food or drinks you did not order, though you will usually need to say so explicitly.

**This is not what we agreed.**  
Ini bukan yang kita sepakati.  
*EE-nee BOO-kahn yahng KEE-tah seh-pah-KAH-tee.*

A short, firm phrase for taxi fares that exceed a quoted price, scooter "damage" claims, and tour-operator price-creep.

## Getting help

**Where's the nearest police station?**  
Di mana kantor polisi terdekat?  
*Dee MAH-nah KAN-tor poh-LEE-see tair-deh-kaht?*

Alternatively, dial **110** — the dispatcher will give you the nearest station address in English.

**I want an official taxi.**  
Saya mau taksi resmi.  
*Sah-yah mao TAHK-see reh-smee.*

Or simpler: open Grab or Gojek on your phone. These are metered, tracked, and generally free of the "meter is broken" scam.

**Please call 110.**  
Tolong telepon seratus sepuluh.  
*Toh-lohng teh-leh-POHN seh-RAH-toos seh-POO-looh.*

Indonesian numbers: *seratus* (100), *sepuluh* (10). Or simply show the number on your phone screen.

**Help! Thief!**  
Tolong! Maling!  
*Toh-LOHNG! MAH-leeng!*

Shouted in a crowded market or street, this is understood by every bystander as "someone stole from me, help me stop them." The word *maling* (thief) carries legal weight in Indonesia.

**Does anyone speak English?**  
Ada yang bisa bahasa Inggris?  
*Ah-dah yahng bee-sah bah-HAH-sah eeng-grees?*

**Please help me.**  
Tolong bantu saya.  
*Toh-lohng BAN-too sah-yah.*

## Reading Indonesian rupiah amounts

Indonesian rupiah prices are written with the symbol **Rp** or the code **IDR**. The rupiah has low per-unit value, so restaurants often drop three zeros on price boards — *50k* or *Rp 50.000* means 50,000 rupiah. Decimal conventions use the period as thousands separator (*1.000.000* = one million), which is the opposite of American convention. A few helpful markers:

- Paper notes: 1,000 (green), 2,000 (gray), 5,000 (brown), 10,000 (purple), 20,000 (pale green), 50,000 (blue), 100,000 (red). All feature national heroes on the face.
- Coins: 100, 200, 500, 1,000 (limited circulation).
- One US dollar is roughly 16,000 rupiah as of late 2025 — verify on xe.com before travel since the rate drifts.
- A common sanity check: 100,000 IDR ≈ $6 USD; 1,000,000 IDR ≈ $60 USD.

| Amount | Written | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 IDR | seribu | *seh-REE-boo* |
| 10,000 IDR | sepuluh ribu | *seh-POO-looh REE-boo* |
| 50,000 IDR | lima puluh ribu | *LEE-mah POO-looh REE-boo* |
| 100,000 IDR | seratus ribu | *seh-RAH-toos REE-boo* |
| 500,000 IDR | lima ratus ribu | *LEE-mah RAH-toos REE-boo* |
| 1,000,000 IDR | satu juta | *SAH-too JOO-tah* |
| 5,000,000 IDR | lima juta | *LEE-mah JOO-tah* |

If a vendor quotes a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it out:

**Please write the number down.**  
Tolong tulis angkanya.  
*Toh-lohng TOO-lees ANG-kah-nyah.*

Almost every vendor will oblige, often on the back of a receipt or a paper napkin. Pointing at the calculator app on your phone also works.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Indonesia, polite firmness works better than raised voices — raising your voice is read as a loss of *kesopanan* (composure and respect) and rarely helps your case. A calm *tidak, terima kasih* is already a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *tolong tinggalkan saya sendiri* ("please leave me alone") and *jangan sentuh saya* ("don't touch me") are both firm without being rude, and *saya akan menelepon polisi* ("I'm calling the police") is the firmest card in the deck.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar — and a calm *tidak, terima kasih* with a slight smile is, in itself, proof you know enough Bahasa Indonesia that the interaction is going to be harder than they expected.
