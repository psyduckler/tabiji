## The lanyard

Margie cleared customs at Cairo International Terminal 3 at twelve
minutes past midnight, on five hours of broken sleep, with a
phone she had set up properly for international travel two
evenings earlier and a pre-paid Vodafone SIM in her bag from the
Heathrow stopover. She had a printed reservation for the Marriott
Mena House in Giza and an Uber app installed and tested. She had
been preparing for this since Sunday.

She walked out of the customs hall and the first man who spoke to
her was wearing a navy polo shirt with the words OFFICIAL TAXI
embroidered in white over the left breast and a laminated badge on
a lanyard around his neck. He stepped in front of her before she
reached the official taxi counter ten meters away.

*"Welcome, madam. Mena House? I take you. Fixed price thirty US
dollar, no problem."*

Margie smiled politely. She did not break stride. She kept walking
toward the official counter.

The man followed for two paces, repeated the price, then peeled off
toward the next arrival. Margie did not look back.

The official rate from CAI to Giza Mena House on the night surcharge
was 350 Egyptian pounds. Around eleven US dollars. She booked an
Uber from the airport curb at the rideshare zone, watched the driver
match the photo, and was at the hotel by 1:14 a.m.

She thought about the lanyarded man twice on the drive. The first
time was about how confident he had looked. The second time was
about how completely the checklist had handled it before she even
landed.

## The trick

The pattern is Authority Costume: a uniform-style polo, a
laminated badge, a fixed-price quote at the curb. Airport arrival
scams exploit eight seconds. The eight seconds between customs and
the official counter. You have just deplaned.
You are disoriented. You do not know the city's transport system.
You have luggage. The first authority-looking person who speaks to
you gets a head start.

The single counter-move that defeats all five is procedural and
applies in every country. Walk past the men with signs. Walk past
the men with lanyards. Walk to the official terminal counter
inside the building. Use the rideshare zone if rideshare is
operating. Use a major-bank ATM (with the bank's name on it) for
cash. Buy a SIM in town if you have a working data eSIM bought
before you flew. Eight seconds of forward motion, no eye contact,
and the entire arrival-scam ecosystem peels away on its own.

> **Normal:** An airport-authority taxi rank with a posted tariff
> board, marked vehicles, a printed receipt with the airport
> authority's logo, and a fare in local currency. SIM kiosks
> labeled with the carrier's brand at posted retail prices.
>
> **Scam:** A "fixed price" quoted in dollars at the curb by
> someone with a generic polo, a laminated badge with no airport
> seal, no posted board, and no receipt before payment. SIM-kiosk
> markup of two to four times the in-town price for the same
> carrier plan.

## The five mechanics

Different airports lean on different ones, but the architecture is
identical from CUN to PEK to FCO to JNB.

**Unlicensed taxi intercept.** Drivers operating outside the
airport's licensed taxi system, positioned close to the customs
exit, quoting fares two to four times the licensed rate. The
vehicles are real cars but not affiliated with the airport's
taxi cooperative or the rideshare zone. Most reported in: Bangkok
(BKK), Mexico City (MEX), Madrid (MAD), Bali (DPS), Beijing (PEK).

**Meet-and-greet hustles.** Touts holding generic *"Private
Transfer"* or *"Hotel Shuttle"* signs, sometimes in matching
uniforms, sometimes affiliated with prepaid websites that charge
80–150 USD for what costs 25–40 USD at the official counter
inside. The most professional-looking of the five.

**Fake online transfer-booking websites.** Airport-transfer sites
that look legitimate, charge three to four times the curb price
for the same ride, and sometimes no-show entirely after taking the
prepayment. The URL was bought last year. The "trusted by 50,000
travelers" badge was bought too.

**Currency-exchange counter spread.** Airport currency-exchange
counters offer rates 8–15 percent below interbank. The "no
commission" marketing hides the spread. Compounding: many airports
have only one or two operators, so there is no competition. Not
aggressive, not deceptive in any single transaction, just
systematically expensive in a way most travelers never compute.

**SIM-card kiosk markup.** Airport SIM kiosks charge two to four
times what the same plan costs in town. The plans look local
because the carrier branding is real, but the airport-channel
pricing is a separate SKU at a different markup. Convenient,
official-looking, and effectively a one-time tax on impatience.

## Where it runs (54 countries, Mexico and Thailand co-dominant)

The airport-arrival scam is the second-most geographically
widespread variant in the atlas (after taxi meter manipulation),
documented at major international airports across all six
inhabited continents. Eight countries account for over forty-five
percent of all recorded incidents.

Mexico (CUN, MEX), Thailand (BKK), Spain (MAD, BCN), Indonesia
(DPS), India (DEL, BOM), Egypt (CAI, HRG), Morocco (CMN, RAK),
Brazil (GIG, GRU). Heavy concentration at second-tier and
tertiary airports as well: Phuket, Ho Chi Minh City, Marrakech,
Cusco, La Paz. The pattern follows the tourist economy, not the
airline-industry rankings.

Visitax-style fake-tax extractions are concentrated in Cancún
(real Visitax is 271 pesos online; hustlers charge 200 USD cash).
Meet-and-greet hustles concentrate in Bali, where the airport
taxi cooperative actively blocks rideshare access at the curb,
forcing passengers to walk to a designated parking-deck zone.
Currency-exchange spreads are worst at Casablanca CMN and the
Bali DPS arrivals hall.

## The five red flags

Two or more in the first sixty seconds: walk to the official
terminal counter before you commit to anything.

- **Anyone in a uniform-style shirt approaching you before the
  official counter.** Real airport staff stay at the counter.
- **A "fixed price" quoted in dollars, not local currency.** The
  legitimate rate is in local currency on a posted board.
- **A laminated badge with no organizational logo.** Real
  uniformed staff have the airport authority's seal or a major
  carrier's name.
- **A printed receipt presented at the curb before you have
  paid.** The receipt is part of the costume. Real receipts come
  after the transaction.
- **A waved credit-card reader at the curb instead of a meter
  inside the cab.** The reader is a Square POS connected to a
  personal account.

## The phrases that shut it down

Memorize one phrase per country before you fly. Refusal works
because it signals you know the play.

| Country | Phrase | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| Mexico | *"No gracias, voy con Uber."* | No thanks, I'm going with Uber. |
| Thailand | *"Mai aow krap/ka. Meter dai mai?"* | I don't want fixed. Will you run the meter? |
| Indonesia | *"Tidak, saya pakai Grab."* | No, I'm using Grab. |
| Egypt | *"La shukran, indi Uber."* | No thank you, I have Uber. |
| Morocco | *"Non merci, j'ai un transport."* | No thank you, I have a ride. |
| Spain | *"No gracias, tarifa fija al centro, por favor."* | No thanks, the fixed-rate flat fare to the center, please. |

Always check the airport's official posted rate at the rank or on
the airport-authority website before you fly. The fares are public
and stable.

## If this happens to you

You said yes to the lanyarded man. You paid 200 USD in cash for
the "Visitax." You took the meet-and-greet driver who quoted 60
EUR for what should have been 12. You converted at the CMN counter
and lost 24 USD to spread.

The financial recovery depends on the variant.

For cash payments to unlicensed drivers and fake-tax extractions:
file an immediate report at the airport-police office (every
international airport has one, usually labeled in English).
Include dated photos of the driver, the receipt, and the lanyard
if you can take them safely. The report number is what your travel
insurance will require for reimbursement under the "fraud loss"
clause of most policies.

For credit-card payments at the curb (the prepaid taxi voucher,
the inflated meet-and-greet): file a card chargeback within seven
days. The transaction descriptor will reveal the merchant's
business name; cite the police report and the official rate as
the dispute basis.

For currency-exchange spread: not recoverable. The spread was
disclosed, even if it was disclosed in fine print. Treat the loss
as the cost of not waiting until town.

For meet-and-greet no-shows on prepaid bookings: chargeback
through your card. The "trusted by 50,000 travelers" badge does
not protect a transaction where no service was rendered.

The next time, twenty dollars on a working eSIM bought before the
flight and forty seconds spent looking up the rideshare-zone
location on the airport authority's website would have prevented
all five sub-variants.

Margie was at the Mena House breakfast terrace by seven the next
morning, watching the Pyramids appear through the haze over her
coffee. The lanyarded man at the customs exit had cost her zero
dollars and forty seconds of forward motion. The Uber driver had
cost her eleven.
