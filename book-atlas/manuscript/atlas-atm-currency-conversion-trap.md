# A · ATM Currency Conversion Trap

## The button

Harry was paying for dinner at a trattoria on Vicolo del Mattonato
in Trastevere on a Saturday at nine fifteen. The bill was 92
euros for two: pasta, a half-bottle of Chianti, dessert, the
*coperto* (the per-person cover charge), service included. The
waiter brought the handheld payment terminal, inserted Harry's
Visa, typed the amount, and turned the screen toward him.

The screen displayed two buttons.

The left button, smaller and gray, read EURO 92.00.

The right button, larger and highlighted in green, read USD 108.56
with the additional text *"Tasso garantito 1.180."* (*Guaranteed
rate 1.180.*)

The waiter watched, polite but expectant.

Harry knew this one. He had read about it on the flight over. The
actual EUR-to-USD rate that evening was around 1.069. Visa's
network conversion would charge him about 99 USD on his statement
for a 92-euro meal. The DCC offer at 1.180 was about nine percent
above the network rate. The difference on this single dinner was
roughly 8.50 USD.

He pressed the EUR button.

The waiter looked slightly disappointed and processed the charge.
Harry's statement showed 99.10 USD a few days later. Across the
fourteen days of the trip, the EUR-button habit kept three figures
out of the conversion-spread budget on a series of similar
transactions.

## The trick

The pattern is Brand-Mimicry Storefront: the DCC button looks like
a normal POS option, defaulted by the terminal or pre-selected by
the cashier, with the markup hidden in the small print. Dynamic
currency conversion (DCC) depends on you accepting the
home-currency option at the point of sale. The local-currency
option is present on essentially every legitimate POS terminal,
ATM, and booking platform that offers DCC. The scammer just
defaults the visible button to
home-currency and adds the markup. The defensive routine is a
single trained habit. Always pay in the local currency.

The defense across all four mechanics is identical: tap the
local-currency option. EUR for Europe. GBP for the UK. JPY for
Japan. CZK for Czech Republic. HUF for Hungary. The local-currency
button is one tap away.

> **Normal:** A POS terminal that asks "EUR or USD?" with both
> options at equal prominence; either button charges your card and
> Visa, Mastercard, or Amex performs the network conversion at the
> wholesale rate.
>
> **Scam:** A POS terminal with the home-currency button
> highlighted, pre-selected, or made larger; the cashier hands you
> the device with the home-currency option already tapped, locking
> in a 5–15 percent markup that the merchant or POS provider keeps.

## The four mechanics

**The restaurant or shop POS.** The most-encountered variant. After
the meal or the purchase, the POS terminal asks *"Choose your
currency: EUR or USD?"* The home-currency rate adds five to twelve
percent to the bill. Some terminals make the local-currency option
subtle (smaller text, secondary button) to push tourists toward
DCC. The waiter or cashier may pre-select the home currency before
handing you the terminal. Most reported in: Rome, Florence, Milan,
Venice; Barcelona Las Ramblas; Madrid; Paris cafés and luxury
boutiques; London tourist-zone restaurants.

**The ATM home-currency lock-in.** Documented heavily at Euronet,
Travelex, and some local-bank ATMs across Italy, Czech Republic,
Hungary, Spain, Portugal. After PIN entry and amount selection, the
ATM displays *"Do you accept the conversion? Lock in your home
currency rate?"* The offered rate is typically 8–15 percent worse
than your card issuer's network rate. The decline option is
labeled subtly: *"Continue without conversion,"* *"Use card network
rate."* Most reported at: Euronet ATMs at Rome Termini, Florence
Santa Maria Novella, Prague Old Town, Budapest Váci Street,
Madrid Atocha, Barcelona Sants, Lisbon Rossio.

**The hotel checkout auto-DCC.** Hotels routinely auto-select
home-currency conversion at checkout for visible-foreign cards. The
receipt shows two amounts: local and home. The auto-DCC selection
happens silently. Many tourists do not notice the conversion until
they review the credit-card statement weeks later. The hotel keeps
the markup as additional revenue or shares it with the POS
provider. Most reported in: Marriott, Hilton, IHG, Accor, Hyatt
properties in Italy, Spain, France, Germany, the UK; tourist-zone
independent hotels in Czech Republic, Portugal, Greece, Croatia,
Hungary.

**The online-booking-platform DCC.** Booking.com, Hotels.com,
Expedia, Agoda all default to home-currency *"guaranteed rate"* for
many bookings. The framing in checkout reads: *"You'll be charged
in [home currency] for a guaranteed rate"* as a pre-selected
option, with 5–12 percent markup. The "guaranteed rate" marketing
is misleading. The home-currency rate is locked at booking but at
the platform's chosen markup. The property-currency option is
hidden behind a small dropdown link.

## Where it runs (8 countries, Italy dominant)

DCC is documented at every major European tourist destination and
at most major airports globally. Italy alone accounts for roughly
twenty-five percent of global exposure, driven by aggressive POS
DCC across the entire restaurant-and-tourist-shop ecosystem.
Spain, Czech Republic, France, the UK, Hungary, Portugal, and
Greece round out the top eight.

The European Banking Authority issued guidelines in 2018 requiring
POS terminals to disclose the markup percentage. France enforces
the disclosure. Italy technically requires it but the disclosure
is often subtle (smaller font, secondary screen). Outside Europe,
DCC is structurally legal but exploits tourist confusion about
foreign-exchange mechanics.

## The five red flags

If two or more fire when you are about to tap a button, the DCC
variant is in play.

- **A pre-selected home-currency button.** Local currency is the
  default at every legitimate POS. If the home-currency button is
  highlighted, the cashier or the terminal has selected DCC for
  you.
- **A "guaranteed rate" message at the ATM.** This is the lock-in
  pitch. The "guaranteed rate" is guaranteed at the markup, not
  at your card's network rate.
- **A waiter or cashier gesturing toward the home-currency
  button.** Some POS providers pay restaurant-chain commission on
  DCC selections. Your cashier may be incentivized to point at the
  bigger button.
- **A hotel checkout receipt showing two amounts.** The auto-DCC
  has already happened. You can demand a re-charge in local
  currency at checkout if you catch it before signing.
- **A booking site showing your trip total in home currency by
  default.** Click *"Show all currencies"* or *"Pay in property
  currency"* in the booking dropdown.

## The phrases that shut it down

| Language | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Italian | *In euro, per favore.* (In euros, please.) |
| Spanish | *En euros, por favor.* / *En la moneda local, por favor.* |
| French | *En euros, s'il vous plaît.* |
| Czech | *V korunách, prosím.* |
| Hungarian | *Forintban, kérem.* |
| German | *In Euro, bitte.* |
| Portuguese | *Em euros, por favor.* |
| English | "In the local currency, please." |

For an ATM, look for the option labeled *Continue without
conversion*, *Use card network rate*, or simply *Decline*. Tap
that.

## If this happens to you

You reviewed your card statement after the trip and found 8–15
percent DCC markups across multiple European transactions. The
statement charges may say *"Visa Conversion"* or list the original
EUR amount alongside a worse USD amount than your card's standard
rate would have produced.

DCC losses are partially recoverable through Visa or Mastercard
chargeback when the markup was added without your explicit
selection. The grounds are *"Currency Conversion (DCC) Without
Cardholder Consent."* You file under Reason Code 11 (Mastercard)
or Dispute Reason 13.6 (Visa).

The dispute requires evidence. Take photos of your physical
receipt at every transaction. The receipt shows the conversion
explicitly: *"DCC: 1.180 EUR/USD"* or similar. If the receipt does
not disclose the rate, the chargeback succeeds more easily because
the merchant is in violation of EBA disclosure rules.

For ATM lock-ins where you accepted the rate by tapping the wrong
button, the recovery is harder. The chargeback fails because you
authorized the conversion. The actionable response is preventive
for future trips.

For hotel auto-DCC where you did not authorize: file a chargeback
with the receipt. Hotels lose these disputes consistently because
the silent auto-selection is widely known to violate disclosure
rules.

For online-booking-platform DCC at the time of booking: chargeback
is harder because the booking-flow disclosure usually exists, even
if it is buried. The actionable response is to always click *"Show
all currencies"* before paying.

Harry came home with a fourteen-day card statement that ran two
pages and DCC selected on zero of the transactions. He estimated
the savings against the alternative button at about 220 dollars
across the trip. He bought his wife a bottle of Brunello with the
difference and called the math even.
