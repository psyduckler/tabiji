# Your first 24 hours in a new country

<!-- comic-insert -->
![Hero illustration for 07 First 24 Hours](../build/images/hero-first-24-hours-arrival.jpg){ width=100% }

Marcus stepped off the eleven-hour overnight from Doha into
Bangkok's Suvarnabhumi Terminal at 6:14 in the morning, on three
hours of broken sleep, with a phone that had not connected to a
network since his stopover and a credit card he had forgotten to
enable for international transactions. He had been a digital nomad
for four years and still made the same arrival-day mistakes that
morning that he had made on his first trip overseas. The next
eighteen hours of this chapter are what he should have done from
the moment he cleared immigration.

The first day is when most scammers want you. Jet-lagged, currency-
disoriented, slightly anxious, signal-poor on local norms. The window
is roughly the first eighteen hours after you clear immigration.
After that you have a hotel address, a working SIM, a rideshare app
loaded, a credit card lock toggled. The window closes and the scammers
move on to the next arrival.

This chapter walks through that window hour by hour. Most of it is
ordinary travel hygiene that becomes critical because the alternative
is a chapter in Part II.

## Hour 0 to 1: Immigration to baggage

Almost no scams happen inside the secure zone of an airport. Use the
hour. Top up the data plan on your phone if you bought an eSIM
in advance. Pull up the rideshare app and confirm the app works in
country (Uber does not exist everywhere; the local equivalent is
listed in your country book). Read the printed exchange rate at the
foreign-exchange counter and write it down on a notes app. You are
about to need to recognize when the rate the city offers you matches
or doesn't.

Photograph the immigration stamp in your passport. Some countries
(Argentina, Egypt, Vietnam) issue a separate paper stub at the border
that you must keep. Lose the stub and re-entering or leaving becomes
slow. Photograph it. Then keep the original folded into the
passport itself.

## Hour 1 to 2: Arrivals hall

This is where most airport scams run. *Airport Arrival Scams* in
Part II covers the unlicensed-taxi curb intercept, the
meet-and-greet hustle, the fake "Tourist Information" booth, the
currency-counter spread, and the SIM-kiosk markup — all five
variants live in this corridor.

The defense is procedural. Walk past anyone holding a sign with your
name unless your hotel told you in writing they were sending a
specific driver. Walk past anyone offering "official taxi" who is not
inside the rideshare pickup zone marked on the airport's terminal
map. Walk past anyone offering to "help with your bags." Walk to the
SIM-card counter, the ATM, the rideshare pickup zone. In that order.

The SIM counter is your first stop because everything else gets
easier with data. Use your credit card (not cash from the airport
ATM) so you have a record of the charge. Take the receipt. Make sure
the SIM activates before you leave the counter.

The ATM is your second stop because cash from a bank ATM in the
airport runs at a 1 to 3 percent spread, much better than the
foreign-exchange counter. Use a card you have already toggled to
allow international transactions. Take the receipt. Photograph the
ATM if you can; if your card is later compromised, the bank will
ask which ATM you used and on what date.

The rideshare pickup zone is your third stop. Confirm in the app
that the driver matches the photo and license plate before you get
in. Confirm the destination matches the address you booked.

## Hour 2 to 4: Transit to hotel

In the rideshare car, route the driver yourself by typing the hotel
address into the app. Watch the route on the screen for unexpected
detours. The Commission Detour pattern (Pattern 4 in Part I) runs
hardest on first-day arrivals because the operator assumes you do
not know where you are going. If the driver suggests a "better"
hotel, declines to take the route the app shows, or stops at "his
cousin's currency exchange," the answer is no. If the driver insists,
ask to be let out at the next public location and order a different
ride.

Pay through the app. Cash payments to a rideshare driver are the
single largest source of post-trip disputes; the app has the receipt
and the route, the cash transaction has neither.

## Hour 4 to 8: Hotel check-in

At reception, ask three questions before you go to the room:

1. **What is the local emergency number?** Not the one in the book.
   The one this hotel actually uses. Some countries route emergencies
   through different numbers based on location.
2. **Where is the nearest ATM the hotel staff personally use?** This
   filters skimmed ATMs in tourist neighborhoods (Chapter A, *ATM
   Skimming*). Hotel staff do not use skimmed ATMs.
3. **Is there a tourist tax, and what is the receipt?** If yes,
   you should be paying it to the hotel and getting a receipt with
   the city name and the tax line item. If the "tax" appears on
   the bill but the receipt does not name a tax authority, refer
   to the web atlas's *Tourist Tax Bogus Levy* entry.

Get the hotel's printed business card with the address in the local
script. Carry one in your wallet at all times. The card replaces your
phone for taxi drivers when your battery is dead.

## Hour 8 to 18: First sleep

Sleep is a defense. Jet lag is the single largest contributing factor
to most first-day scam losses, because the cognitive cost of
verification (Pattern 5, the Made-Up Closure; Pattern 6, the
Brand-Mimicry Storefront) is high when you are exhausted. Sleep
before you make any large purchase. Sleep before you respond to any
"limited time" offer. Sleep before you sign anything.

Set an alarm. Do not nap into 3 a.m. and then go for a walk; that
is the window most catastrophic scams run in. Eat something. Drink
water. Sleep flat.

## Hour 18 to 24: Calibration walk

After sleep, take a short walk in the immediate neighborhood of the
hotel. Find one café, one ATM, one pharmacy, one convenience store.
Note the prices on three things you buy regularly (a coffee, a bottle
of water, a snack). Those prices are the calibration for everything
you will be quoted later. A 4 dollar coffee at the hotel café and a
3 dollar coffee at the corner stall is the local market. A 12
dollar coffee at a "fancy" tourist-zone café is the start of the
*Tourist Trap Restaurants* pattern documented in the web atlas
and adjacent to *Restaurant Bill Padding* in Part II. The calibration walk is what makes the
distinction obvious.

Buy the bottle of water with cash from your earlier ATM withdrawal.
Confirm the change is correct. The first cash transaction is the one
where vendors will sometimes test whether you can count in the local
currency. Counting is not rude. It is the right thing to do.

## What this hour-by-hour walkthrough does

The first twenty-four hours of any trip is the most important window
for both the traveler and the scammer. The scammers know which terminal
exit, which taxi rank, which hotel district, which ATM is in their
operating area. The structure above gives you a sequence that makes
each of those operating areas safer one by one.

After hour twenty-four, the city becomes a place you know how to
move in. Most of the scams in Part II move out of high-probability
range. The remaining ones are the ones you read about by destination
in your country book.
