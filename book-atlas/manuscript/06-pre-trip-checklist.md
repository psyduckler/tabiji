# The pre-trip checklist

<!-- comic-insert -->
![Hero illustration for 06 Pre Trip Checklist](../build/images/hero-pre-trip-checklist-margie.jpg){ width=100% }

Margie sat at her kitchen table on a Sunday evening with her flight
to Cairo on Tuesday morning, a glass of red wine, this book, and a
phone she had never bothered to set up properly for international
travel. She was sixty-two. She had been scammed once before, a Lake
Como gondola overcharge in 2019 that cost her ninety euros and the
better part of a sleepless night, and she was not making the same
mistake twice.

She gave herself an hour. It took her under forty minutes. The nine
items below are what she did, in roughly the order she did them.
Working through them gets you to the airport with the protections
that make eighty percent of the chapters in Part II irrelevant.

Take a screenshot of this page. Refer to it the night before each trip.

## 1. Book your first night through a verified platform

Booking.com, Hotels.com, Expedia, Agoda, Airbnb, the hotel chain
direct. Not a "deal" forwarded over WhatsApp by a "fixer." Not a
listing on an unfamiliar website that came up in a Google ad. Not a
phone number you got from a hostel forum five years ago. The first
night is the night you are most jet-lagged, most disoriented, and
least able to recover from a bad booking. Pay the platform tax. The
platform's customer service is the insurance.

## 2. Save your hotel address in three places

In your booking confirmation email. As a screenshot on your phone.
Written on a paper card you can hand to a taxi driver who does not
read your alphabet. Some cities (Bangkok, Tokyo, Seoul, Cairo) make
the third option matter, because the taxi driver may not read Roman
characters. Some hotels print a courtesy card on the back of the
business card. Take five.

## 3. Buy a local SIM at the airport, on arrival

Twenty to thirty dollars buys you a working data connection that
turns half the scams in this book into things you can verify before
you fall for them. The Made-Up Closure pattern (Pattern 5) collapses
in the face of a working phone. Most airports have at least one
licensed cell carrier counter immediately after baggage claim.
Activate before you leave the terminal.

## 4. Set up the local rideshare app

Uber. Grab. Bolt. Ola. Careem. DiDi. Gett. Whichever operates in
your destination. Install before you fly, log in once on the airport
Wi-Fi, link a card. The rideshare app turns a 60-dollar airport taxi
overcharge into a 12-dollar metered ride with a route on a map. It is
the single most useful app on a traveler's phone.

## 5. Lock your cards' international permissions in your bank app

Most major banks let you toggle international transactions on and
off, and let you set per-transaction limits. Toggle on for your trip
dates. Set a limit that covers normal travel spending but blocks the
fifty-thousand-dollar fraudulent charge that will not look like
yours. If your bank's app has a "lock card" toggle, learn where it
is. You may need it within an hour of an incident.

## 6. Document your passport, visa, and cards safely

Photograph your passport (the photo page) and any visas. Save the
images to a password manager (1Password, Bitwarden, Apple Keychain)
or to an encrypted cloud folder you can log into from any browser
without your phone. The embassy replaces a lost passport faster
when you arrive with the photographed copy in hand.

Do **not** photograph the front and back of your credit cards. The
back of a card carries the CVV, and a photo of both sides in a
breached cloud account or a compromised email inbox is full card
fraud waiting to happen. Instead, save four data points per card in
your password manager:

- the issuer name (Chase, Amex, Capital One)
- the card network (Visa, Mastercard, Amex)
- the last four digits
- the issuer's twenty-four-hour international fraud line, printed
  on the back of every card; type it in, do not photograph it

That is enough to phone the issuer and cancel by identity
verification at three in the morning from a hotel landline if your
wallet is gone. None of it exposes your card if the password
manager is compromised. Do not email any of this to yourself; email
is not a secure storage channel.

## 7. Note three local emergency numbers

The general emergency number for the country (112 in most of Europe,
911 in North America, 119 or 113 in South America, 110 or 100 in
much of Asia, varies elsewhere). The country-specific tourist police
hotline if it has one (1363 in India, 126 in Egypt, 1155 in Thailand,
the Carabinieri at 112 in Italy). The non-emergency number for your
embassy in the city you are arriving in. Save all three to your
phone contacts before you fly.

## 8. Read the destination-specific country book

This atlas is the field guide. The country book in the Travel Safety
Series for your destination is the deep dive. The week before the
flight is the right time. By the time you land, you will know which
neighborhoods to be alert in, which scams concentrate at your
arrival airport, and which Hindi or Spanish or Arabic phrase ends
the most common scam at your hotel.

## 9. Tell someone your itinerary

A family member, a friend, anyone not on the trip. Hotel addresses,
flight numbers, any extended changes. Two reasons. First, the
catastrophic scams (express kidnapping, drink spiking, the fake
police shakedown that turns into a body search) are easier to recover
from when someone outside the country is paying attention. Second, if
your phone goes and you cannot remember your own hotel address at
3 a.m., a phone call to a friend can sometimes recover faster than
the alternative.

## What this checklist does

The checklist neutralizes most of Patterns 5 and 6 (the Made-Up
Closure and the Brand-Mimicry Storefront), because both patterns rely
on you being unable to verify the original claim. It significantly
reduces the cost of Patterns 1, 2, and 3 (the Captive-Position Lever,
the Authority Costume, the Sub-Market Quote), because the rideshare
app and the local SIM cap your downside on every transit decision and
your upside is verification before you commit.

It does not neutralize Pattern 4 (the Commission Detour), because
that one runs on social engineering more than information asymmetry.
Pattern 4 is the one Part I prepares you for and Part II teaches
you to recognize.

Margie's evening took under forty minutes. The trip to Cairo had
its moments: a tout at the airport curb who quoted three times the
metered fare, a Khan el-Khalili papyrus-school markup she nearly
fell for on the second day, a felucca captain who tried to double
the price after the boat left the dock. The checklist did not
prevent those moments. It made each of them shorter, less
expensive, and easier to walk away from.
