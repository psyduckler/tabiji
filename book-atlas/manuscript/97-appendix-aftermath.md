# Appendix H: After you've been scammed (the thirty-day window)

Priya was sitting on the edge of the bed in her hotel room in Bandra
West, Mumbai, on day five of what was supposed to be a three-week
trip. She had filed the police report on day one. Her bank had locked
the card on day one. She had eaten and slept since. The bar bill,
filed under fraud and disputed, was already in her bank's queue.

What she had not done was process what had happened. The dating-app
match had felt real for the first hour. The drink had been ordered in
front of her. The bill, when it landed, was for items she had never
seen the kitchen prepare. The man at the door had asked, calmly, for
her credit card number twice before she understood that the
pleasantries were over.

It was day five and she could not stop replaying the second time he
asked.

This appendix is for what happens then. It picks up where the
recovery playbook in Appendix B leaves off and runs through the
thirty-day window when financial recovery becomes real, when the
emotional cost gets named, and when the decisions about the trip and
about future travel get made.

Most readers will never need this appendix. The minority who do find
themselves here are not alone. The patterns below come from hundreds
of post-scam reports filed with us by travelers across the twenty-
four countries we cover, and from Priya, who eventually made her
flight to Goa on day eight.

Day five, the financial side of what had happened to Priya was
already in motion in the background. The mechanical recovery work
below is what was happening while she was sitting on the edge of
the bed. Both threads, the financial and the emotional, run in
parallel for most readers who find themselves here. The next two
sections cover them in the order they tend to need attention.

## The financial settlement

Most of the financial recovery from a tourist scam is mechanical and
takes between fourteen and forty-five days.

**Your bank's fraud dispute** typically resolves between seven and
fourteen business days for amounts under five thousand dollars.
Larger amounts or more complex cases (multiple transactions, mixed
genuine and fraudulent charges, foreign currency conversion) can take
thirty to sixty days. The bank either reverses the charges and re-
issues credit, or denies the dispute and explains why. Denials are
worth contesting once. Twice if the second appeal involves new
evidence (a more detailed police report, a video, a witness
statement).

**Travel insurance** typically pays out between fourteen and thirty
business days after a complete claim is filed. Complete means the
police report, the receipts, and the underlying booking confirmation.
Incomplete claims sit in queues for months. The single most common
reason for travel-insurance denial is the missing police report,
which is why Appendix B treats the police report as a first-hour
priority.

**Embassy emergency travel documents** typically resolve within
twenty-four to seventy-two hours of the embassy visit. Replacement
of the full passport happens once you are home and is its own
multi-week process administered by your country's passport authority.

If two of the three above pay out in full and the third partially
covers, you will recover most of the financial loss. The deductibles
on travel insurance and the spread between actual and recoverable
fraud are typical residual costs. Plan for ten to twenty percent
unrecoverable.

## What happens to the money your bank does not recover

The fraud-dispute process is asymmetric. In documented cases,
the bank or card issuer often absorbs the loss when the dispute
is filed within the standard window. Outcomes vary by issuer,
documentation, card-present versus card-not-present status, and
country. The remaining one in five comes back to the
cardholder. There are several reasons this happens:

- The transaction was a "card-present" payment with a chip-and-pin
  authorization. Some banks treat these as harder to dispute.
- The dispute was filed more than sixty days after the transaction
  date, beyond Visa or Mastercard's standard chargeback window.
- The transaction was a wire transfer rather than a card payment.
  Wire-transfer fraud has much weaker recovery options than card
  fraud, which is why Appendix B argues so hard against wiring
  money to an unfamiliar party.
- The traveler authorized the transaction at the time and is now
  disputing on the basis of fraud after the fact. Banks treat this
  category cautiously.

If your dispute comes back denied, the next step depends on which of
the above applies. Sometimes a more detailed police report is enough
for a re-submission. Sometimes the path is through a separate
financial-fraud authority (Action Fraud in the UK, IC3 in the US, the
Australian Cybercrime Online Reporting Network). The country book for
your destination identifies the right escalation channel.

## The emotional cost

The financial recovery is paperwork. The emotional recovery is
different.

In the post-scam reports we receive, the pattern that comes back
most often is a window of two to four weeks where the scam intrudes
on day-to-day life. Replaying the encounter. Second-guessing the
moment they did not walk away. Feeling a residual mistrust toward
strangers who match the profile of the scammer. Sometimes a
reluctance to plan the next trip.

This is normal. It is not a sign that something is wrong with you.
It is a sign that you are processing an experience your nervous
system was not expecting. The window typically closes by week four
to six, often without explicit attention.

The patterns we have seen help:

**Tell the story out loud, multiple times.** Telling a friend, a
spouse, a family member, even a co-worker. Many travelers report
that each retelling reduces the emotional charge of the memory.
The detail you remember at
week one will fade by week three, which is why we recommend writing
it down (in Appendix B's first-week list) before that happens.

**Keep traveling.** Most travelers who are scammed continue to
travel, and most of them describe the second trip as "the trip that
returned travel to me." Canceling future plans because of one scam
is the strongest sign that the scam has cost more than money.

**Recognize the asymmetry.** The scammer ran the script ten times
that week. You ran it once, with all the cognitive load of being a
tourist in a country you did not know, being tired, being unfamiliar
with the local language. The scammer's win has nothing to do with
your intelligence and everything to do with their thousand previous
runs of the same script. The script is the technology. You were not
the failure.

**Avoid the public-shaming spiral on travel forums.** Many forums
respond to "I was scammed" posts with a chorus of "you should have
known better." This is not helpful and almost always wrong. Knowing
better requires having read about the scam, which most readers had
not done at the time of their incident. That is precisely why this
book exists. The right response to a public-shaming post is to
close the tab.

## The travel-insurance question

If you do not currently buy travel insurance, the question after a
scam is whether to start.

Travel insurance covers different things in different policies, and
the variations matter for scam recovery specifically. Some policies
cover scam losses up to a small amount (typically five hundred to
one thousand dollars). Some cover only theft, not "voluntary"
transactions like the gold ring's ten euros. Some cover the cost of
replacing documents (the passport, the visa) but not the cash that
was lost.

For most leisure travelers on trips of a week or longer, an annual
multi-trip policy at one to three hundred dollars is reasonable
insurance, and the deductible is recovered the first time you claim.
If you travel for business, your employer's coverage is often
better than what you would buy individually. If you travel
infrequently, single-trip policies are easier and cheaper but
require you to remember to buy them.

The country book for your specific destination contains a fuller
discussion of which insurers cover the specific kinds of scams that
country is known for. The atlas-level guidance is: travel insurance
matters most when something catastrophic happens, and the catastrophic
tier in Appendix E is the right reading list for whether to buy
coverage.

## When to call a professional

A small number of post-scam situations require professional help
beyond what this book can provide.

**Mental-health professional.** If the emotional window we
described above does not close after six weeks, or if it includes
intrusive thoughts, sleep disruption, or avoidance behavior that
is changing your daily life, talk to a clinician. Brief
trauma-focused therapy is often helpful for travelers in this
window; many people see meaningful relief over a short course of
sessions, though outcomes depend on the individual, the clinician,
and the nature of the experience. Seeking support is not a
measure of fragility. It is a measure of your nervous system
having registered a real event.

**Lawyer.** If the financial loss is large (more than ten thousand
dollars), if the scam involved identity theft beyond the immediate
fraudulent charges, or if there is a chance of pursuing the operator
through civil court in the destination country, a lawyer who
specializes in cross-border consumer fraud is the right next step.
The country book identifies named firms in the destination.

**Embassy victim assistance.** Most major embassies have a victim-
assistance officer for catastrophic incidents (drug-related, body
search, abduction-adjacent). If your case falls in this category,
the embassy is the first call, not the third. Refer to Appendix B's
fifteen-minute window.

## The follow-through

If a scam in this book happened to you, write to us at
editorial@tabiji.ai. The detail you can share, sanitized as much as
you want, makes next year's edition sharper for the reader who comes
after you.

Every report goes into the research pipeline. Many of them have
shaped chapters in this book that started from a single email. The
network of travelers who report after the fact is the closest thing
this category has to peer review.

The one specific thing we ask: include the date, the city, and the
exact dollar or local-currency amount, even if approximate. Those
three pieces of information are what allow us to update the
geographic concentration data and the pricing-tier breakdowns in
the chapter your scam fits into.
