## The cone

Margie and her sister walked into Jemaa el-Fnaa at sunset. The
square was filling with food stalls, drum circles, snake charmers,
juice vendors, storytellers. The Koutoubia minaret glowed in the
late light. They were walking along the southern edge of the
square toward the Café de France for the rooftop view.

Three women in colorful kaftans materialized beside them, smiling.
One of them was on the closer side of Margie's sister and held a
small henna cone in her right hand. She spoke in fast English,
French, and Arabic.

*"You are beautiful, free henna, just one minute, lucky symbol, no
money, free for you, very nice."*

Before Margie's sister had finished saying *no, thank you*, the
woman had reached out, grasped her left wrist firmly, and pressed
the henna cone to the back of her hand. Three quick lines of paint.
The artist started a small flower design.

*"No, no, please stop,"* Margie's sister said. The artist smiled
and continued painting. *"Just one minute, just lucky symbol, very
nice."* She finished the flower in twenty seconds, then stepped
back, gesturing at the wet paint.

*"Now, just two hundred dirham, please, for the artist, very
lucky."*

Two hundred dirham was about twenty US dollars. The other two
women had moved closer, blocking the path back the way they had
come.

## The trick

The henna tattoo street ambush stacks two patterns: Manufactured
Reciprocity (a "free, lucky symbol" framing that creates social
debt before refusal is possible) and Captive-Position Lever (the
half-finished design on your skin and the surrounding group of
crew members close the position before the price is named).

The hustle runs in two stages. Stage one is grab-and-paint
coercion: the artist grabs your wrist or your companion's wrist
without consent and applies a quick design before you can step
back. Stage two is price extraction: a 200–800 dirham demand for
the half-finished design. Refusal means walking through
the rest of the square with an incomplete pattern on your skin
and the artist's voice rising behind you.

The trap relies on two physical conditions. The wrist must be
accessible (palms down, hands at sides, bag straps held low). The
square must have group-surround geometry: two or three crew
members converging on the target as one paints, blocking the easy
walk-away. Jemaa el-Fnaa, Khan el-Khalili, Sultanahmet, and the
Jaipur Pink City all have the geometry. The ambush works there
because it works there.

A second risk runs parallel to the price extraction. Some henna
artists add paraphenylenediamine (PPD) to make *black henna* that
develops in under twelve hours. Real henna takes overnight to
darken and fades to brick-red. PPD-laden henna develops in three
to six hours and looks black or near-black on application. PPD
causes severe skin reactions in five to ten percent of users:
chemical burns, permanent scarring, lifelong PPD sensitivity that
later triggers reactions to hair dye, leather, and rubber. The
chemical risk is the more severe of the two harms.

The defense is two rules.

The hands-in-pockets rule: when crossing tourist squares in
Marrakech Jemaa el-Fnaa, Cairo Khan el-Khalili, Istanbul
Sultanahmet, and Jaipur Pink City, keep both hands in your front
pockets or holding bag straps high enough that the wrist is
covered. The artist needs wrist access to initiate. No access, no
ambush.

The black-henna refusal rule: never accept henna that looks black
on application or develops in under twelve hours. Real henna is
brown-green when wet and brick-red when dry. Anything black is
PPD. The chemical risk is not negotiable.

## The five mechanics

**Marrakech Jemaa el-Fnaa grab-and-paint.** The canonical variant.
Three women in kaftans converge on a tourist couple along the
southern edge of the square or near the Koutoubia approach. One
grabs the wrist, the other two block the path back. Closing prices
100–500 dirham. Documented since at least the 1990s.
Marrakech tourist police 19 issue ongoing advisories.

**Cairo Khan el-Khalili Pyramids approach.** Henna artists work
the bazaar's southern entrance and the Giza Pyramid visitor
parking. Mechanic identical to Marrakech. PPD use is common in
Cairo variants because the dark color sells faster to tourists who
do not know to wait twelve hours. Closing prices 50–300 EGP.

**Istanbul Sultanahmet Hagia Sophia approach.** Henna artists work
the cobbled approach between Hagia Sophia and the Blue Mosque.
Smaller-volume than Marrakech but with the same group-surround
geometry. Closing prices 100–500 lira.

**Jaipur Pink City and Delhi Chandni Chowk.** Indian variant.
Henna artists at the Hawa Mahal approach and the Jama Masjid
southern gate in Delhi. Mechanic identical: grab, paint, demand.
Closing prices 200–1,000 rupees. Indian tourist police 1363.

**Black-henna PPD risk variant.** Cross-cuts all four countries.
Hustlers apply *black henna* that develops in three to six hours.
The dark color is faster and looks more dramatic, which sells
better to tourists who want a photo. The PPD chemical risk does
not appear until twenty-four to seventy-two hours after
application. By then the artist is in a different square and the
tourist is in a different country. ER visits and lifelong allergy
sensitization are documented in dermatology literature for every
country in the family.

## Where it runs (4 countries, Morocco dominant)

Henna ambush concentrates at four major tourist squares. Morocco
runs the highest documented volume, driven by Jemaa el-Fnaa.
Egypt runs the second-highest, driven by Khan el-Khalili and the
Giza approach. Turkey and India run smaller-volume variants with
the same architecture.

The pattern travels with the henna craft tradition and with
sunset-square pedestrian density. Cities with henna culture but
broad sidewalks and weak group-surround geometry produce far less
of it (Tunis medina, Aswan markets, Hyderabad bazaars). Cities
without the craft tradition do not produce it at all (most of East
Asia, all of the Americas).

## The five red flags

Two or more in the first ten seconds of an approach: hands in
pockets, walk through without stopping.

- **A woman in a colorful kaftan, sari, or local dress holding a
  small cone of brown paste in her right hand.** The cone is the
  initiator.
- **A *free, lucky, just one minute* opening line.** This is the
  Manufactured Reciprocity bait.
- **A second or third person standing behind you or to the side
  at the moment of the approach.** This is the group-surround
  signature.
- **A reach toward your wrist while you are mid-sentence saying
  no.** The artist's hand is moving before your refusal completes.
- **A *black* henna or a *develops in three hours* claim.** This
  is the PPD chemical-risk variant.

## The phrases that shut it down

Stated while continuing to walk, hands in pockets, wrists covered:

| Language | Phrase | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| English | "No, thanks. Hands off." (firm, hand to wrist) |  |
| Arabic (Morocco) | *La, shukran, ma kannchih.* | No, thanks, I don't want it. |
| French (Morocco) | *Non, merci, pas de henné.* | No, thanks, no henna. |
| Arabic (Egypt) | *La, shukran, mish ayza henna.* | No, thanks, I don't want henna. |
| Turkish | *Hayır, teşekkürler, kına istemiyorum.* | No, thanks, I don't want henna. |
| Hindi | *Nahin, dhanyavaad, mehndi nahin chahiye.* | No, thanks, I don't want henna. |

If the cone is already on your wrist: do not pay. Walk through the
group, around any blocker, into the open square. The unfinished
design fades in three to ten days. The cash demand is zero.

## If this happens to you

You handed over the dirham equivalent of fifteen US dollars to
make the encounter end. The henna design fades in seven to ten
days. The cash is mostly unrecoverable.

For the cash recovery: the cash is gone. The actionable response
is preventive for the next square. The hands-in-pockets reflex is
trainable in one trip; you do not encounter the variant again on
the same trip if you train the reflex on day one.

For PPD chemical reactions (skin redness, burning, blistering, or
darkening at the application site within twenty-four to seventy-two
hours): visit a local clinic same day. Marrakech: Polyclinique du
Sud or Clinique Internationale. Cairo: Cleopatra Hospital or
As-Salam International. Istanbul: Acıbadem or Memorial Hospitals.
Jaipur: Fortis or Sawai Man Singh. The clinics know the PPD
reaction pattern; emergency oral and topical steroids reduce
permanent scarring if started within seventy-two hours.

For permanent skin reaction or scarring: photograph the site daily
for two weeks, document the application location and the artist
description, and consult a dermatologist on return home. PPD
sensitization is permanent and triggers later reactions to hair
dye, leather, and rubber. Document for medical records. Travel
insurance covers documented PPD-reaction medical care with a
clinic report.

For pressure-tactic complaints: Marrakech Tourist Police 19, Cairo
Tourist Police 126, Istanbul Tourist Police 155, Indian Tourist
Helpline 1363. The reports do not retrieve the dirham but
contribute to enforcement waves at the named squares. Royal
Gendarmerie of Morocco runs periodic enforcement at Jemaa el-Fnaa
based on accumulated complaints.

Margie's sister handed over a hundred dirham. She refused the
second hundred. The artist accepted and walked off. The other
two women dispersed. The flower on the back of her sister's hand
was brown-green, not black; the henna was real, not PPD-laden.
It faded over nine days. They had mint tea on the Café de France
rooftop and watched the square fill with drums and torches as
the call to prayer started. The next morning, on the way to the
licensed Maison de la Tisseuse cooperative in Mouassine, Margie
noticed they were both walking with their hands tucked into the
bag straps without thinking about it.
