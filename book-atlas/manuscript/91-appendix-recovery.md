# Appendix B: The post-scam recovery playbook

This appendix is what to do if a scam in this book happens to you. It
generalizes across countries. The country book for your destination
contains the specific addresses, phone numbers, and embassy details for
that location. Use this appendix as the framework, the country book
as the lookup.

The playbook has four windows: the first fifteen minutes, the first
hour, the first twenty-four hours, and the first week. Each window is
short on purpose. Do the items in order. Do not skip ahead.

## The first fifteen minutes

This is the window when most card-fraud damage happens. A scammer who
has captured your card details, photographed your card, or run a
contactless tap on a hidden reader has minutes to make purchases or
move money before automated fraud monitoring catches up. The first
fifteen minutes are the ones that limit the damage.

**1. Get to a safe place.** A hotel lobby, a major restaurant, a
police station, or any well-lit public space. Do not walk back along
the route where the scam happened. Do not confront the scammer. Most
operators disengage and disappear in under a minute. You disengage by
walking the other way.

**2. Lock your cards.** Open your bank app. Most major banks have a
"lock card" or "freeze card" toggle. Toggle it on. This pauses the
card without canceling it, so legitimate transactions reverse cleanly
once you confirm the fraud. If your bank does not have a lock toggle,
call the bank's international fraud line, which is on the back of the
card and saved in your password manager (the *Pre-Trip Checklist*,
item six).

**3. Check the most recent transactions.** Open the transaction list.
Anything you do not recognize from the last hour is a fraud item.
Screenshot it. The screenshot is the proof for the dispute later.

**4. Confirm what was taken physically.** Wallet. Phone. Passport.
Bag. Inventory in your head and out loud if you are with someone.
Mental clarity at this stage is high because adrenaline is working
in your favor. Use it.

**5. Photograph the scene if you can do it safely.** The street name,
the storefront, the crosswalk. The CCTV recovery window depends on
this. Do not return to the location to get the photograph if you have
already left.

## The first hour

This is the window when filing the police report matters most. The
report unlocks travel-insurance claims, replaces a passport, and
creates the evidence trail that any subsequent dispute or investigation
will depend on.

**1. File a police report.** Walk to the nearest station. The country
book for your destination lists tourist-police stations by neighborhood.
If you are in a country with no separate tourist police, the regular
station works. Bring your passport, your card statements (the
screenshot from the first fifteen minutes), and any photographs.

The report is paperwork, not investigation. Police are unlikely to
recover the money or arrest the operator. The report's value is its
existence: a numbered document with the date, time, and amounts. That
document is what the insurance company, the credit card issuer, and
your embassy will all ask for.

**2. Get the report number in writing.** A photograph of the printed
report is fine. A receipt with the case number is fine. A handwritten
note on a hotel napkin is not fine. Insist on documented, official
paper.

**3. Call your bank's fraud line.** Even if you locked the card in
the first fifteen minutes, the formal fraud claim has to be opened
verbally with a representative. They will ask for the police report
number. The case is escalated as soon as both pieces are in their
system.

**4. Notify your travel insurer.** Most travel insurance policies
require notification within twenty-four hours but reward early
notification. If you bought travel insurance through your credit card
(many premium cards include it), the issuer's app or website usually
has a "make a claim" form. Fill the basics now. The detail can come
later.

**5. Tell someone outside the country.** A family member, a friend, a
co-worker. A short message: where you are, what happened, that you
are safe. Two reasons. First, if anything escalates, the person
outside the country can contact your embassy on your behalf if your
own ability to do so becomes compromised. Second, having said it out
loud once makes the next twenty-four hours easier.

## The first twenty-four hours

This is the window when administrative work compounds. Most of it is
straightforward, but skipping a step here means complicated paperwork
in the months ahead.

**1. Replace your passport, if it went.** Walk to your country's
embassy or consulate. Bring a passport photograph (the embassy can
take one if you do not have one), the police report, and any
identification you still have. Most embassies issue an emergency
travel document within twenty-four to seventy-two hours that gets you
home. The full passport replacement happens once you are back.

If your country does not have an embassy in the city you are in,
the country book identifies the nearest one. It also lists consular-
protection arrangements between allied embassies. Most Commonwealth
countries assist each other. Many EU countries assist any EU citizen.

**2. Replace cards.** Your bank will overnight a replacement to a
hotel address if you ask. Some banks (Capital One, Chase, HSBC) have
international branch networks where you can collect cash on a
temporary card the same day. Ask the bank's fraud line about
options when you call them.

**3. Replace your phone, if it went.** A new phone is a several-
hundred-dollar problem and a several-hour problem. Local phone
shops near major hotels usually stock used iPhones and Androids.
Buy used. Restore from cloud backup at the hotel. If your phone
provider has a SIM-replacement clause, call the international
service number (saved in your travel notes from the *Pre-Trip Checklist*) to
swap a new SIM into the new phone.

**4. Re-photograph everything.** Passport. New cards. Hotel address.
Itinerary. Travel insurance number. The password-manager copies
from the *Pre-Trip Checklist* still exist, but the new physical
objects need new photographs.

**5. Eat something. Sleep.** Recovery work is exhausting. Adrenaline
gives you a head start. By the twenty-four-hour mark it is gone, and
the cognitive cost of every remaining administrative task gets higher.
Sleep is part of the playbook.

## The first week

The first week is the window when the financial recovery becomes real
or fails to become real, depending on whether the paperwork from the
first day was complete.

**1. Confirm card disputes are filed.** Most banks file the dispute
within twenty-four to forty-eight hours of the verbal claim. Confirm
with a follow-up call or the bank's app. The dispute is officially
filed only when you have a dispute number, not just a fraud number.

**2. File the travel insurance claim.** The full claim with all
supporting documents (police report, card statements, receipts where
you have them, hotel records of where you stayed). Most insurers
respond within seven to ten business days for claims under five
thousand dollars; longer for catastrophic cases.

**3. Decide whether to continue the trip.** This is a personal
decision and depends on what happened. Most travelers continue.
Some don't. The trip you have planned is not less worthwhile because
of what happened, and the country you are in is not less worth
visiting. But you are tired, your trust is depleted, and decisions
made tired and depleted are bad ones. Take the day. Sleep on it.

**4. Write down what happened.** Date, time, location, exact words
spoken, exact amounts. The detail you remember at one week is
detail you will lose by month one. The written record is what you
will need if any dispute escalates.

**5. Tell us.** Email editorial@tabiji.ai. Your report goes into
next year's edition. Every report makes the book sharper for the
traveler who reads it after you. We try to answer every email. We do
not publish identifying details without permission.

## What this playbook is not

It is not a guarantee. Some scams (the catastrophic-tier chapters
indexed in Appendix E) cause damage that the playbook can mitigate
but cannot reverse. A drink-spiking
incident leaves traces of itself for weeks. An express-kidnapping
has psychological aftermath that the playbook does not address. For
those, refer to Appendix H, *After you've been scammed*, which
addresses the thirty-day window beyond what this appendix covers.

It is not legal advice. Every country's reporting and dispute laws
are different. The playbook reflects what works in most travel
destinations most of the time. Local counsel is appropriate in
catastrophic cases.

It is, however, the best general framework we have, drawn from
hundreds of post-scam reports filed by travelers worldwide. Following
it will not guarantee recovery. Skipping it makes recovery much less
likely.
