A scam works because it has been run a thousand times before yours. The
woman who bends down to pick up a gold ring on the Pont des Arts is not
improvising. The man at Cairo's Terminal 3 with the lanyard saying
"official taxi" is not freelancing. The polo-shirted "Delhi Tourism"
agent at New Delhi Railway Station is not new on the job. Each of them
is reading a script that was old when their grandfathers ran it. The
script works because you have never seen it before, and they have run
it ten times this week.

This book documents thirty of those scripts.

Each chapter is one scam. We picked them because we have evidence,
across countries, that the same play runs in the same way on
travelers who have never met each other and never will. The Gold
Ring Trick that ran on a New Zealand backpacker in Florence in 2019
is the same play that ran on an American retiree on the Pont des
Arts in 2025 and the same play that ran on a Korean honeymoon
couple in Las Ramblas last spring. The currency changes. The
language costume changes. The mechanic does not.

That is the proposition of this book. Thirty mechanics, the most
documented and most-encountered scams in the global tourist
corridor, every one of them traveling unchanged across borders.
Learn the mechanics and you do not need to memorize a thousand
local variants. You will spot a brand-new costume the first second
it appears.

## What this book is not

It is not a list of countries to fear. The countries where these scams
run are also the countries with the most beautiful and worthwhile travel
in the world. Italy has both pickpockets at the Roma Termini ticket
machines and the Sistine Chapel. Egypt has both the Giza camel-handler
hostage and the Karnak hypostyle hall at golden hour. India has both
the Paharganj rebooking trap and the silence at sunrise over the
Ganges. The presence of scams is not a reason to stay home. The presence
of unprepared travelers is the reason scams thrive.

It is also not a fear book. Most travelers who read this will return
home untouched, and that is the point. The scams in here are concentrated
in tourist corridors, run on travelers who look unprepared, and disengage
the moment they realize they are reading a script you have already
read. Five minutes of preparation per scam buys you a calmer trip and
a smaller wallet pulled less often.

It is not a country guide. We have those, one per country, twenty-four
volumes deep and growing. Each country book takes a destination and
runs through every scam in that country with the local detail you need
the week before your flight. This book runs in the other direction.
One scam, all countries. Read this first. Read your destination volume
second.

It is not a comprehensive list of every scam ever run on a traveler.
There are more than thirty. The full atlas at tabiji.ai/scams/atlas
documents over sixty. We picked the thirty highest-impact and
most-frequent for the bound book, all of them meeting our
three-confirmation rule (see Appendix I, *Sources and methodology*)
and traveling across at least two countries unchanged. A scam that runs
only in Marrakech and only on Tuesdays does not make this book. A scam
that runs in Marrakech, Cairo, Istanbul, Mumbai, and Bangkok with the
same opening line does.

## How this book reads

The book has three parts.

**Part I** is the most important section in the book, and the shortest.
It documents the seven universal patterns that underlie almost every
scam in the rest of the book. The Captive-Position Lever. The
Authority Costume. The Sub-Market Quote. The Commission Detour. The
Made-Up Closure. The Brand-Mimicry Storefront. Manufactured
Reciprocity. Seven fingerprints. Thirty costumes. If you read no
further than Part I, you will spot eighty
percent of the scripts in this book the moment they begin running on
you.

**Part II** is the thirty-scam atlas. Thirty chapters, alphabetical
by scam name, each one a single scam. Every chapter follows the same structure. A scene
in which one of our four recurring travelers (Margie, Priya, Harry, or
Marcus, or sometimes an anonymous stand-in for a profile that none of
them fits) walks into the trap. The mechanic. The pricing tiers, where
they exist. The geography. Five red flags. The exit phrases in three
or four languages. A recovery sequence for the first sixty minutes if
the trap closed. Cross-references to the country books where you can
read the local variant in detail.

**Part III** is the appendices. The exit-phrase index spans eleven
languages. The recovery playbook generalizes across countries. The
emergency contacts cover every country with a Tabiji book. The
by-traveler-type index helps the solo female traveler, the over-sixty
traveler, the parent traveling with kids, the digital nomad, and the
budget backpacker each find the chapters most relevant to them. The
by-severity index separates the catastrophic from the merely annoying.
The glossary translates every cultural term in the book. The scope
clarifier names the adjacent categories of risk this book deliberately
does not cover — cybercrime, drug incidents, abductions — and points
you to where to read about each. The aftermath chapter covers the
thirty days after a scam, beyond the first hour. The
methodology essay is for the reader who wants to know how Tabiji
researches and why some scams do not make the cut.

## The four recurring travelers

Throughout Part II you will meet four travelers more than once.

**Margie** is sixty-two, recently retired, traveling in the years
she finally has the time. She has more savings to spend on a trip
than she has had in her life and has therefore become exactly the
demographic several of these scams optimize for. She appears in
the chapters where social grace, shopping pressure, and the
politeness reflex of an older traveler get exploited: a Sydney
art-market stall, a back-room carpet showroom in Istanbul, an
inflated *coperto* in Trastevere, a Marrakech henna ambush.

**Priya** is thirty-four, solo female, several years into the
kind of career that lets her take three weeks off twice a year
to go somewhere new. She is scammed less often than the others
because she has read more before she landed, but the chapters
where she appears are the ones a prepared solo traveler still
encounters: a Buenos Aires *cueva*, a Mexico City taxi at 2 a.m.,
a Vatican blank-PDF scalper, a Pont des Arts gold ring, a Beijing
tea-house invitation.

**Harry** is sixty-four, traveled enough to think he has seen
most of this before. The chapters where Harry appears are the
ones where seeing most of it before is exactly the vulnerability
the scam exploits: a Mykonos beach club's hidden minimum, a
Bangkok Sukhumvit drink-spike, a Sharm el-Sheikh hotel-lobby
shakedown, a Jaipur gem-shop pitch, a Times Square three-card
monte.

**Marcus** is thirty-four, a digital nomad, lives out of a
thirty-five-liter backpack, makes most of his bookings on his
phone in the airport he is leaving. The chapters where Marcus
appears target the rushed, the Wi-Fi-only, and the
I-can-figure-this-out-on-the-go traveler: an Airbnb apartment
that does not exist, a phishing email styled like Booking.com,
a tampered transit-kiosk QR sticker, a counterfeit *controllore*
on the Rome–Florence Frecciarossa.

The four are composite figures. Each scene they walk through is drawn
from real reports filed by real travelers. We changed some names. The
scams happened.

## A note on the year

This is the 2026 edition. We update annually. By the time you read this,
some currency amounts will be slightly higher, a handful of named
addresses will have moved, and one or two scams will have been added or
deprecated as scammers iterate and law enforcement catches up. The
mechanics, however, are stable. They have been stable for decades. They
will be stable for decades more.

The 2027 edition will be a free re-download from your Amazon library if
you bought this one on Kindle. The same is true every year, for as long
as we keep publishing.
