# C · Currency Exchange Cambio

> **Pattern: Sub-Market Quote** · 4 mechanics · 13 countries · 5–20% silent loss per transaction · *Updated April 2026*

## The clipboard

Marcus walked south on Calle Florida from the obelisk toward Plaza
de Mayo at three in the afternoon on a Wednesday. The pedestrian
strip was full: tango performers, leather-shop hawkers, day-trip
groups. On every block a different man shouted *"¡Cambio, cambio!
Dollar, euro, real! Best rate today!"*

One of them, mid-thirties, friendly, gestured with a clipboard
showing handwritten rates. USD to ARS at 1,420 pesos per dollar.

The official banking rate that morning had been 1,250.

Marcus had two hundred US dollars in twenties. The Western Union
pickup he had used the day before had given him 1,250 per dollar.
This was 170 pesos better per dollar, on two hundred dollars, or
about thirty-four thousand pesos. About twenty-seven dollars. Worth
the walk.

The man could not exchange on the street. He led Marcus down a
side passage off Florida between *Galerías Pacífico* and the
leather-goods row, into a small unmarked apartment on the second
floor of a turn-of-the-century building. A woman behind a counter
took the cash, fanned the twenties, nodded.

*"Excelente,"* she said. *"But the rate today, at this hour, you
understand, is 1,250. Not 1,420. The rate on the street is for
higher-volume transactions only. For two hundred dollars I can
offer 1,250."*

Marcus had started the transaction expecting 284,000 pesos.

He was now being offered 250,000.

The 34,000-peso difference, about twenty-seven dollars, was the
*cueva* margin on the rate-switch variant.

## The trick

The pattern is Sub-Market Quote. The rate the *arbolito* (the
*little tree*, the street hustler) calls is too good to walk away
from. The rate is the bait. The actual exchange
happens in a back-room *cueva* (an unmarked exchange office) where
the rate switches, the count is fast, or one or two counterfeit
notes mix into the stack.

Calle Florida runs the play continuously through the trading day.
The Argentine BCRA (the central bank), the *Comisaría Turística* on
Avenida Corrientes, and the Policía de la Ciudad have logged the
*arbolito*-to-*cueva* pattern for two decades.

Under the 2024–2025 Milei reforms the historic *blue dollar*
arbitrage has largely collapsed. The legal Western Union pickup
rate now matches or exceeds the street rate, with zero counterfeit
risk. The *arbolitos* are still on Florida every afternoon. They
are working on the assumption that arriving tourists do not know
that.

The defense is one decision made before the trip: use the official
channels (Western Union pickup, Wise, Revolut, major-bank ATMs at
branch lobbies) and never enter the cambio supply chain. The
*arbolito*-to-*cueva* play cannot operate against a tourist who
already has pesos from a Western Union counter.

## The four mechanics

**Street arbolito to back-room cueva.** An *arbolito* shouts
*"¡Cambio! ¡Cambio! Best rate!"* and offers a rate that beats the
official banking rate by 10–15 percent. The *arbolito* is the
front-end of a *cueva* operation. The street rate is bait. The
actual exchange happens in a back room where rate-switch,
counterfeit-mix, or short-count fraud follows. Most reported in:
Buenos Aires Calle Florida, Tangier ferry exit, Cancún Hotel Zone,
Cairo Khan el-Khalili, Istanbul Sultanahmet.

**Airport-window markup.** Airport exchange counters charge 8–15
percent over interbank with hidden commission lines on the receipt.
The Global Exchange chain at Moroccan and Mexican airports posts
rates 15–20 percent worse than interbank plus a 3–5 percent
commission line buried at the bottom of the receipt. The
transaction itself is legitimate (real cash, correct count) but the
rate is pure tourist tax. Most reported at: Casablanca CMN, Cancún
CUN, Cairo CAI, Istanbul IST, Marrakech RAK, Mexico City MEX.

**Back-room cueva rate-switch.** An *arbolito* leads you off the
public street into an unmarked apartment or shop. The cashier
behind the counter quotes a rate 5–15 percent lower than what was
promised, framed as a "commission" or "today's rate at this hour."
Variants include fast-fan-count (bills counted face-down too fast
for verification), counterfeit-mix (one or two fakes slipped in),
and rapid sleight-of-hand swaps. Most reported in: Buenos Aires
Microcentro *cuevas*, Caracas downtown, La Paz Sopocachi.

**Small-bill short-count.** The cashier counts audibly to the agreed
amount, slides the stack to you, then takes back "one to recount"
or "one to verify against the watermark" and palms it. The total
comes back one to three notes short. Runs at small cambio shops,
hotel front desks, and tourist-zone exchange counters globally.
Most reported in: small Cancún Hotel Zone resorts, Cairo Khan
el-Khalili, Istanbul Grand Bazaar, Bangkok Sukhumvit.

## Where it runs (13 countries, Argentina and Morocco co-dominant)

Cambio scams concentrate where two conditions overlap: structural
currency mismatch (high inflation, weak local currency, or capital
controls) and dense tourist foot traffic that creates a stable
supply of marks. Latin America, North Africa, and Southeast Asia
account for most documented variants.

Argentina and Morocco co-dominate. Argentina drives the *arbolito*
volume on Calle Florida. Morocco drives the airport-window markup
volume at Casablanca CMN. Egypt runs both variants at smaller scale.
Vietnam runs the small-bill short-count at hotel front desks.
Mexico runs the airport-window variant at CUN and the resort-
cashier variant on the Hotel Zone.

You will see less of this in countries with stable currencies and
robust ATM networks (Western and Northern Europe, Japan, the United
States). When the local currency is stable, the cambio supply chain
has no margin to extract.

## The five red flags

Two or more at the moment of approach: walk away.

- **A street rate quoted above the official banking rate.** The
  banking rate is the floor for legitimate exchange. Anything above
  is bait or carries embedded counterfeits.
- **An exchange that requires moving off the public street.**
  Legitimate exchanges happen at the counter visible to passersby.
- **A counter with no posted rate.** Reputable exchange offices post
  the buy and sell rates on a board updated daily.
- **A receipt with a hidden "commission" line in small type.** Real
  commission is disclosed before the transaction.
- **A cashier who takes a note back to "verify" after handing the
  stack to you.** The verification is the swap.

## The phrases that shut it down

Said while continuing to walk, no eye contact:

| Language | Phrase | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| Spanish | *No, gracias, ya tengo pesos.* | No thanks, I already have pesos. |
| French (Maghreb) | *Non merci, je n'ai pas besoin de change.* | No thanks, I don't need to exchange. |
| Arabic | *La shukran, la urīd taghyīr.* | No thank you, I don't want to exchange. |
| Turkish | *Hayır teşekkürler, paraya ihtiyacım yok.* | No thanks, I don't need money. |
| English | "No thanks, I'm using Western Union." |  |

The *I'm using Western Union* line specifically signals to Buenos
Aires *arbolitos* that you know the post-Milei math.

## If this happens to you

You exchanged at a cambio and the count came up short, the rate
switched in the back room, or the airport window charged fifteen
percent over interbank. Recovery depends on which variant ran and
where you noticed.

Within five minutes (best case): if you are still at the cashier
window, count the stack again loudly and announce the discrepancy.
Most cashiers reverse the take when caught at the window because
the alternative is the police being called. Once you have walked
away, recovery is near zero.

Within thirty minutes: file a report at the local tourist police.
The report number is required by your travel-insurance carrier for
currency-fraud claims (covered up to a per-incident cap on most
premium-tier policies, typically 200 to 500 USD).

Within twenty-four hours: if cards or accounts were exposed (some
cambio scams pair with QR-code phishing or skimming), call your
card issuer and freeze. Most premium-tier travel cards include
currency-fraud insurance up to a small cap.

The cambio loss itself is rarely recoverable. The actionable
response is preventive for the next encounter, not recovery for
this one. Treat the loss as cost-of-traveling and update the
routine: Wise or Revolut card plus major-bank ATM in every country
going forward.

Marcus walked back onto Calle Florida with his 250,000 pesos in
his backpack and stopped at a Western Union four blocks south on
Avenida de Mayo. He sent himself two hundred dollars. The pickup
rate the next morning was 1,310 pesos per dollar. The two hundred
dollars came back as 262,000 pesos. He used the *cueva* loss as a
reminder.
