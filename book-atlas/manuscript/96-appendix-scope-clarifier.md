# Appendix G: Beyond tourist scams (what is not in this book)

This book covers tourist scams. That is a specific category, and the
boundaries matter. Several adjacent categories of fraud target
travelers but are different in mechanics, defense, and recovery. We
do not cover them here. This appendix is the scope clarifier so you
know where to look when something happens that this book does not
address.

The categories below all share with tourist scams the property that
they target travelers. They differ in operating zone, evidence trail,
and the legal and financial recovery available.

## What this book does not cover

### 1. Visa and immigration fraud

"Expedited visa" services that promise to fast-track your tourist
visa for a fee, "official visa renewal" calls that ask for your
passport details over WhatsApp, "visa extension" agencies near land
borders that claim official authority. None of these are tourist
scams in our taxonomy, and most jurisdictions treat visa fraud as a
serious crime with separate consular protocols.

If a visa-related interaction feels off, the right response is to
hang up, decline, and contact your embassy directly. Refer to the
country book for your destination for the embassy's after-hours line.

### 2. International romance scams

The traveler met online who builds an emotional relationship over
weeks or months, then asks for money for a ticket, a medical
emergency, a customs problem, or a visa. The pattern overlaps with
some travel-side scams in this book but the mechanic is fundamentally
different: it is built on relationship rather than situational
pressure, and it scales over time rather than minutes.

Romance scams are well-covered elsewhere. The U.S. Federal Trade
Commission, the U.K. Action Fraud unit, and Interpol all publish
detailed defensive material specifically for this category. If you
have been or are being targeted, those are the right resources.

### 3. Investment fraud during travel

The "limited time" property investment in Mexico, the "discounted
shares" of a hotel chain pitched at a tourist resort, the "diamond
wholesale" opportunity at a Cape Town safari lodge. These are
financial fraud, not tourist scams. They overlap geographically with
tourist scams but the evidence trail (financial regulators, civil
courts) and recovery options (securities-fraud divisions) are
entirely different.

If a tourist-zone interaction pivots toward investment, the right
move is to leave the room. Always. No legitimate investment
opportunity requires you to commit while on vacation.

### 4. Identity theft via Wi-Fi or device compromise

Public Wi-Fi networks at airports and hotels can be man-in-the-middle
exploited to capture login credentials. The compromise sometimes
shows up as fraudulent purchases or account takeovers weeks after
the trip. This is computer security, not tourist scam, even when
the operator is sitting in the same coffee shop as the victim.

Use a VPN on public Wi-Fi. Avoid logging into banking apps on hotel
Wi-Fi unless you have a trusted VPN running. Replace passwords
periodically. The Electronic Frontier Foundation publishes good
defensive material on this.

### 5. Workplace and business-trip fraud

The "client" who wants to meet at an unfamiliar address in a city
you do not know. The "supplier" who needs an urgent advance for a
shipment. The "conference badge" issued by a company that turns out
not to be the conference organizer. Business-trip fraud overlaps
with travel but operates inside a working framework and often has
legal recovery paths through corporate fraud channels rather than
tourist police.

If a business interaction during a trip feels off, contact your
company's security team before you respond. Do not negotiate from
the hotel room.

### 6. Trafficking and abduction

In a small number of cases each year, what begins as a tourist scam
escalates into trafficking-related crime. This is a narrower category
than the catastrophic-tier scams in Appendix E and is concentrated in
specific cities and corridors documented by the U.S. State Department
and equivalent authorities elsewhere. We mention it for completeness;
it is not covered here.

If you are concerned about trafficking-related risk in a specific
destination, the State Department's Trafficking in Persons Report
is the canonical reference. Read it before you travel to any country
where this is a concern.

### 7. Domestic-tourism scams in your home country

A tourist in their own country is still a tourist, and many of the
scams in this book run in domestic contexts (Times Square in New
York, the Las Vegas Strip, the M25 highway in the UK, the Champs-
Élysées in Paris targeting French citizens from other regions).
We focus on international travel, but most of the chapters apply
unchanged when read about your own country.

### 8. Drug-trade-adjacent scams

Some chapters in this book mention drug-related operations as the
mechanism (the fake drug-deal tourist trap, the fake drug-search
police sting). We cover these because the scams themselves are
non-drug scams that use drug-trade signaling. We do not cover
actual drug-trade fraud, which is a different and more dangerous
category we recommend avoiding entirely.

### 9. Banking and ATM fraud at scale

A few chapters touch on small-scale ATM compromise (skimming,
the ATM currency-conversion trap). Larger-scale banking fraud (cards
used after the trip, account-takeover fraud, online wire fraud)
is a separate category covered better by the bank's fraud department
and your country's financial-fraud reporting framework.

If you suspect banking fraud after a trip, call the bank's fraud
line first. A police report may be required for the dispute and your
local financial-fraud authority (Action Fraud in the UK, IC3 in
the US, the Australian Cybercrime Online Reporting Network) is the
right escalation.

### 10. Catfishing and influencer scams

The "luxury hotel review" pitch from an Instagram account that turns
out to be running a paid-promotion racket. The "free travel" social
media offer that requires upfront payments. These are influencer-
economy scams, not tourist scams. Defensive material is published by
the relevant social media platforms; we do not cover them.

## The decision tree

If something has happened to you on a trip and you are not sure which
book or which authority to consult:

1. Did it happen at a physical location with another physical
   person? If yes, this book likely covers it. Check the chapter
   list. Refer to Appendix B for the recovery playbook.

2. Did it happen online or over a messaging platform? If yes, it is
   likely covered better by the relevant platform's fraud department
   and your country's online-fraud authority. Refer to your bank,
   your card issuer, and the cybercrime reporting line in Appendix C
   for your country.

3. Does it involve money you sent before you traveled, or money
   sent after you returned? If yes, it is likely a separate
   category (advance-fee fraud, romance fraud, investment fraud).
   See the relevant section above.

4. Is it ongoing? If yes, the priority is safety, not recovery.
   Walk to a public space. Call the local emergency number. Notify
   your embassy. The recovery question can wait until you are safe.

## What we do cover

Thirty tourist-zone scams that travelers encounter on the ground in
the cities they visit. The mechanics, the defenses, the recovery
paths. The patterns that underlie them all. The web atlas at
tabiji.ai/scams/atlas covers a broader catalog of additional scam
types beyond the thirty in this book.

If your situation does not match the scope above, this book may not
be the right resource, but the authorities listed in this appendix
will be. Save the relevant phone number before you travel. Most
expensive scam stories begin with the phrase "I didn't know who to
call."
