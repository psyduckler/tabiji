# F · Fake Government Tourist Office

> **Pattern: Authority Costume / Brand-Mimicry Storefront** · 4 mechanics · 7 countries · 30–200 USD per incident · *Updated April 2026*

## The booth

You exit Bangkok Suvarnabhumi at eleven on a Saturday night. The
arrivals concourse is crowded; signs alternate Thai and English. Twenty
meters past customs, just inside the public area, a brightly lit
booth reads TOURIST INFORMATION CENTER in red letters with a faded
gold logo that vaguely resembles the Tourism Authority of Thailand
seal. Two women in matching polo shirts sit behind the counter. A
third stands with a clipboard.

The clipboard woman waves at you.

*"Sir, Tourist Information, hotel booking, taxi to city, all here,
very fast, very cheap."*

You stop. You ask how much for the taxi to your hotel in Sukhumvit.
She quotes 800 baht. The official airport public-taxi metered rate
is approximately 350–450 baht plus tolls of 50–70 baht. Her quote
is roughly double. She hands you a printed brochure with hotel
options. The cheapest is 1,800 baht per night for a property listed
on Booking.com at 1,100 baht.

You verify the booth on your phone. The TAT website,
tourismthailand.org, lists official TAT counters at Suvarnabhumi:
one in the public area near Gate 4, one at the AOT Limousine
counter inside arrivals. The booth in front of you is labeled
TOURIST INFORMATION CENTER but does not appear on the official
directory. The faded gold logo is private branding designed to
mimic the TAT seal.

You walk past. The official Public Taxi queue is sixty meters
beyond, signed in English and supervised by AOT Airport staff in
visible uniforms. The metered fare to your Sukhumvit hotel comes to
430 baht with the toll. The booth would have charged you 800. The
booked-direct hotel is 1,100. The booth's hotel was 1,800. Total
avoided overcharge: about thirty US dollars.

## The trick

Fake government tourist offices are the Brand-Mimicry Storefront
variant of the Authority Costume family. The booth sits in the
arrivals concourse, the medina gate, or the corner near a major
attraction. The signage reads TOURIST INFORMATION or GOVERNMENT
TOURIST OFFICE in official-looking red and gold. The polo shirts
match. The brochure rack carries a national tourism authority
logo. None of it is real.

The booth runs three commission streams: hotel kickbacks of thirty
to sixty percent above market, taxi quotes of one and a half to
two times the metered rate, and tour bookings at twice the
GetYourGuide / Klook / Viator price. The traveler pays the booth's
quote because the official-looking signage suggests a sanctioned
service.

The defense is one five-second rule: verify the office address
against the official tourism authority website before engaging.

- Thailand: tourismthailand.org
- Egypt: egypt.travel
- Morocco: visitmorocco.com
- Vietnam: vietnam.travel
- Turkey: goturkiye.com
- Indonesia: indonesia.travel
- India: incredibleindia.org

Fake offices are never on the official directory. The phone check
resolves the question before any money changes hands.

The defense in depth is the refusal of curb-side and driver-
initiated office redirects. Real tourist information offices are
inside terminal buildings or at posted city locations on a
tourism-authority list. Curb-side booths and "let me show you a
special office on the way" detours are the variant by definition.

## The four mechanics

**Airport arrivals booth.** The most-documented variant. Booths in
the arrivals concourse run hotel and tour kickbacks at 30–60
percent above market. Real official tourism counters exist at
these airports but the fake booths sit closer to the customs exit
and capture tired arriving travelers first. Most reported in
Bangkok Suvarnabhumi, Cairo CAI Terminal 1, Hanoi Noi Bai Terminal
2, Istanbul IST arrivals, Bali Ngurah Rai curb, Marrakech RAK
arrivals.

**Taxi-driver / tuk-tuk redirect.** A taxi or tuk-tuk driver, en
route to your stated destination, insists on a brief stop at *a
special tourist information office I always recommend*. The
"office" is a kickback shop (craft shop, leather souk, unlicensed
tour operator). The driver takes commission on any purchase or
booking. Most reported in Marrakech medina taxi rides, Cairo
airport taxis, Bangkok tuk-tuk circuits, Hanoi cyclo and taxi
rides.

**Closed-attraction redirect.** A stranger near a major attraction
tells you it is closed today and offers to redirect you to a
"tourist information office" or alternative. The "office" is a
kickback craft shop or fake museum. Overlaps with the Closed-
Attraction Redirect chapter but specifically frames the redirect
through tourist-office authority. Most reported at Bangkok Grand
Palace and Wat Phra Kaew approaches, Istanbul Sultanahmet near
Hagia Sophia and the Blue Mosque, Cairo Egyptian Museum and Khan
el-Khalili.

**Brochure-display fake office.** A small shop or streetside stall
displays prominent TOURIST INFORMATION CENTER or GOVERNMENT
TOURIST OFFICE signage with a brochure rack and English-speaking
"staff." The shop sells overpriced tours, hotel bookings, or
directs tourists to kickback taxi services. The signage and
branding mimic real tourism-authority logos. Most reported in
Marrakech medina near Bab Doukkala and Place Jemaa el-Fnaa, Hanoi
Old Quarter near Hoan Kiem Lake, Cairo Khan el-Khalili and Tahrir
Square, Istanbul Sultanahmet and Taksim Square.

## Where it runs (7 countries, Thailand dominant)

Fake government tourist offices concentrate at major airport
arrivals halls and at the entry points to high-traffic medina /
old-town zones. Thailand alone accounts for about a quarter of
global exposure, driven by Bangkok Suvarnabhumi arrivals booth
density. Egypt, Vietnam, Morocco, and Turkey carry the bulk of the
remainder, with Indonesia and India running smaller-volume
variants on the same architecture.

The pattern depends on three structural conditions: large arriving
tourist volume, weak enforcement of the "Tourist Information"
trademark (so private operators can use the language), and
official tourism authorities whose counter locations are not
obvious to a jet-lagged arrival. Cities with strong terminal-
signage discipline (Singapore Changi, Tokyo Narita, Seoul Incheon)
do not produce the variant.

## The five red flags

Two or more in any "tourist office" interaction: walk past, type
the official tourism-authority URL on your phone, find the real
office.

- **A booth or shop sitting at the curb, just past the customs exit,
  or in a doorway corner, rather than at a posted government
  location.** Real tourism information sits at official addresses.
- **A logo that looks like a national tourism authority seal but is
  faded, slightly wrong, or carries a different acronym than the
  official body.**
- **A taxi or tuk-tuk driver insisting on a brief stop at a
  tourist-information office on the way to your stated
  destination.** The detour is the commission funnel.
- **A stranger telling you a major attraction is closed today and
  offering to redirect you to "the tourist office."** Verify
  closures on Google Maps in real time; almost all major
  attractions post real-time hours.
- **An office quoting hotel or tour prices thirty percent or more
  above the price you can find on Booking.com or GetYourGuide on
  your phone.** Quote-versus-app comparison resolves the variant
  in under a minute.

## The phrases that shut it down

Stated while continuing to walk:

| Language | Phrase | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| English | "I have my own information, thanks." (no eye contact, keep walking) |  |
| Thai | *Phŏm/dichăn mee khâw-mun ėeng láaeo, khàwp-khun.* | I have my own information, thanks. |
| Arabic (Egypt) | *Andi maʿloomati, shukran.* | I have my information, thanks. |
| Vietnamese | *Tôi đã có thông tin của mình, cảm ơn.* | I have my own information, thanks. |
| Turkish | *Kendi bilgilerim var, teşekkürler.* | I have my own information, thanks. |
| French (Morocco) | *J'ai mes propres informations, merci.* | I have my own information, thanks. |

The phrase signals you have already pre-booked through legitimate
channels. The variant cannot compete with platform booking and
disengages immediately.

## If this happens to you

You booked a tour through a "Tourist Information Center" that
turned out to be a kickback shop, or paid 60 USD for a 10 USD taxi
at the airport booth.

Within twenty-four hours: file a credit-card chargeback if any
portion was paid by card. Grounds: "service not as described" or
"merchant misrepresentation." Visa and Mastercard chargeback
windows are 60–120 days. Submit the original brochure, the receipt,
a photo of the booth's signage, and a description of what was
actually delivered.

Within seven days: file a complaint with the country's tourism
authority. TAT (Tourism Authority of Thailand), Egyptian Tourist
Authority, ONMT Morocco, Vietnam Tourism Department, the Turkish
Ministry of Culture and Tourism, and the Indonesian Ministry of
Tourism all accept English-language complaints. The complaint helps
the authority track booth concentrations and update advisories.

For closed-attraction redirect cases that ended at a tourist-trap
shop: cash for the shop purchase is rarely recoverable. The time
and inconvenience are unrecoverable. The actionable response is
preventive: book attractions and tours through GetYourGuide, Klook,
or Viator with verified itineraries.

Recovery rates: well-documented cases recover at moderate rates
through credit-card chargeback; outcomes vary by issuer. Tourism-authority complaints are mostly
informational rather than refundable but contribute to next-season
enforcement at the booth concentrations.

You did not engage the booth. You walked past, took the metered
public taxi, checked into the hotel you had booked the week before,
and slept off the flight. The next morning you opened the TAT
official directory at tourismthailand.org and saved the locations
of the real Suvarnabhumi tourism counter and the city-center TAT
office on your phone for the rest of the trip.
