# Scams that aren't

Harry walked through the spice section of the Marrakech souk on a
Tuesday afternoon and was almost certain he had just been ripped off
by a saffron seller. The opening price had been four hundred dirhams
for a tin he later bought down the street for eighty. He spent the
next two hours moody about it. Then he found out, from the riad
owner over dinner, that the seller had quoted four hundred because
that is the opening number in a souk negotiation, that two hundred
would have been the polite closer, that one-fifty would have been a
fair deal, and that walking away over the gap was not how the system
worked. The seller had not been scamming Harry. The seller had been
waiting for Harry to play.

This chapter is about that confusion, costing nothing in money but
plenty in mood. Travelers spend a lot of energy worrying about
behaviors that are not scams. The energy is wasted, and the wariness
it produces makes the real scams harder to spot, because everything
starts to look like one.

What follows are eight common situations that travelers flag as
scams but that are not. If something on your trip looks like one of
these, relax. If something does not look like one of these and looks
like a chapter from Part II, follow Part II.

## 1. The currency-exchange spread

You hand over a hundred dollars at a money-changer in Istanbul. You
get back the equivalent of ninety-three dollars in lira. The seven
dollars looks like theft.

It isn't. It is the spread, the difference between the rate the
exchanger pays for currency and the rate they sell it at. Every legitimate
exchanger in the world charges a spread. It is how they pay rent.
Airport exchangers charge the highest spreads (often 8 to 15 percent).
City exchangers charge lower (3 to 6 percent). Bank ATMs charge the
lowest, usually 1 to 3 percent on top of the interbank rate.

The real scam is the unmarked spread (Chapter C, *Currency Exchange
Cambio*), where the rate is verbally quoted as one number and the
amount handed back reflects another. If the rate is on the board and
the math works out at that rate, you are getting a normal commercial
service.

## 2. Tipping

In countries where tipping is expected (the United States, Mexico, much
of South America, parts of the Middle East), the bill not including a
service charge is not the restaurant cheating you. It is the cultural
norm. The 15 to 20 percent you add is customary and strongly expected
in sit-down restaurants and most tipped contexts.

In countries where tipping is not expected (Japan, South Korea, much
of continental Europe at counter service), being told the bill is the
bill, with no expectation of a tip, is also not a scam. It is the
cultural norm.

Where tipping turns into a real scam is the surprise *coperto* or
*otoshi* charge, where a fee is added to the bill without disclosure
(Chapter C, *Cover Charge (Coperto / Otoshi / Couvert)*). That is real. Routine
restaurant service charges in cultures that expect them are not.

## 3. Cultural haggling

In a Marrakech souk, a Cappadocia carpet shop, an Istanbul Grand Bazaar
stall, a Hanoi market, the opening price is not the real price. It is
the start of a negotiation that the seller expects you to engage with.
Walking away when the seller asks four times what the item is worth is
not a response to a scam. It is the second move in a normal commercial
exchange.

The pattern that turns it into a scam is the pressure-sale closure
(Chapter G, *Gem & Jewelry Shop Pressure*; Chapter C, *Carpet Shop
Pressure Sale*), where the door closes behind you and a tea ceremony
substitutes for a sales meeting. That is the scam. Haggling is not.

## 4. Ride-share fees that look high

Your Uber from Tan Son Nhat in Ho Chi Minh City to District 1 quotes
260,000 dong. The driver who hangs around the curb offers 150,000.

The Uber is not scamming you. The 260,000 reflects: the driver's cut
(70 to 80 percent), Uber's platform fee, surge pricing if it is rush
hour, airport surcharge, fuel costs the driver pays, and a small
profit. The driver at the curb who offers 150,000 is operating outside
the platform, with no insurance, no recorded route, and the option to
abandon you mid-trip if a better fare appears (covered in the web
atlas as *Unlicensed Airport Taxi Overcharge*). The platform fare
is the right fare.

## 5. The hotel "tourist tax"

Many cities and regions levy a tax per person per night, collected at
the hotel, payable in cash at check-in or check-out. Venice. Florence.
Rome. Barcelona. Berlin. Cusco. Most of Switzerland. The tax is a
real tax, going to a real municipal account, usually for tourism
infrastructure or trash collection. Hotels have no choice but to
collect it.

If the receipt names the tax with the amount and the period, you are
paying a tax. If the "tax" appears as a line item on the bill but the
hotel cannot produce a receipt or a tax certificate, that is the
tourist-tax scam (covered in the web atlas as *Tourist Tax Bogus
Levy*). Real tourist
taxes have receipts.

## 6. The drink minimum at a beach bar

Many beach clubs in Miami, Tulum, Mykonos, Phuket, and the Côte d'Azur
operate with a minimum spend per person to sit on a sun lounger. The
minimum is usually printed on the menu or posted at the entrance. It
is a normal commercial structure, especially at high-traffic beaches
where the operator pays for sand maintenance and lifeguard cover.

The scam is the unposted minimum that scales to whatever the operator
thinks you can pay (Chapter B, *Beach Chair Lounger Hustle*). The
posted minimum is just a price.

## 7. The "no-meter" honest taxi in some cities

In a few cities, mostly in Egypt and parts of South Asia, taxis
genuinely do not run meters and the price is genuinely a flat negotiation
at the curb. Cairo. Aswan. Karachi. Some Tehran neighborhoods. The
fixed-fare quote is not always a scam. It is sometimes how the local
taxi market actually works.

The way to tell: ask the hotel concierge or check a recent local guide
what the standard fare is from your origin to your destination. If the
quote is within 20 percent of standard, you are paying the going rate.
If the quote is 3 times standard, you are paying the foreigner premium
(documented in the web atlas as *Taxi Meter Manipulation* and
*Unlicensed Airport
Taxi Overcharge*). Standard is not the scam. Three-times-standard is.

## 8. The polite "tea-and-talk" with a stranger

A Turk on Istiklal Caddesi in Istanbul invites you to a tea house. He
is friendly, his English is good, and the tea is excellent.

Sometimes that is exactly what it is. Turks are some of the most
hospitable people on the planet, and inviting a foreigner for tea is
a normal gesture. Same in Iran. Same in Georgia. Same in many parts
of the Middle East.

The pattern that turns it into a scam is the upsell at the end (Chapter
T, *Tea House Invitation*), where after an hour of conversation a bill
appears for a hundred euros' worth of "premium" tea you didn't order.
If the tea house is a normal café with menu prices on the wall, and
you order from the menu, you are having tea with a stranger. If the
"tea house" has no posted prices and the bill comes at the end
with no receipt, you are reading *Tea House Invitation*.

## The principle

Most behaviors that look strange to a traveler are normal in the place
you are visiting. Most things that turn out to be real scams have a
small set of fingerprints in common (covered in Part I). Match the
fingerprints. Don't match every unfamiliar moment to the worst-case
chapter in this book. The point of reading the book is to be more
relaxed in-country, not less.

If you are not sure, the test is simple. Is there a price you can
verify? Is there a receipt? Is there a posted menu? Is there a meter?
If yes to any of those, the encounter is most likely a normal
commercial transaction. Verify the receipt or menu independently
when stakes are high; the book documents specific cases where
receipts and menus are themselves part of the scam.
If none of them apply, read the chapter that fits the situation and
decide.
