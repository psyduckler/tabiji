## The cambio

Priya was halfway down Calle Florida between Plaza de Mayo and Plaza
San Martín when the chant started from every doorway.

*"¡Cambio, cambio! Dollar, euro!"*

The Buenos Aires sun was fierce on a Thursday afternoon in late
November. Two blocks of *arbolitos* (the *little trees*, the
cambio hustlers who lined the
pedestrian strip and rotated every ten meters). One stepped in
front of her with a small calculator: *"Mil doscientos por dólar
hoy, amiga, best rate in Buenos Aires."*

It was slightly above what her hotel had quoted the night before, so
she slowed for a second look.

That was the whole game.

He waved her toward an upstairs *cueva* (a back-office exchange) off
Florida 165, past a closed clothing-store entrance, up a narrow
stairwell, into a back office with a metal grille and a man behind a
desk. The rate quoted on the street had now dropped to 1,050 pesos
per dollar. *"Exchange rate moved in the last hour, amiga."* She
was already there.

She handed over 200 dollars. The man counted back a stack of 1,000-
and 2,000-peso notes too quickly to follow. The top three were
crisp. The next five were suspiciously flat. One more came from a
separate envelope.

By the time she stepped back onto the sidewalk and paused at a café
to recount, three of the larger notes did not pass the watermark
check, and the total was forty thousand pesos short of the rate the
*arbolito* had originally quoted on the street.

## The trick

The pattern is Sub-Market Quote dressed in Brand-Mimicry Storefront:
a too-good-to-walk-away rate as bait, then a back-room exchange
that looks like a legitimate cambio. Counterfeit-currency-as-change
runs on a five-step routine. The
bait is a sub-market rate or a too-cheap-to-believe deal. The
handoff is a fast count where the bills are fanned with a thumb-
flick. The substitution is a sleight of hand where one or two
counterfeit or short notes mix into a stack of real ones. The
walk-away assumes you will not count carefully until later. The
denial, if you return, is total.

The unifying thread across all four mechanics is the bill that
leaves your hand. Every variant rides the few seconds between the
bill leaving your hand and you noticing the swap.

The defense is the always-count rule. Count cash before it leaves
your hand and after it returns to you. Use a flat surface. Use the
local watermark check (most central banks publish a video showing
the security thread, the holographic strip, and the watermark for
each denomination). Refuse fast counts. If the scammer runs the
fan too fast, ask for a re-count: *Más despacio, por favor* (more
slowly, please).

## The four mechanics

**Taxi bill swap.** You hand the driver a 1,000-peso note for a
700-peso fare. He turns half away to "break it" and produces a
different note from a sleeve: *"This is counterfeit, my friend; you
owe me seven hundred in real money."* The bill in his hand is the
counterfeit. The bill you handed him is in his sleeve. r/BuenosAires
and *Comisaría Turística* incident logs document the routine across
all three Buenos Aires taxi-mafia variants. Most reported in:
Buenos Aires (Microcentro, Recoleta, San Telmo), Mexico City
(*sitio* cabs), Cairo (no-meter taxis from Tahrir Square).

**Small-shop change padding.** You buy a 50-peso coffee with a
1,000-peso note. Change comes back fanned across the counter: eight
or nine notes, two or three of them subtly flat. By the time you
have walked out, three of the larger denominations do not pass the
watermark check. The shop denies all memory of the transaction the
moment you return. Most reported in: Buenos Aires Microcentro
kiosks, Hanoi Old Quarter convenience shops, Cairo Khan el-Khalili
souk stalls, Marrakech medina stalls.

**Cambio counterfeit-mix.** A street hustler calls a rate above the
bank's posted rate and waves you toward a *cueva*, a back-room
bureau, or a curbside handoff at a ferry exit. The rate quoted on
the street drops once you commit. The bills are fanned with a
thumb-flick that is hard to follow. One or two counterfeit or
discontinued notes mix into the stack mid-fan. r/Morocco and
r/argentina threads document the pattern. Street currency exchange
is illegal in Morocco, which is the second layer of the trap. Most
reported in: Buenos Aires Calle Florida, Mendoza Peatonal
Sarmiento, Tangier ferry exit, Casablanca Bab Marrakech, Marrakech
Grand Socco.

**Private-ATM company load.** Standalone ATMs in pharmacies, mini-
markets, hotel lobbies, and gift shops are loaded by their owners
rather than a major bank's cash-handling division. A
small percentage of dispensed notes are counterfeits or
discontinued bills mixed in among genuine ones. The withdrawal
receipt is real. The bills are mixed-quality. Argentine BCRA
bulletins flag the standalone-ATM channel as a primary counterfeit-
distribution route. Defense: use only major-bank ATMs at branch
lobbies (Banco Nación, Santander, BBVA, HSBC, Galicia, Macro in
Argentina; Attijariwafa, BMCE, Banque Populaire in Morocco;
Vietcombank or BIDV in Vietnam).

## Where it runs (8 countries, Argentina dominant)

Counterfeit-currency-as-change concentrates in countries with high
inflation, large-denomination notes that are easy to fake, and
informal currency-exchange economies. Argentina, Morocco, and Egypt
account for roughly two-thirds of documented variants in the 2026
archive. Vietnam, Mexico, Cambodia, Tunisia, and Algeria run
smaller-volume variants with the same architecture.

The Argentine *blue dollar* economics changed under the 2024–2025
Milei reforms. The historic upside that made street cambio worth
the risk no longer exists. The legal Western Union pickup rate
matches or exceeds the blue rate, with zero counterfeit risk. The
*arbolitos* on Calle Florida know this. They are still working the
strip on the assumption that arriving tourists do not.

## The five red flags

Two or more at the moment of payment or at the cambio handoff: stop
the transaction.

- **A street rate offered above the bank's posted rate.** The
  posted rate at any major Buenos Aires bank is the floor. Anything
  above is bait or comes with embedded counterfeits.
- **A back-office *cueva* or upstairs handoff for a street-quoted
  exchange.** Legitimate exchanges happen at the counter visible to
  the street.
- **A fast fan-count of bills.** Real change-makers count slowly,
  audibly, with each denomination called out.
- **A taxi driver who turns half-away to "break" the note.** The
  swap happens in the half-turn.
- **A standalone ATM in a pharmacy, mini-market, hotel lobby, or
  gift shop.** Major-bank ATMs are at major-bank branches.

## The phrases that shut it down

Memorize one phrase per country. Refusal works because it signals
you know the play.

| Language | Phrase | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| Spanish (Argentina) | *Más despacio, por favor. Voy a contar.* | Slower please. I'll count. |
| French (Morocco) | *Plus lentement, je vais compter.* | More slowly, I'll count. |
| Arabic (Egypt) | *Bishweesh, hatasab.* | Slowly, I'm counting. |
| Vietnamese | *Chậm thôi, tôi đếm.* | Slowly, I'm counting. |
| English | "Slowly, please. I'm going to count." |  |

If the cashier refuses to slow down or fans the count again, walk
out. The fan was the play.

## If this happens to you

You are at the hotel. Three bills do not pass the watermark check,
the cambio shorted you by 700 dirham, or the taxi driver palmed
your 1,000-peso note and you handed him a second one to keep the
peace.

The script is engineered to work on smart, traveled, well-prepared
people. If it weren't, the *arbolitos* would not be on Calle
Florida every afternoon.

Photograph every disputed note on a flat surface, showing the
serial number clearly. Photograph the receipt if you have one.
Photograph the cashier counter or the taxi license plate if you can
find it on Google Maps Street View. Note the time of the
transaction. Then call the local Tourist Police within twenty-four
hours. The report number is what your travel-insurance carrier and
your card issuer will need.

For credit-card disputes on cambio or taxi-related counterfeit
losses: open a dispute with your issuer the same day. Most major
card networks recognize the bill-swap pattern and reverse charges
when you submit a Tourist Police report number plus a photo of the
disputed bills. Amex's chargeback process is often the most responsive. Visa and
Mastercard outcomes vary; well-documented cases generally resolve
within thirty days. The window is short: file within sixty days of the
charge, ideally within seven.

For cash losses: bring three things to the Tourist Police report.
The disputed bills (do not destroy them). A photo of every serial
number. The receipt or transaction record (cambio printed slip,
taxi receipt, ATM withdrawal slip). Even when individual recovery
fails, the report puts the serial numbers into the city's
counterfeit register. The BCRA in Argentina, Bank Al-Maghrib in
Morocco, and the State Bank of Vietnam all track suspect serial-
number ranges, and reported counterfeits sometimes match clusters
under active investigation.

Priya filed at the Comisaría Turística on Avenida Corrientes the
next morning. The four notes are still in a Ziploc bag in her bank
records folder. She used Western Union for the rest of the trip.
