# The Seven Universal Patterns

Read this part slowly. The thirty scams in the rest of this book
differ in setting and currency. A gold ring on the Pont des Arts. A
camel ride at Giza. A "tourist office" in Paharganj. A felucca on
the Nile. A black taxi at CST station in Mumbai. A papyrus shop in
Khan el-Khalili. Different scams, different cities, different
decades. Underneath, they run on seven repeating patterns.

Memorize the seven and you will spot a brand-new scam the moment it
begins. The variations we describe in Part II are illustrative, not
exhaustive. Scammers iterate every season. The patterns do not.

The seven patterns also explain why the same scam runs in cities
that have no other connection. The bend on the Pont des Arts and
the bend on the Las Ramblas are not connected by people. They are
connected by the pattern. Two operators in two countries who have
never met, running the same play, because the play works.

## 1. The Captive-Position Lever

<!-- comic-insert -->
![Captive-Position Lever pattern icon](../build/images/icon-captive-position-lever.jpg){ width=40% }

You are on a moving auto-rickshaw in Hanoi. You are on a felucca in
the middle of the Nile. You are halfway up the elephant ramp at Amber
Fort. You are three blocks deep into the Khan el-Khalili behind a
"shortcut" guide. You are in the middle of a Cappadocia carpet shop's
back room with the front door closed. That is the moment the price
reveal lands.

Refusing the new price now is socially and physically harder than
refusing it at the curb. The boat will not turn around for ten more
minutes. The elephant will not kneel until you pay. The carpet shop
owner will not show you the door until the tea ceremony ends. You are
in a captive position, and the price has changed.

**The fingerprint:** the price was either never quoted, quoted
suspiciously low, or quoted in a way that produced an ambiguous "per
person versus per ride" trap. The reveal happens after you have given
up your exit option. The captive position is what makes the lever
work.

**The defense:** every transit decision, every shop entry, every
"I'll show you something" moment is made at the curb. Confirm the
total in writing if possible, before the wheels turn or the door
closes. If the price changes mid-trip, the practical response in
many low-risk overcharge situations is to pay the
originally agreed amount, walk to a busy area or a uniformed officer,
and refuse to engage further. You are not obligated to renegotiate
under duress.

> Pressure Sale (C), Drink Spiking & Bar Bill Trap (D), Express
> Kidnapping Taxi (E), Gem & Jewelry Shop Pressure (G), Henna Tattoo
> Ambush (H), Phone Snatch Motorcycle (P), Tea House Invitation (T).

## 2. The Authority Costume

<!-- comic-insert -->
![Authority Costume pattern icon](../build/images/icon-authority-costume.jpg){ width=40% }

A saffron robe at a temple gate in Bangkok. A laminated "ASI Guide"
badge at the entrance to a tomb in the Valley of the Kings. A navy
polo with a "Delhi Tourism" lanyard at the New Delhi Railway Station
platform. A vest at Marina Beach in Chennai demanding parking fees.
A high-vis safety vest at a Cairo airport curb. A cassock at a
Florentine church door asking for a donation. A police-style uniform
on the Khao San Road in Bangkok.

The costume signals authority. It says the person knows the system,
has the right to ask, and is here to help. Almost none of them do.

**The fingerprint:** the costume mismatches the official channel.
Real Indian Tourism staff are inside the government building at 88
Janpath, not on the platform at New Delhi Railway Station. Real
Egyptian guides are licensed by the Ministry of Tourism, hired in
advance, and never collect cash inside tombs. Real Bangkok police
do not stop tourists for impromptu drug searches in the middle of
the Khao San Road. Real Florentine clergy do not ask tourists for
cash donations at the door.

**The defense:** the costume is the warning, not the credential.
Verify the channel. The official ticket counter. The government
address. The hotel-booked guide. The metered taxi. The posted
opening hours. If a person in a costume is asking you to bypass
the channel, the costume is not real. The channel is the only thing
that proves authority.

> *Runs in this book:* Airport Arrival Scams (A), Bus and Train
> Station Scams (B), Fake Drug-Search Police Sting (F), Fake
> Government Tourist Office (F), Fake Tour Guide (F), Three Card
> Monte (T).
>
> *Also in the web atlas:* Deaf-Mute Petition, Fake Charity
> Petition, Fake Monk Donation, Fake Police Shakedown, Photo-Fee
> Shakedown, Tourist Tax Bogus Levy.

## 3. The Sub-Market Quote

<!-- comic-insert -->
![Sub-Market Quote pattern icon](../build/images/icon-sub-market-quote.jpg){ width=40% }

A 50 rupee auto from Mumbai's CST when the meter would be 150. A
500 baht "all-day Bangkok tour" when the regulated tour costs
1,500. A 100 dirham camel ride at Giza when the parking-lot
operators average 250. A 20 euro Vatican "skip-the-line" ticket
when the official line is free. A 30 dollar flat-rate JFK taxi
when the metered fare is 65.

The quote is too good to walk away from. That is the point.

**The fingerprint:** the sub-market quote is a function of the
captive position the operator is about to put you in (see
Pattern 1). The cheaper the quote, the more dramatic the inflation
will be once the trip is underway. A scammer offering you a 50
rupee CST ride knows you will pay 500 by the destination. The
50 was the bait, not the deal.

**The defense:** if the quote is meaningfully below market, it
is the bait. Pay the going rate at the official channel. Three
hundred extra rupees for a metered Ola from CST is the cheapest
insurance on this whole list. Twenty extra euros for a verified
Roman tour booked through your hotel is cheaper than the
"discount" papyrus shop run by your "free guide."

The corollary: the *too-good* signal is calibrated to your demographic.
A backpacker hears a 50 rupee quote as plausible because they have
been told India is cheap. A retiree hears a 30 dollar JFK quote as
plausible because the alternative is the 65 dollar meter. The trick
is the tailored unbelievability that becomes belief.

> *Runs in this book:* Airport Arrival Scams (A), ATM Currency
> Conversion Trap (A), Counterfeit Currency Returns (C), Currency
> Exchange Cambio (C), Drink Spiking & Bar Bill Trap (D), Express
> Kidnapping Taxi (E), Fake Skip-the-Line Tickets (F).
>
> *Also in the web atlas:* Free Walking Tour Tip Extortion, Taxi
> Meter Manipulation, Tuk-Tuk Rickshaw Detour, Unlicensed Airport
> Taxi Overcharge.

## 4. The Commission Detour

<!-- comic-insert -->
![Commission Detour pattern icon](../build/images/icon-commission-detour.jpg){ width=40% }

The auto driver mentions his "cousin's silk shop." The Hanoi taxi
driver knows a "wholesale" pho restaurant. The Cusco "guide" knows a
"local artisan" who weaves alpaca. The Marrakech "free tour" leader
knows a tannery that sells leather goods at a "fair price." The
Bangkok hotel concierge recommends a "trusted" jeweler at the
edge of Chinatown.

Every variant is the same. The journey from A to B routes through a
third location where someone earns 30 to 40 percent on whatever you
buy.

**The fingerprint:** the route deviates from your stated destination,
and the deviation is framed as helpful. The shop owner greets the
driver or guide by name. The prices have no tags. The chai or tea
or coffee is offered free.

**The defense:** state at the start of every ride and every walk:
"no shops, direct to destination only." Free chai is never free.
Browse independently in the named bazaars and buy serious items only
at fixed-price emporiums. The good news is that the legitimate
fixed-price markets exist almost everywhere there is a tourist
corridor. Cottage Industries Exposition in Delhi. Khan el-Khalili
silver fixed-price stalls in Cairo. The Souk Cherifia in Marrakech.
Find them before you accept a "free ride" that ends in someone
else's family business.

> *Runs in this book:* Carpet Shop Pressure Sale (C),
> Closed-Attraction Redirect (C), Fake Antique Souvenir Markup (F),
> Fake Tour Guide (F), Gem & Jewelry Shop Pressure (G), Tea House
> Invitation (T).
>
> *Also in the web atlas:* Free Walking Tour Tip Extortion,
> Limoncello Tasting Upsell, Massage & Spa Pressure, Timeshare
> Presentation Trap, Tourist Trap Restaurant, Tuk-Tuk Rickshaw
> Detour.

## 5. The Made-Up Closure

<!-- comic-insert -->
![Made-Up Closure pattern icon](../build/images/icon-made-up-closure.jpg){ width=40% }

The Red Fort in Delhi is "closed for a government holiday." Your
Paharganj guesthouse is "full tonight" because of a wedding.
Your CCU Kolkata airport hotel "had a fire last night." The Cairo
Egyptian Museum is "under maintenance, but I can take you to a
better one." The Mathura toll plaza requires a "mandatory rest
stop." The Charminar shop your auto driver knows is the *only* one
with the "real" silk because the others are "tourist traps."

**The fingerprint:** the inconvenient truth is delivered by the
person who is best positioned to "help" with the alternative. The
substitute is conveniently ready. You cannot verify the original
claim from where you are standing.

**The defense:** call the source directly. Your hotel from your
booking confirmation. The monument's official website for opening
hours. The airline app for flight status. The embassy hotline for
visa questions. Almost without exception, the original claim was
fabricated. The phone in your pocket is the easiest possible
verification, and the scammer is counting on you not making the
call.

If you are in a country where data roaming is expensive and you
have not bought a local SIM, the Made-Up Closure pattern is at its
strongest. Buy the SIM at the airport. Twenty dollars buys you the
ability to verify in real time. The book pays for itself there.

> *Runs in this book:* Airbnb Off-Platform Fraud (A),
> Closed-Attraction Redirect (C), Fake Booking Website (F), Fake
> Drug-Search Police Sting (F), Fake Government Tourist Office (F),
> Fake Tour Guide (F).
>
> *Also in the web atlas:* Fake Hotel Listing Fraud, Hotel Room
> Theft, Tourist Tax Bogus Levy, WhatsApp Fixer Deposit.

## 6. The Brand-Mimicry Storefront

<!-- comic-insert -->
![Brand-Mimicry Storefront pattern icon](../build/images/icon-brand-mimicry-storefront.jpg){ width=40% }

A "Government Tourist Information Office" near the Jaipur Railway
Station with maps on the wall and a "Ministry of Tourism" logo
that has one letter different from the real one. A "Bawarchi"
restaurant in Hyderabad with two i's instead of one. A "Karma Royal"
scratch card promotion in Bali. A *ticketstajmahal.com* site that
ranks above asi.nic.in on a Google search for Taj Mahal tickets.
A "Tripadvisor Recommended" sticker on a Bangkok restaurant window
where Tripadvisor never recommended anything. A "Skip-the-Line
Vatican" booth one block from the actual Vatican entrance, run by
nobody affiliated with the Vatican.

**The fingerprint:** the brand name is one letter, one syllable,
one suffix off the real one. The storefront, the website, the
lanyard, all polished enough to fool a glance.

**The defense:** Google the exact spelling, every time. The real
RTDC office (Rajasthan Tourism Development Corporation) is inside
or directly adjacent to the railway station, not in a shop nearby.
The real Bawarchi is at Lalita Bagh in Begumpet. The real ASI
ticket-booking site is at asi.nic.in or asi.payumoney.com, and
nowhere else. The real Tripadvisor recommendation is on
Tripadvisor, not on a sticker that the restaurant printed itself.

The strongest version of this pattern is the brand-mimicry
website. A scammer registers *bookings.com* (the s does the work),
*official-vatican-tickets.com*, *taj-mahal-tickets-india.com*. The
domain is real. The bookings the site takes are not. Always
navigate to the official site by typing the brand directly into
the address bar, not by clicking the top Google result, which
might be a paid ad from the scammer.

> *Runs in this book:* Airbnb Off-Platform Fraud (A), ATM Skimming
> (A), Cover Charge (Coperto / Otoshi / Couvert) (C), Counterfeit
> Currency Returns (C), Fake Antique Souvenir Markup (F), Fake
> Booking Website (F), Fake Government Tourist Office (F), Fake
> Skip-the-Line Tickets (F), QR Code Quishing (Q), Restaurant Bill
> Padding (R).
>
> *Also in the web atlas:* Fake Hotel Listing Fraud, Tourist Trap
> Restaurant, WhatsApp Fixer Deposit.

## 7. Manufactured Reciprocity

<!-- comic-insert -->
![Manufactured Reciprocity pattern icon](../build/images/icon-manufactured-reciprocity.jpg){ width=40% }

A bracelet tied to your wrist before you can refuse. A henna
pattern applied to your hand while you said no. A "lost" gold ring
offered as a gift on the Pont des Arts. A free walking tour that
ends with social pressure for a 50-euro tip. A rose dropped on your
restaurant table while you eat. A street poet who recites a verse
"for the lady" then asks for payment. A free tea poured in a Tangier
carpet shop before any negotiation has started.

The mechanic that connects them all is the same. A thing arrives in
your possession, or a service is rendered into your social space,
before you have agreed to pay for it. The price arrives the moment
you cannot easily refuse, because refusing now requires giving the
item back, returning the favor, or breaking the social contract
that the scammer has just imposed.

**The fingerprint:** the transfer is unsolicited and physical, or
the service is rendered before any negotiation. The "gift" lands.
The "favor" is given. The price arrives after the transfer, when
refusing it feels ruder than paying.

**The defense:** keep your hands closed and your wrists in your
pockets in tourist plazas. Decline offers before any item moves
and before any service starts. When a stranger initiates physical
contact (a bracelet, a henna syringe, a rose pressed into your
hand), step back without breaking stride. When a stranger offers a
"free" anything (tea in a carpet shop, a song, a tour, a poem),
the price has already been calculated. Set your refusal before any
service is rendered.

The pattern is the social technology of debt. Most cultures have
norms against accepting and not reciprocating, and the scammer
weaponizes the norm. The defense is recognizing that no genuine
exchange of generosity opens with a stranger in a tourist plaza.

> *Runs in this book:* Aggressive Street Vendor (A), Carpet Shop
> Pressure Sale (C), Distraction-Theft Pickpocket Team (D),
> Friendship Bracelet Trap (F), Gem & Jewelry Shop Pressure (G),
> The Gold Ring Trick (G), Henna Tattoo Ambush (H), Tea House
> Invitation (T).
>
> *Also in the web atlas:* Free Walking Tour Tip Extortion.

## Spend ten minutes here

Before you read any chapter in Part II, sit with the seven patterns
above for ten minutes. They will outlast every individual scam in
this book. New variations appear every season. They are always one
of these seven, in a new costume.

If you remember nothing else from this book, remember that. The
costume is what changes. The pattern is what stays.
