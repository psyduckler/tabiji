# Appendix I: Sources and methodology

This appendix explains how the thirty scams in this book were
selected, verified, and updated. It is for the reader who wants to
know what makes a scam "real" in our taxonomy and what makes a
candidate scam fail to meet the bar. It is also the appendix the
press, the embassies, and the academic researchers who cite this
book are most likely to read first.

## The three-confirmation rule

A scam appears in this book if and only if we have three independent
confirmations of it running in the field. The three categories are:

1. **A published news report or official advisory.** A national
   newspaper, a regional paper of record, an embassy travel advisory,
   a tourism-board warning, or a regulatory authority's public
   notice. We weight this category most heavily because it carries
   editorial review and accountability.

2. **A police or tourism-authority record.** A specific arrest, a
   complaint filing, an investigation, a regulatory action, a
   tourist-police bulletin. The record does not need to be public; it
   does need to be documentable to a named officer or unit. We
   typically obtain these through freedom-of-information requests in
   countries that allow them, through cooperative tourist-police
   relationships in countries we have established contacts in, or
   through published court documents.

3. **At least three firsthand traveler accounts** filed in the same
   calendar year as our research. The accounts must come from
   independent sources: not three posts on the same Reddit thread,
   not three replies to one Tripadvisor review. They can come from
   Reddit (r/travel, r/solotravel, country- and city-specific
   subreddits), Tripadvisor, Lonely Planet's Thorn Tree forum,
   travel-blog comments, or direct submissions to editorial@tabiji.ai.

A candidate scam needs at least one source from each of the three
categories before it enters the editorial pipeline. Most candidate
scams that get this far make the book. About one in five do not, and
the next section describes why.

## Why some candidate scams do not make the book

The most common reasons a candidate fails:

**The scam is too local.** A scam that runs only in Marrakech and
only on Tuesdays does not meet our cross-country bar. It might
appear in the Morocco volume of the Travel Safety Series, where the
local detail is the value, but it does not appear in this atlas. The
atlas threshold is two countries minimum.

**The mechanic is not really a scam.** The "rude shop owner" complaint
that turns out to be a normal cultural haggling style. The "bad
service" complaint at a high-volume tourist restaurant. The "wrong
change" complaint that turns out to be the standard currency-exchange
spread. We recategorize these as Scams That Aren't (Chapter 4) rather
than as scams.

**The pattern is unstable.** A scam that ran reliably in 2022 but
appears to have stopped running in 2024 and 2025. We track these as
"deprecated" entries; they may return, but they do not appear in the
current edition until they re-emerge with current evidence.

**The single-source problem.** A spectacular story from one news
outlet that no other source has confirmed. We hold these for the next
edition and re-check whether independent sources have surfaced. About
half do. Half do not, and those drop.

**The scam is a category we explicitly do not cover.** Refer to
Appendix G for the scope clarifier. Visa fraud, romance scams,
investment fraud, business-trip fraud are all real categories of
travel-adjacent harm, but they are not tourist scams in our taxonomy.

## Three categories of candidate that get held back

For concreteness, three categories of candidate scam that fail our
bar in any given year.

The first is the **single-city scam**. A scam that runs reliably in
one specific neighborhood of one city, well-documented locally, but
without a second-country presence. These belong in the destination
volume of the country they concentrate in (where the local detail
is the value), not in this atlas. Every annual research cycle
produces several of these. The atlas threshold is two countries
minimum, and we hold the line.

The second is the **single-source scam**. A spectacular story from
one news outlet that no other publication has confirmed and no
police record has independently corroborated. These often look real
on first read. The published account is detailed, the dollar
amounts are specific, the city is named. But without a second
independent source we cannot verify whether the script ran more
than once or whether the published account was itself unreliable.
We hold these for the next research cycle and re-check whether
independent reports have surfaced. Roughly half pick up the second
source within twelve months. The other half drop quietly.

The third is the **off-taxonomy candidate**. A travel-adjacent
fraud whose victim was a tourist, but whose mechanics belong to a
different reference. Medical-tourism fraud, immigration-fee fraud,
romance fraud that began on a dating app during a trip,
investment-pitch fraud at a resort. These are real categories of
harm and we forward the reports we receive to the relevant
consumer-protection or consular authority. They do not appear here
because the recovery paths, evidence trails, and defensive moves
for each one are different from those for tourist scams. Refer to
Appendix G for the full scope clarifier.

Three rejection categories, three different reasons. The book
contains the candidates that pass the bar. The ones that do not are
either real but unproven, or proven but out of scope.

## Why scams cluster geographically

A question we get from readers and from press: why does the gold
ring trick run in Paris and not in Tokyo? Why do express
kidnappings concentrate in a handful of Latin American capitals?
Why are camel-handler shakedowns specific to Giza and not Petra?

Three forces shape the geography.

**Tourist density and pedestrian rhythm.** A scam needs a captive
target population that is moving slowly enough for the operator to
intercept. Paris has the Pont des Arts and Notre-Dame; New York has
Times Square; Bangkok has the Khao San Road. Cities with dispersed
tourist flow (most of Tokyo, much of Zurich) do not produce the
density needed for street-level scams to scale. Scams concentrate
where pedestrians do.

**Local economy and policing tolerance.** A scam needs an operator
willing to run it and a police force willing to look away (or
unable to keep up). The gold ring runs in Paris partly because the
local market for low-effort street operations is robust and partly
because Pont des Arts policing is reactive, not proactive. The same
script runs in Berlin too rarely to bother documenting because
German pedestrian-zone enforcement is faster and more punitive.
Scams concentrate where enforcement is slack.

**Cultural reciprocity norms.** The Manufactured Reciprocity
pattern (the gold ring, the friendship bracelet, the henna tattoo)
runs hardest in cultures where strangers giving gifts to strangers
is socially plausible. It runs poorly in cultures with strong
contract-norms and weak reciprocity-norms (most of Northern Europe,
much of East Asia). The Authority Costume runs hardest in countries
where the relationship between official authority and informal
deference is loose. The Captive-Position Lever runs everywhere
because boats and rickshaws are universal. The geography of each
pattern is the geography of the social technology it exploits.

These three forces compound. A city with high tourist density,
slack pedestrian-zone enforcement, and a culture of stranger-
hospitality (Marrakech, Cairo, Cusco) produces a different scam
landscape than a city with high density, tight enforcement, and
contract-norm dominance (Singapore, Tokyo, Copenhagen). The
chapter weightings in our country books reflect that asymmetry,
which is part of why a destination volume's page count is not a
proxy for that country's beauty or worth visiting. It is a proxy
for how much defensive material a traveler benefits from before
arriving.

## How sources are weighted

Within each of the three categories, sources are weighted by
reliability. A national newspaper outranks a city tabloid. A
tourism-board advisory outranks a travel-blogger's warning. A police
arrest record outranks a "police told me" anecdote.

Where sources conflict, we report the conflict. If the New York
Times reports a scam costs an average of two hundred dollars and
the Bangkok Post reports the same scam costs an average of fifty
dollars, both numbers appear in the chapter, with attribution to
the source.

We do not weight by recency above a threshold. Ten reports across
five years and five reports in the last six months both meet the
bar, because tourist scams iterate slowly. The script changes; the
mechanic does not.

## How currency amounts are reported

When a chapter reports that a specific scam charges, say, "fifty
to two hundred euros," the range reflects the actual amounts charged
in documented incidents in the calendar year of the edition. The low
end is the smallest documented charge; the high end is the largest
documented charge that is not a clear outlier. Outliers (a single
charge ten times the typical range) are flagged in the source notes
but excluded from the headline range.

Local currency is the primary unit. We add a USD equivalent when the
math materially affects the reader's understanding. The USD
equivalent reflects the exchange rate in the month of the most
recent edition revision, which is in the back-cover edition flap
and the colophon. Future editions update.

## How we keep sources current

The book is on an annual revision cycle. Each year between January
and April, the editorial team:

1. Re-pulls every named news source from the previous year. Articles
   that are still live remain. Articles that have been deleted or
   hidden behind paywalls migrate to archived URLs at archive.org.

2. Re-checks every named police or tourism-authority contact. Phone
   numbers that have changed update. Hotline names that have
   re-branded re-name.

3. Re-pulls every Reddit, Tripadvisor, and forum thread that
   contributed. Threads that have been deleted are marked. New
   threads from the past year are reviewed for additions.

4. Audits the geographic spread of every scam. A scam that has lost
   coverage in two of its three documented countries gets de-listed
   or moved to "deprecated" status. A scam that has gained coverage
   in two new countries gets a geographic-update note in the chapter.

5. Audits the currency ranges. Inflation and exchange-rate movement
   typically nudge ranges five to ten percent year over year. Larger
   shifts trigger a chapter rewrite.

The annual cycle is what makes the 2026 edition different from what
the 2027 edition will be. It is also what justifies the annual-edition
pricing structure: each edition is a new book, not a re-cover of
the previous one.

## Named victims, named scammers

We change the names of victims unless they are publicly named in a
court document, a news report they themselves spoke to, or in their
own published account. Margie, Priya, Harry, and Marcus are
composite figures drawn from patterns across hundreds of victim
reports.

We do not change the names of scammers when they are arrested,
charged, or named in a court record. We do not name scammers from
unverified victim reports because of the risk of misidentification.
The fingerprint of the scam is the operating script, not the
operator.

## What a "tourist" is in our taxonomy

We define a tourist as anyone visiting a place for fewer than ninety
consecutive days for purposes other than work, study, or family.
Day-trippers from neighboring cities count. Short-term cruise-ship
passengers count. Layover travelers in transit through an airport
count. Long-term visitors on visas (digital nomads on six-month
visas, retirees on five-year residency permits) do not count for the
purposes of this book, although the chapters often apply unchanged.

The reason for the boundary is that tourist scams optimize for
specific behaviors: the cognitive load of arriving in a new place,
the lack of familiarity with local prices, the willingness to make
quick decisions because the trip is short. Long-term visitors lose
those vulnerabilities within the first month. The scams move on.

## How to challenge our entries

If you have evidence that a chapter in this book is wrong, write to
us at editorial@tabiji.ai. We try to read every email and we re-open
disputed entries for the next edition's research cycle.

The most common challenge is the demographic claim. A chapter that
identifies a scam as "concentrated on solo female travelers" gets
challenged by male readers who have experienced the same scam, and
vice versa. We re-examine the sourced incidents and update the
demographic notes if the evidence has shifted.

The second most common challenge is the geographic claim. A reader
reports the gold-ring trick in a city we did not document. We add
the city to the next edition if the report meets the three-source
rule above; otherwise we hold it for accumulation.

The third most common challenge is the currency range. Fairly often,
a reader reports a scam at a substantially higher or lower amount
than our range. We update the range if the new evidence is
documented (a receipt, a police report, a screenshot), or annotate
the range if the new evidence is anecdotal but credible.

## Why we do this work

Tourist-scam information has historically been scattered: travel
forums, a couple of bestselling guidebooks, and embassy advisories
that get updated rarely and read less. The information that does
exist tends to be specific to one country or written for a different
purpose (legal compliance, journalistic exposé, listicle SEO).

We saw a gap. The information was real. It needed to be collected,
verified, and presented in a single reference that travelers could
actually use. The atlas is the result. The country books are the
result. The website is the result.

We do this work because the readers who write back tell us it
works. They report fewer incidents than they used to. They report
faster recoveries when something does happen. We do not run a
randomized control trial on traveler outcomes, so we cannot give you
a number that is anything other than anecdotal. We can tell you that
the volume and tone of reader feedback has shifted from "I wish I'd
known" to "your book caught it before I did," which is the change we
were aiming for.

If this book has helped you, the most useful thing you can do for
the next reader is to write us with your story. Every report makes
the next edition sharper.
