# Appendix F: Glossary

Words used throughout this book that may be unfamiliar to readers
new to international travel. Each entry is short. Each is intended
to make a chapter clearer, not to teach the language.

If a word in a chapter does not appear here, the chapter glosses
it inline on first use.

---

**ASI.** Archaeological Survey of India. The government body that
manages most major Indian heritage sites including the Taj Mahal, the
Red Fort, Hampi, and Ajanta. ASI tickets are sold inside the gate at
official counters or online at asi.payumoney.com. Anyone selling
"ASI tickets" outside the gate is not ASI.

**Baksheesh (Egyptian Arabic and Persian).** A general term for a tip,
gratuity, or small payment. Baksheesh is a normal part of Egyptian
service economies. Demanding baksheesh inside a paid attraction (a
"guide" inside a tomb, a "helper" at a paid ticket counter) is not
normal and is the scam variant.

**Bouquinistes (French).** The riverside booksellers along the
Seine in Paris. Used as scenery in *The Gold Ring Trick*.

**Cambio (Spanish).** Currency exchange. The cambio counter or cambio
shop. The Florida Avenue cambio hustle and the Buenos Aires cambio
counterfeit-peso swap both run inside cambio storefronts that look
official from the street.

**Carabinieri (Italian).** Italy's national gendarmerie, distinct
from the Polizia di Stato. Either force can take a tourist's report,
but the Carabinieri have a stronger presence in tourist zones in
historic cities.

**Carat versus karat.** Carat is a unit of mass for gemstones. Karat
is the purity unit for gold (24 karat is pure gold; 14 karat is
roughly 58 percent gold). British English uses "carat" for both. We
use "karat" for gold throughout this book.

**Coperto (Italian).** A "cover charge" added per person at Italian
restaurants for the bread, the table service, and the privilege of
sitting. A real coperto is one to three euros, posted on the menu, and
is normal in Italy. An undisclosed twelve-euro coperto is the scam
variant covered in *Cover Charge (Coperto / Otoshi / Couvert)*.

**Cyrillic.** The alphabet used by Russian, Bulgarian, Macedonian,
and several other languages. Streets, signs, and storefronts in
Cyrillic-script countries can be hard to read for tourists who do not
recognize the letters. The Russian phrase appendix in Appendix A gives
both Cyrillic and Latin transliteration.

**Dargah (Arabic, used in South Asia).** A Sufi shrine. The Sheikh
Salim Chishti dargah at Fatehpur Sikri is the setting for the chadar
ceremony scam variant in *Fake Tour Guide*.

**Devanagari.** The script used to write Hindi, Sanskrit, and
several other Indian languages. Devanagari versions of phrases appear
in the Hindi entry of Appendix A for screenshot use.

**Espresso versus filter coffee.** In most of continental Europe,
"coffee" without qualification means espresso. A traveler who orders
"a coffee" and is surprised by the small cup is not being scammed.

**FIR (Indian English).** First Information Report. The document
filed at an Indian police station to report a crime. A scam victim's
FIR is the document that unlocks travel-insurance claims and the
embassy's replacement-passport process.

**Galabeya / galabiya (Egyptian Arabic).** The traditional long
robe worn by Egyptian men, especially in rural areas and in
service-industry roles in tourist zones. The galabeya appears in *Fake Drug-Search Police Sting* and
*Fake Tour Guide* in their Egyptian variants.

**Hanzi.** Chinese characters. Mandarin uses simplified Hanzi
(mainland China and Singapore) or traditional Hanzi (Taiwan and
Hong Kong). The Mandarin phrases in Appendix A are presented in
simplified Hanzi for mainland-China use.

**Henna.** A plant-based dye applied as a temporary tattoo,
typically on the hands and feet. Real henna fades over one to two
weeks. The "black henna" variant used in some tourist-zone ambushes
contains hair-dye chemicals and can cause severe skin reactions;
this is one reason *Henna Tattoo Ambush* ranks the henna ambush as more
dangerous than its small dollar amount suggests.

**IRCTC.** Indian Railways Catering and Tourism Corporation. The
official authority that operates Indian Railways. The only legitimate
ticket-booking site is irctc.co.in. Any other "Indian Railways" site
is the brand-mimicry pattern.

**Kazakhstani tenge versus tengo (linguistic confusion).** The
Kazakhstani tenge is currency. *Tengo* is "I have" in Spanish. The
two have caused traveler confusion in trip-planning conversations
between Latin American and Central Asian travelers and have nothing
to do with each other.

**Khan el-Khalili.** The historic souk in Cairo, located in the
Islamic Quarter. Khan el-Khalili is a frequent setting in Egypt-
related chapters (the papyrus "school" markup, the silver
fixed-price stall variant, the Bedouin tea ceremony upsell).

**Koban (Japanese).** Small police box, found on most Japanese
neighborhood corners. There is no direct equivalent in most of the
world. Walking to a koban is the de-escalation move for almost any
Japanese-tourist incident. Koban officers in tourist zones often
speak some English.

**KOMBI.** A shared minibus, especially in Eastern Europe and parts
of Africa. Sometimes used for cheap transit. Sometimes used as a
brand-mimicry of an official bus by an unlicensed operator. Verify
that the kombi has a posted route and printed fare.

**Lassi versus bhang lassi.** Lassi is a yogurt-based Indian drink.
Bhang lassi contains cannabis. The catastrophic variant of the
drink-spiking scam in *Drink Spiking & Bar Bill Trap* involves bhang lassi served to
travelers who did not order it; the cognitive impairment that
follows is the entry point to the bar bill trap.

**Pesce all'etto (Italian).** Fish priced "per hundred grams" rather
than "per fish." A coastal Italian seafood specialty that is also
the fingerprint of a specific bill-padding mechanic covered in
*Restaurant Bill Padding*.

**Plainte (French).** A formal police report filed at a
commissariat. A plainte produces a numbered document that is the
basis for travel-insurance claims and embassy assistance after a
scam in France.

**RTDC.** Rajasthan Tourism Development Corporation. The actual
state-tourism authority for Rajasthan. The "RTDC office" near
Jaipur Railway Station that turns out to be a fake commission shop
is the brand-mimicry variant covered in *Fake Government Tourist Office*.

**Romaji.** Phonetic Latin transliteration of Japanese. Used in
Appendix A so English-speaking readers can pronounce Japanese
phrases without learning hiragana or katakana.

**SATE.** Servicio de Atención al Turista Extranjero (Spanish).
The Spanish national tourist police, a unit of the Policía
Nacional. SATE has English-speaking officers in major tourist
cities including Madrid, Barcelona, Seville, and Granada.

**Skimmer.** A device attached to an ATM card slot or point-of-
sale terminal that captures card details. Skimmers are most common
on tourist-zone ATMs and on parking-meter card readers in some
cities. Bank-lobby ATMs are skimmed at much lower rates than
freestanding ATMs in tourist plazas.

**Souk (Arabic).** A traditional market, usually covered, with
stalls selling spices, textiles, metalwork, and food. Souks exist
across the Arabic-speaking world (Marrakech's Souk Cherifia, Cairo's
Khan el-Khalili, Damascus's Souk al-Hamidiyah). Most souks are
beautiful, useful, and almost entirely free of scams. The pressure-
sale variant runs in carpet shops at the perimeter of the souk
proper, not in the souk's main pedestrian lanes.

**Tola (Indian, gold weight).** A traditional Indian unit of mass,
roughly 11.66 grams. Sometimes used by gem-shop scammers who quote
prices in grams and tolas interchangeably to confuse the math.

**Tripadvisor "recommended."** Tripadvisor recommendations are
visible on the Tripadvisor website. Window stickers reading
"Tripadvisor Recommended" are not produced by Tripadvisor and have
no relationship to the Tripadvisor algorithm. The sticker is a
brand-mimicry variant.

**Tuk-tuk and rickshaw.** Three-wheeled motorized passenger
vehicles. Found across South and Southeast Asia and parts of
Africa. Some places (Bangkok, Delhi, Phnom Penh) operate tuk-tuks
as a normal part of the urban transit fabric; others are for
tourists only. Always confirm price at the curb before you get in.

**Yen, won, baht, dong, rupee, peso, lira, dirham, koruna.**
Currencies of, respectively: Japan, South Korea, Thailand, Vietnam,
India and Pakistan and parts of South Asia, the Spanish-speaking
world and the Philippines, Turkey, the Arabic-speaking world and
parts of North Africa, the Czech Republic. The chapters use the
local currency for amounts. Where the math matters, the chapter
also gives an approximate USD equivalent.

**ZTL (Italian).** Zona a Traffico Limitato. A restricted-traffic
zone in many Italian historic city centers. Driving into a ZTL
without authorization triggers a fine that arrives weeks or months
later from the rental-car company. The ZTL fine cascade is the
specific scam variant covered in the web atlas at
tabiji.ai/scams/atlas; it is not in this book's thirty chapters.
