# C · Cover Charge (Coperto / Otoshi / Couvert)

> **Pattern: Brand-Mimicry Storefront** · 5 named variants · 4 countries · 5–50 USD per meal · *Updated April 2026*

## The line at the bottom

Margie and her sister sat at a small table on Vicolo del Cinque in
Trastevere on a Thursday evening, the second-to-last night of a
Rome-and-Florence week.

The waiter was polite. He brought the menu, listed the specials in
fast Italian-accented English, and waited the right amount of time
for them to choose. Margie ordered the *cacio e pepe*. Her sister
ordered the *amatriciana*. They split a half-liter of house red and
a bottle of water.

The bill arrived forty-five minutes later. Total: 84 euros. They
had expected about fifty.

Margie put on her reading glasses.

The pasta was 14 euros each on the bill. The menu had listed twelve.
The wine was 18 euros on the bill. The menu had listed fourteen.
And there, at the bottom of the bill, a line that had not appeared
on the menu in any size of type:

*COPERTO 12,00 (×2 = 24).*

Twelve euros per person for the *coperto* (the per-person cover
charge for table linen, silverware, and bread).

Margie pulled the menu back over. The *coperto* was disclosed in
the footer in small print. Two euros per person. Not twelve. The
pasta and wine prices on the menu matched the original quotes:
twelve and fourteen.

The bill was inflated by thirty euros off-menu.

## The trick

The cover-charge surprise works because the line at the bottom of
the bill arrives after you have eaten. Standing up to leave without
paying is socially awkward. Disputing it requires reading the menu
again in front of the waiter. Most travelers pay rather than
escalate over what feels like a small line item.

The pattern is Brand-Mimicry Storefront. The inflated *coperto*
looks like a normal *coperto*; the inflated version hides under
the same name and the same expectation of "this is what they do
here."

> **Normal:** A *coperto* of 1.50 to 3.50 euros, listed on the
> menu (often in the footer), charged to every table for table
> linen, silverware, and bread.
>
> **Scam:** A *coperto* of 5 to 12 euros, missing from the menu
> entirely or present in microprint, applied only when the bill
> arrives.

The defense is three rules, applied in order.

The on-menu rule: photograph the menu before ordering. The printed
prices are the binding contract. Italian, French, German, and
Japanese consumer law all recognize the menu price as the maximum
chargeable.

The pre-order question: ask before ordering, in the local language,
*c'è un coperto?* (is there a cover charge?) The server's answer is
the binding price. They cannot legally raise it after the order is
placed.

The bill audit: before paying, compare the bill to the menu line by
line. Sixty seconds of audit catches ninety-five percent of off-
menu inflations.

## The five mechanics

**Italian *coperto*.** Per-person charge for table linen, silverware,
and bread, traditionally 1.50 to 3.50 euros. Tourist-trap trattorie
in Rome (Trastevere, Vatican area, Trevi), Florence (Duomo, Ponte
Vecchio), Venice (San Marco, Rialto), and Naples (Spaccanapoli)
inflate to 5–12 euros per person. The Lazio region (containing
Rome) banned the *coperto* entirely in 2006. Many restaurants still
charge it. The inflated version is twice illegal: the regional ban
plus consumer-fraud statutes against off-menu pricing.

**Japanese *otoshi* / *tsukidashi*.** Compulsory small appetizer
charge in *izakaya* (Japanese pubs), traditionally 300–700 yen per
person. The dish (pickled vegetables, bean sprouts, octopus salad)
arrives with the first drinks. Tourist-trap *izakaya* in Shinjuku
Kabukicho, Roppongi, and Shibuya inflate to 1,500–5,000 yen per
person without disclosure. Defense: ask *otoshi wa arimasu ka?* at
the door, and check for English signage.

**French *couvert*.** Per-person service charge, largely abolished
by the *service compris* reform (the 15 percent service charge
included in the menu price by default). Tourist-trap bistros in the
Quartier Latin, Saint-Germain, and the Champs-Élysées area
occasionally add a 2–5 euro *couvert* without menu disclosure. The
Code de la Consommation Article L. 121-7 prohibits this. Defense:
verify *service compris* on the menu and refuse any added line.

**German / Austrian *Gedeck*.** Bread-basket fee in Bavarian and
Austrian tourist trattoria-style venues, 2–5 euros per person
legitimately. Tourist inflation to 8–15 euros in Munich
Marienplatz, Berlin Hackescher Markt, Vienna Stephansplatz,
Salzburg Old Town. German consumer law (BGB § 307) requires
*Gedeck* disclosure on the menu.

**Iberian / Greek pre-set platter.** Bread, olives, tuna pâté,
butter, dips placed on the table without asking. Spanish *cubierto*
(Madrid, Barcelona tourist), Portuguese *couvert* (Lisbon Bairro
Alto, Porto Ribeira), Greek mezédes fee (Plaka, Mykonos, Santorini),
Croatian *kuvert* (Dubrovnik, Split). Charges 3–10 euros per
platter. Returning the platter untouched, before any item is
consumed, removes the charge.

## Where it runs (4 countries, Italy dominant)

Cover-charge surprises concentrate in tourist-density restaurant
districts where the variant's small per-meal capture (5–50 USD)
compounds across high turnover. Italy runs the highest volume,
driven by Roman Trastevere and Florentine *centro storico*. Japan
runs the highest closing prices, driven by Kabukicho *otoshi*
inflation. France, Germany, and the Iberian-Greek pre-set-platter
zone round out the geography.

You will not see this in countries with explicit no-cover-charge
norms (the United States, the United Kingdom, most of Northern
Europe). The variant travels with the cultural acceptance of small
table-service fees.

## The five red flags

Two or more at the table: ask for the menu back and re-read it
before any food arrives.

- **A bread basket placed on the table without your ordering
  it.** This is the pre-set-platter variant about to land. Decide
  before any item is touched whether you want it or want it
  returned.
- **A waiter who lists the specials in fast English without prices
  attached.** Specials with no quoted prices are where off-menu
  inflation begins.
- **A *coperto* line in the menu footer in barely-readable type.**
  Reputable disclosure is at standard menu type size. Microprint
  disclosure is operating the variant.
- **An *izakaya* with no English signage at the door.** Real
  *izakaya* with normal *otoshi* publish the *otoshi* charge at the
  door for tourists who do not read Japanese.
- **A bill that itemizes both *service compris* and *couvert*.** In
  France, this is two charges for the same thing. The *couvert*
  line is the variant.

## The phrases that shut it down

Asked before ordering, in the local language. The server's answer
is the binding price.

| Language | Phrase | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| Italian | *C'è un coperto? Quanto costa?* | Is there a cover charge? How much? |
| Japanese | *Otoshi wa arimasu ka? Ikura desu ka?* | Is there an otoshi? How much? |
| French | *Y a-t-il un couvert? Le service est-il compris?* | Is there a couvert? Is service included? |
| German | *Gibt es ein Gedeck? Wie viel?* | Is there a Gedeck? How much? |
| Spanish / Portuguese / Greek | *No, gracias, llevar.* / *Não obrigado, levar de volta.* / *Óchi efcharistó.* | (return the platter, untouched) |

For the pre-set-platter variant: as soon as the waiter places it,
decide. If you do not want it, return it untouched immediately.
Once any item is consumed (even one olive), the charge stands.

## If this happens to you

The bill arrived with an inflated or undisclosed cover charge.

If you are still at the table, ask politely for the manager and
present the menu side-by-side with the bill. Most reputable
restaurants will correct the bill on request. Restaurants running
the variant will refuse and cite "tradition" or "what we do here."
That refusal
is the diagnostic.

Pay by credit card if possible. Avoid cash for any disputed bill.
Photograph the menu (footer, small print, dish prices), the bill
(every line), and the cover-charge line specifically. Note the time
and the restaurant address.

File a credit-card chargeback within thirty days under "billed
amount differs from agreed amount" or "item not as described." Visa,
Mastercard, and Amex all accept this category for restaurant over-
billing. Recovery rates are high when the documentation is complete:
photographs of the menu, the bill, and the disputed line.

For Italian restaurants violating the Lazio *coperto* ban or
charging an inflated *coperto*: file a parallel complaint with the
Carabinieri (112) at the time, or with the Italian consumer
protection ministry afterward. The Lazio ban makes the inflated
*coperto* unenforceable on demand and gives the chargeback strong
grounds.

For French restaurants violating the Code de la Consommation: file
with DGCCRF at signal.conso.gouv.fr.

For German violations: file with Verbraucherzentrale Bayern, Berlin,
or the relevant state office.

For Japanese *otoshi* inflation: file with the Tokyo Metropolitan
Government Bureau of Industrial and Labor Affairs, consumer
protection division.

Margie and her sister paid the corrected fifty-six euro bill on
Margie's Visa card and disputed the original eighty-four. The
chargeback came back at twenty-eight euros credited within
twenty-three days. The dispute did not save the evening but it did
pay for breakfast.
