### The Five Rules (~900 words)
Same five rules from dental, with surgical examples and stakes-appropriate weight:

1. **Do not buy a transformation before you understand the diagnosis.**
   - Surgical example: a "$4,500 nose job" is not a diagnosis; "rhinoplasty to address a dorsal hump while preserving tip projection given your existing alar cartilage anatomy" is closer
   
2. **The cheapest quote is not the cheapest outcome.**
   - Surgical example: a $3,500 BBL package becomes substantially more expensive if it's done with intramuscular fat injection without ultrasound guidance and you become the next case in the South Florida or Dominican Republic mortality data

3. **You need a plan for aftercare before you need aftercare.**
   - Surgical example: revision rates 5–20% depending on procedure; complications can require hospitalization; post-op care is weeks not days for most procedures

4. **Pressure is information.**
   - Surgical example: same as dental, with the discount-deadline pattern that's even more aggressive in cosmetic ("book today, surgery in 4 weeks")

5. **Keep your exit options open.**
   - Surgical example: most cosmetic surgery is more irreversible than most dental work; once tissue is removed or implanted it can rarely be restored to baseline

### Read This First: The 20-Minute Safety Pause (~1,500 words)
Eight questions adapted from dental:

1. What diagnosis is the plan based on? (For BBL: was a CT or MRI of donor site fat distribution discussed? For breast augmentation: was the existing tissue/anatomy assessed in person?)
2. What records did the surgeon review? (Photos alone are inadequate for most surgical planning; in-person exam is non-negotiable for surgical decisions)
3. What parts of the plan are irreversible? (Most of it. Be specific.)
4. What is included in the quote and what is not? (Surgeon's fee, anesthesia, OR/facility fee, implants, garments, medications, follow-up visits, revision policy, complication policy)
5. What happens if something goes wrong after you return home? (Specifically: hospitalization coverage, revision-surgery policy, who manages remote follow-up)
6. How many trips and how much time are realistically required? (Most cosmetic surgery: minimum 7-day stay; some procedures: longer)
7. What is the clinic asking you to do before you have full information? (Deposit, flights, nonrefundable hotels, social media consents, "demonstration case" agreements)
8. Have you received at least one independent opinion? (Includes mental health screening for cosmetic-specific patients — a self-administered BDD screen at minimum)

### Decision Gate (~250 words)
Pause if:
- You only know the procedure name, not the procedure plan
- The surgeon has not examined you in person and the clinic considers photos sufficient
- You're being asked to combine multiple procedures in one operation without explicit written discussion of the combined risk
- The "all-inclusive package" price doesn't itemize the surgeon's fee separately
- You feel rushed by the clinic AND by your own family
- You cannot identify the specific named anesthesia provider and their credentials
- The surgeon will not name the implant manufacturer for any implant device
- You've answered yes to 3+ items on the BDD self-screen and have not yet had a mental health consultation

### Chapter 3 — Reviews, Before-and-Afters, and the Fog of Social Proof (~1,800 words)

**Composite Scenario C: The Five-Star Fog** (hair transplant clinic with uniformly glowing reviews on its own platforms; mixed signals elsewhere)

Sections:
- Reviews answer emotional questions first: kindness, cleanliness, communication. They answer surgical questions last (was the diagnosis accurate, did the bite/symmetry/scar quality hold up over time)
- Most reviews are written too early in the cosmetic-surgery healing arc — "I cried when I saw my new nose" at week 2 is not the same as the result at month 18
- The before-and-after photo problem: cosmetic surgery transformation imagery is the most curated visual content on the internet; selection bias is extreme
- The "model patient" problem: clinics often photograph their best 5–10 cases and show them to every prospective patient
- The influencer/discount-code problem (especially for hair transplants and BBL)
- Reading reviews like a field investigator: timing, specificity, similarity, complaint handling, missing middle (same framework as dental, surgical examples)
- The social media group problem: cosmetic surgery groups can be especially vulnerable to moderator bias and undisclosed clinic affiliations

**Decision Gate: Is social proof replacing due diligence?**

### Chapter 4 — Destination Fit Without Rankings (~1,500 words)

**Composite Scenario D: The Wrong Destination for the Patient** (a 58-year-old with controlled hypertension and BMI 34 is "approved" for combination liposuction + abdominoplasty + BBL by a destination clinic)

Sections:
- No "best" destination for cosmetic surgery — only fit
- Destination Fit Matrix (8 dimensions, adapted from dental):
  1. Surgical Fit (does the surgeon have specific experience with your procedure type, including revision experience)
  2. Continuity Fit (can you return for revision if needed)
  3. Communication Fit (consent forms in a language you understand; some surgeons abroad use translators of variable quality)
  4. Travel Fit (flight duration matters more for cosmetic surgery — DVT risk after surgery)
  5. Timeline Fit (8 dimensions for cosmetic — see notes)
  6. Legal Fit (cross-border malpractice is meaningfully harder than dental)
  7. Personal Fit (ability to advocate, body-image stability, support system at home)
  8. Aftercare Fit (wound care, drain management, suture removal, complication management at home)
- The no-rankings approach
- Decision Gate: Would this still make sense if the clinic were not famous and the price were not low?

## Part II — Money and Quotes (~5,500 words across 2 chapters)

### Chapter 5 — The True Cost of Cheaper Surgery (~3,000 words)

**Composite Scenario E: The Bargain That Was Not** ("$4,500 all-inclusive" rhinoplasty becomes $9,800 after arrival, exam, and an unexpected revision-coverage need)

Sections:
- Four prices in cosmetic surgery quotes: advertised price, remote estimate, in-person plan, completed-case cost (parallel to dental)
- The advertised "all-inclusive" trap: what's typically included vs. excluded
- Specific line items that are commonly excluded:
  - Anesthesia (often a separate fee, sometimes substantial)
  - Implants (especially breast implants — "implant fee" can add $1,500–$4,000)
  - Compression garments (mandatory for many procedures, $150–$400)
  - Drain management
  - Lab work and pre-op clearance
  - Lymphatic massage (commonly recommended for body procedures)
  - Hotel for required minimum stay
  - Companion travel
  - Lost income
  - Revision policy (what does revision actually cost the patient — book offers a worksheet)
- The combination-procedure cost trap: "we'll add a tummy tuck for only $2,000 more" — what's missing from that math
- The DCC / currency conversion section (carry over from dental)
- The "demonstration case" discount: signing rights to your photos and videos for marketing in exchange for $500–$2,000 off
- Why biology is not negotiable by airfare (parallel to dental — immediate placement of final teeth was not safe; immediate combination of surgeries is similarly dangerous)
- Worksheet: True Cost Calculator (extended from dental version)

### Chapter 6 — The Cosmetic Surgery Quote Decoder (~2,500 words)

Sections:
- Treatment map: surgeon should provide a written, procedure-by-procedure plan, not just a package name
- Universal quote checklist: ~30 line items with included/excluded/conditional/unclear marking
- Imaging requirements: photos, 3D imaging where applicable, in-person physical exam — what each evaluates
- The surgeon's fee separated from the facility fee separated from the anesthesia fee — this separation matters
- Implants: brand, size, model, projection, surface texture — the book asks for these specifically per the implant device tracking section
- Anesthesia line items: type, provider, monitoring, fasting requirements
- Lab fees: pre-op blood work, EKG (where applicable), pregnancy test (where applicable), other clearance
- Garments and supplies
- Revision policy: in writing, with specific definitions of what triggers covered revision
- Complication policy: in writing, with specific definitions of what's covered (and what's not — most foreign clinics do not cover hospital admission abroad as part of "complication coverage")
- Comparing quotes fairly: same-row, same-row methodology
- Worksheet: Cosmetic Surgery Quote Comparison

### Chapter 17 — Health History, Medications, Allergies (~1,800 words)

**Composite Scenario J: The Missed Medication** (patient on SSRIs and beta-blocker doesn't fully disclose; pre-op screening misses it; an anesthesia complication results)

Sections:

### Chapter 18 — Anesthesia Is the Leading Cause of Cosmetic Surgery Deaths Abroad (~2,500 words) ★ NEW CHAPTER

(This is one of the four net-new chapters with no parallel in the dental book.)

Sections:
- Why anesthesia is the highest-risk variable in foreign cosmetic surgery
- Baseline data: ~1.1 anesthesia deaths per million in US; cosmetic-surgery-specific complication rate; risk multipliers
- Types of anesthesia and what they require: local, regional, sedation (light/moderate/deep), general
- Provider type: anesthesiologist vs. CRNA — what evidence shows about provider type vs. patient/procedure factors
- The book's editorial position: identify the named anesthesia provider, verify their credentials in the destination country, do not accept "our team will manage it"
- Monitoring requirements: pulse oximetry, capnography, blood pressure, ECG — what each monitors
- Emergency protocol verification: rescue cart, malignant hyperthermia kit, airway equipment, transfer plan
- The combination-procedure risk multiplier: combining surgeries multiplies anesthesia time and complication risk
- Specific health conditions that should trigger additional anesthesia consultation pre-op: sleep apnea, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, BMI considerations, prior anesthesia complications, certain medications including GLP-1 agonists
- Day-of script: questions to ask before going under
- Red flags: "we use general anesthesia, do not worry" without specifics; nurse monitoring without anesthesiologist or CRNA on-site; no documented emergency protocol
- Pull quote: a sidebar pull quote about anesthesia being the leading cause of cosmetic surgery deaths abroad

### Chapter 19 — Mental Health Pre-Op Screening (~1,800 words) ★ NEW CHAPTER

(Net-new chapter with no parallel in dental.)

Sections:
- Why this chapter exists: not to medicalize the desire for cosmetic surgery, not to gatekeep, but because BDD prevalence in cosmetic surgery patients is 7–18% and outcomes for this population are poor
- The book does not diagnose; the book screens (with validated tools)
- Body Dysmorphic Disorder: what it is in plain language
- Why surgical outcomes are poor for BDD patients (per peer-reviewed evidence)
- The two validated screening tools (BDDQ and DCQ) — included as appendix worksheet
- What to do with a positive screen: pause, see a qualified mental health professional, do not proceed with surgery until cleared
- Realistic expectation calibration: even non-BDD patients benefit from clarifying expectations before surgery
- The role of pre-op psych consult: required in some jurisdictions, optional in most, recommended by most board-certified plastic surgeons regardless
- Eating disorder considerations (particularly relevant to body procedures)
- Major life-event screening: avoid surgery within 6–12 months of significant grief, divorce, job loss, etc.
- Editorial framing: This is your body and your decision; the book's job is to make sure the decision is made under conditions that protect you. If a screening tool suggests pausing, that's protection, not judgment.

### Chapter 28 — Procedures We Recommend Against Having Abroad in Low-Regulation Settings (~3,000 words) ★ NEW CHAPTER

(The editorial-position chapter. Highest legal-review priority. Sourced citations on every page.)

Sections:
- Why this chapter exists: a buyer-protection guide should be willing to take editorial positions on documented harm patterns; this is journalism, not advice
- The list (subject to plastic surgeon reviewer's sign-off):
  1. **BBL with intramuscular fat injection** (anywhere; recommend ultrasound-guided BBL by board-certified surgeon in accredited facility, or do not have the procedure)
  2. **Combination procedures involving BBL** (the deaths concentrate here)
  3. **BBL or large-volume liposuction in the Dominican Republic** (CDC MMWR documented 93 US-citizen deaths 2009–2022, 92% involving gluteal fat transfer)
  4. **Stem cell rejuvenation, "regenerative" or anti-aging cosmetic treatments abroad** (not regulated as medicine in most jurisdictions; treat the entire category as a warning)
  5. **Surgery in non-accredited "boutique" or "luxury" facilities** without JCI or equivalent accreditation, regardless of country
  6. **Any procedure where the surgeon will not provide their board certification, registration number, and verifiable credentials before deposit**
  7. **Any procedure where the named anesthesia provider's credentials cannot be verified in the destination country's official register**
- The framing: "We are not telling you not to have these procedures. We are telling you the documented evidence on these specific patterns is sufficient that we cannot in good conscience offer 'how to choose well' framing for them."
