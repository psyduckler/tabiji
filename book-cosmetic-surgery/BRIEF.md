## 1. Executive summary

The cosmetic medical tourism market is currently around **$7.4 billion globally (2025)** and projected to reach **$62.78 billion by 2035** at a 23.84% CAGR. Cosmetic procedures account for approximately **25% of all medical tourism**. The market is driven by 40–80% cost savings vs domestic prices, aggressive influencer-led marketing, and patient cost-shock dynamics that mirror the dental market the Volume 1 book serves.

It is also the medical-tourism vertical with the **highest documented patient-harm rate**, especially for one specific procedure: the **Brazilian Butt Lift (BBL)** has a mortality rate estimated between 1 in 3,000 and 1 in 6,241 — the highest of any cosmetic procedure. The CDC's MMWR (Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report) documents **93 U.S. citizen deaths from cosmetic surgery in the Dominican Republic alone between 2009 and 2022**, with the annual rate more than tripling in recent years — and **92% of those fatalities involved gluteal fat transfer (BBL)**.

Volume 2 of the Tabiji Field Guides series is designed to be the consumer-protection field guide this market needs. It uses the same calm, protective editorial voice as the dental book; reuses approximately 60–70% of the framework, voice, and production pipeline; and adds the surgery-specific content that makes the difference between a competent buyer's guide and editorial malpractice in a higher-stakes vertical.

This brief locks the strategic decisions before writing begins. **No manuscript writing should start until this brief is signed off.**

## 3. Audience

**Primary persona:**
- Adult, 30–55 years old, predominantly female (cosmetic surgery is ~85–90% female globally)
- Considering one or more of: BBL, breast augmentation/lift, rhinoplasty, abdominoplasty, liposuction, facelift, eyelid surgery, or hair transplant
- Has received a domestic quote that triggered cost-shock, OR has been exposed to influencer/Instagram marketing for a foreign clinic
- Is in the **research-and-comparison phase**, not yet committed
- Values reading a book vs. only Reddit/forums (a self-selecting more careful subset)

**Secondary personas:**
- Adult considering hair restoration (predominantly male, 25–55)
- Family member or partner of someone planning surgery abroad (the "companion" reader)
- Someone who already had a complication from foreign surgery and is researching what to do next (small but high-engagement segment)

**Audience differences from dental that affect editorial voice:**

| Dimension | Dental book audience | Cosmetic surgery book audience |
|---|---|---|
| Primary motivation | Cost (estimate too high to afford) | Cost AND aspiration (transformation) |
| Time pressure | Often urgent (pain, infection) | Rarely medically urgent — almost always elective |
| Body image stakes | Low to moderate | Very high; vulnerable population |
| Marketing landscape | Professional clinic websites, some influencer | Heavy influencer, Instagram, TikTok, before/after reels |
| Failure mode if poorly chosen | Restoration breaks, expensive remediation | Disfigurement, hospitalization, **death** |
| Family/companion role | Helpful | Often essential — someone signs for sedated patient |
| Mental health screening | Not relevant | Critical pre-operatively (BDD, unrealistic expectations) |

These differences drive specific content additions in Volume 2 — see §6 (Net New Content Map).

## 4. Procedure scope (IN / OUT decisions)

### IN scope for Volume 2

The book covers procedures that are:
- Predominantly **elective**
- Performed in **outpatient or short-stay** settings
- Have **substantial cosmetic-tourism volume**
- Share a similar **decision dynamic** (cost-shock, marketing-influenced, transformation-framed)

Specifically:

**Body procedures:**
- Brazilian Butt Lift (BBL / gluteal fat transfer)
- Liposuction (standard, VASER, laser-assisted, etc.)
- Abdominoplasty (tummy tuck) and "mommy makeover" combination procedures
- Breast augmentation (implants)
- Breast lift (mastopexy)
- Breast reduction
- Body lift, arm lift, thigh lift

**Face procedures:**
- Rhinoplasty (cosmetic)
- Facelift / mini-facelift / SMAS lift
- Brow lift
- Eyelid surgery (blepharoplasty)
- Buccal fat removal
- Chin / jawline implants
- Otoplasty

**Hair restoration:**
- FUE (Follicular Unit Extraction) hair transplant
- DHI (Direct Hair Implantation)
- Sapphire FUE / other proprietary variants
- Beard transplants

### OUT of scope (deferred to future volumes)

The following are **deliberately excluded** from Volume 2 with brief explanations the book itself can use:

| Out-of-scope | Why not in Volume 2 |
|---|---|
| Bariatric surgery | Different market dynamics, different complication profile, longer recovery, different aftercare needs. Recommended for **Volume 3**. |
| Orthopedic surgery (knee/hip) | Older audience, different cost-shock pattern, lower influencer-marketing intensity. Recommended for **Volume 4**. |
| Cardiac surgery abroad | Specialist niche, very different risk model. Not recommended for the series. |
| Cancer treatment tourism | Highly individualized, ethically complex (advanced disease patients). Not recommended for the series. |
| Gender-affirming surgery | Important but requires specialist editorial team and sensitivity considerations beyond the scope of one editor. Could be a future standalone volume with appropriate co-editorship. |
| IVF/fertility tourism | Different decision dynamics; better served by a separate fertility-focused book. |
| LASIK / cataract eye surgery | Lower cost-tourism volume, generally lower harm rate, doesn't fit the cost-shock framing. |
| Stem-cell / experimental "rejuvenation" treatments | The book will mention these only as a **strong warning**; we do not legitimize them with a "buyer-protection" framing. |

This scope is locked unless you propose a change. Adding or removing procedures changes the structure substantially.

## 5. The procedure landscape (with current data)

This section is the briefing data for whoever drafts the manuscript. Numbers are current as of mid-2026 from the research sources cited in §16.

### Top destinations, by procedure category

| Procedure category | Dominant destinations | Typical price (vs US) | Notable risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BBL** | Dominican Republic, Mexico, Colombia, Turkey | $3,500–$8,000 (vs $15,000–$30,000 US) | **Mortality 1:3,000–1:6,241**; almost all from intramuscular fat injection causing pulmonary fat embolism |
| **Breast augmentation** | Mexico, Thailand, Turkey, South Korea | $4,000–$7,500 (vs $8,000–$15,000 US) | Implant device-tracking gap, capsular contracture, BIA-ALCL (textured-implant lymphoma), need for revision |
| **Liposuction** | Mexico, Dominican Republic, Colombia, Turkey | $2,500–$6,500 (vs $5,000–$12,000 US) | Combination procedures (lipo + BBL or lipo + tummy tuck) increase risk dramatically |
| **Tummy tuck** | Mexico, Thailand, Turkey | $4,500–$8,500 (vs $8,000–$15,000 US) | Combination with lipo or BBL is where deaths concentrate |
| **Rhinoplasty** | Turkey, South Korea, Thailand, Mexico | $2,500–$5,500 (vs $7,500–$15,000 US) | Revision rates ~10–15% even in experienced hands; revision abroad is harder than primary |
| **Facelift** | Thailand, Mexico, Turkey, South Korea | $4,500–$9,500 (vs $12,000–$25,000 US) | Long recovery; flying back too early increases complication risk |
| **Hair transplant (FUE/DHI)** | **Turkey** (dominant), Mexico, India, Hungary | $2,500–$5,500 (vs $15,000–$25,000 US) | Lower mortality risk than other procedures, but high "shop-floor" vs surgeon-led variance; many "package mills" |

### Market data (sourced)

- Global cosmetic medical tourism: **$7.40B (2025) → $9.16B (2026), projected $62.78B by 2035** (23.84% CAGR)
- Cosmetic = ~25% of total medical tourism market
- Asia-Pacific: ~40% of the cosmetic medical tourism market, fastest-growing region
- Cost savings driver: 40–80% vs home country
- Top destinations overall: Thailand, Mexico, India, Turkey, Malaysia, Costa Rica, Singapore
- South Korea: dominant for facial contouring (K-beauty influence)
- Turkey: dominant for cosmetic + hair restoration for European/MENA patients; "tens of thousands" of international hair transplant patients per year, with individual clinics performing 25,000+ procedures over their lifetime

### Documented harm patterns (sourced — to be cited in book)

**Dominican Republic (CDC MMWR 73(3), 2024):**
- 93 U.S. citizen deaths from cosmetic surgery, 2009–2022
- Annual rate: 4.1/year (2009–2018) → 13.0/year (2019–2022); peak 17 in 2020
- 92% of fatalities involved gluteal fat transfer (BBL)
- Almost all were women; median age 40
- Cause-of-death pattern: fat embolism, venous thromboembolism, exacerbated by obesity and combination procedures
- Documented infection outbreaks: *Mycobacterium abscessus* (nontuberculous mycobacteria)

**BBL specifically (American Society of Plastic Surgeons, multiple peer-reviewed sources):**
- South Florida: 25 BBL fat-emboli deaths 2010–2022
- 14 of those occurred AFTER the safety guidelines published in 2018 and 2019
- Risk drops dramatically with: ultrasound guidance, board-certified surgeon, injection above the muscle (subcutaneous only)
- Ultrasound-guided BBL has reported zero fatalities to date

These are the data points that will give the book editorial weight. They cannot be omitted.

## 6. The high-risk procedure list

The book takes an **explicit editorial position** on a small number of procedure-destination patterns where the documented harm rate is high enough that "ask better questions" framing is insufficient. This is a meaningful editorial difference from Volume 1.

### Procedures the book recommends against having abroad (in low-regulation settings)

The exact list and language requires the plastic surgeon reviewer's sign-off, but the candidate list is:

1. **BBL with intramuscular fat injection** anywhere — recommend ultrasound-guided BBL by board-certified surgeon, or do not have the procedure
2. **Combination procedures involving BBL** (BBL + tummy tuck, BBL + breast augmentation, etc.) — combination is where the deaths concentrate; the book should clearly state that combining BBL with another procedure multiplies risk
3. **BBL or large-volume liposuction in the Dominican Republic** — given the documented CDC data, this combination warrants the strongest possible advisory
4. **Stem cell rejuvenation, anti-aging, or "regenerative" cosmetic treatments abroad** — these are not regulated as medicine in most jurisdictions; the book should treat the entire category as a warning, not a buyer's guide
5. **Surgery in non-accredited facilities advertised as "boutique" or "luxury"** without JCI or equivalent accreditation, regardless of country
6. **Any procedure where the surgeon will not provide their board certification, registration number, and verifiable credentials before deposit**

### How the book frames this

Per the dental book's calm-protective voice — not as scare tactics. The framing is: *"This book is a buyer-protection guide. There are a small number of cases where the protective answer is not 'how to choose well' but 'do not have this procedure under these conditions.' Here are those cases, with the documented evidence."*

This is a **defensible editorial position** because every point above is grounded in published data (CDC MMWR, ASPS guidelines, peer-reviewed surgical journals). It is also the right thing to do for the reader.

**Open decision:** Confirm the editorial team is willing to take this position. If you want a softer "ask better questions" framing throughout, that's defensible too, but the book becomes weaker as consumer protection. My recommendation is to take the stronger position.

## 8. Net new content map

Content that has no parallel in `book-dental/` and must be written from scratch:

### New full chapters (estimated ~5,000–7,000 words each)

**Chapter X: Anesthesia is the leading cause of cosmetic surgery deaths abroad.**
- Why anesthesia is the highest-risk variable in foreign cosmetic surgery
- Types of anesthesia and what they require (training, monitoring, equipment, recovery)
- Specific questions to ask BEFORE accepting a quote (anesthesiologist credentials, presence vs. nurse-administered, monitoring protocol, ASA classification of facility)
- Red flags: "we use general anesthesia, do not worry" without specifics
- Required reading for any combination procedure or any surgery with sedation longer than 90 minutes
- Anchor data: pulmonary fat embolism risk in BBL, anesthesia complication rates

**Chapter Y: Procedures we recommend against having abroad in low-regulation settings.**
- The high-risk list from §6 above, with full explanations
- The line between "high-risk done well" (board-certified, accredited facility, ultrasound guidance) and "high-risk done in package mill"
- Editorial position: this book will not pretend every procedure is safe with sufficient research

**Chapter Z: Mental health pre-op screening (cosmetic-specific).**
- Body Dysmorphic Disorder (BDD) screening
- Realistic expectation calibration
- The role of pre-op psych consult — required in some jurisdictions, optional in most
- Why post-op satisfaction correlates with pre-op screening, not surgical skill alone
- When to pause the entire decision because the underlying motivation needs work first

**Chapter AA: The companion role for cosmetic surgery patients.**
- Pre-op preparation
- Day-of: who signs what
- Recovery week 1: medical observation responsibilities (drains, swelling, signs of infection, mental state, medication management)
- Travel home: airport navigation while recovering, what to do if a complication appears mid-flight
- Long-term: emotional support during the awkward in-between healing weeks

**Chapter BB: Warning signs in the OR and recovery — non-negotiable rules.**
- What a patient or companion should never accept (e.g., "post-op consent forms presented while patient is sedated")
- Specific clinical warning signs by procedure
- When to demand transfer to a hospital, not the clinic's "recovery suite"
- The "do not fly home" decision tree

### New sections within adapted chapters

- **Implant device tracking** (within Surgical Record chapter): UDI, manufacturer patient cards, lifetime warranties from Mentor / Sientra / Allergan, what to demand for non-US implants, GS1's role as global UDI issuer
- **Combination procedure risk multiplier** (within the Quote Decoder): doing two procedures in one OR session can multiply the complication risk in non-linear ways; the book breaks this down
- **Photography and consent** (within the funnel chapter): cosmetic surgery clinics often require patients to consent to "marketing use" of photos; this should be opt-in, never bundled into a discount
- **Revision policy realism** (within Warranty section): cosmetic surgery revision rates are 5–15% even in experienced hands; foreign-clinic revision policies are often theatrical
- **Hair transplant specifics** (own subsection in multiple chapters): different decision dynamics, different harm profile, why "package mill" risk is the dominant concern rather than mortality

### Editorial sidebars (recurring, like dental's pull quotes)

Plan for 6–8 sidebar callouts (vs. dental's 5):
1. "Hope is important. It is not enough to sign consent." (carry-forward from dental — series voice)
2. "The cheapest complication is the one avoided before boarding." (carry-forward from dental)
3. A new one specific to BBL fat embolism risk
4. A new one specific to combination procedures
5. A new one specific to anesthesia
6. "Your sister/partner/companion is not a nurse. Plan accordingly." (companion role)
7. "Photos cannot show what your bone, skin, and underlying tissue will do." (the limits of before/after marketing)
8. The closing positive scenario sidebar (parallel to "Cheaper dental work is only cheaper when you can finish it well.")

## 10. Editorial risk management

Surgery has higher legal and reputational risk than dental. The following editorial choices are designed to manage that risk.

### Stronger disclaimers throughout

- The Important Disclaimer (front matter) explicitly names anesthesia, surgical complications, mortality risk in elective surgery, the limits of the book, and the requirement that any decision be made under the care of qualified clinicians who have examined the patient
- The Appendix Disclaimer adds language vetted by a medical malpractice attorney (mandatory for surgery; optional for dental)
- Every chapter that touches a high-risk procedure includes a contextual reminder that the book is education, not advice
- The "Procedures we recommend against" chapter (Chapter Y) is explicitly framed as editorial position with citations, not personal advice

### Stronger sourcing requirements

- All clinical claims must cite peer-reviewed surgical literature, CDC MMWR data, ASPS or analogous society guidelines, or named regulatory authorities
- All mortality / complication rate citations must include the source and the date
- The Source Notes section (back matter) is substantially longer than dental's — this is a feature, not a bug

### Defensive editorial choices on specific topics

| Topic | Defensive choice |
|---|---|
| BBL mortality | Use ASPS/peer-reviewed numbers; cite specifically; never round down; include the safer-when-done-correctly framing |
| Dominican Republic deaths | Use the CDC MMWR primary source; the book is reporting CDC's data, not making the claim itself; legally lower exposure |
| "Procedures we recommend against" | Editorial position grounded in cited evidence; the book is journalism, not advice; defended legally as opinion based on documented public-health data |
| Stem-cell / regenerative cosmetic | Treat as warning category, never as buyer-protection category; do not name specific "treatments" we'd review |
| Comparing destinations | Same as dental — no rankings, no "best of" lists. Compare patterns, not countries. |
| Specific clinic or surgeon names | **NEVER** in the published book. The book is framework + disclosed-source data. Specific clinic recommendations open libel risk and undermine the no-rankings position. |
| Patient stories | All composites; explicitly disclaimed. No real patient identifiers, ever. |

### Sensitivity considerations

A sensitivity reader is more important for this book than for dental. Specifically:

- **Body image language** — the book must avoid implying that wanting cosmetic surgery is inherently disordered or shameful. It should also avoid the opposite extreme (uncritical celebration of "transformation"). The voice should be: this is your body and your decision; we are here to help you make it under conditions that protect you.
- **Weight-related framing** — the obesity-as-comorbidity discussion (relevant to BBL safety) must be clinically accurate without weight-shaming language. The framing is: certain medical conditions including high BMI affect anesthesia risk and should be disclosed; this is not a judgment of the patient.
- **Gender language** — cosmetic surgery patients are predominantly women but increasingly diverse. Default to gender-neutral language; use "she" only when describing a specific named composite.
- **Hair loss language** — for the hair restoration section, avoid pity-framing or shame-framing; use neutral clinical language about hair loss patterns and the patient's own stated goals.

### What we WILL NOT do

- Will not recommend specific clinics, surgeons, or destinations
- Will not promise outcomes
- Will not minimize documented mortality data
- Will not sensationalize documented mortality data either
- Will not accept payment from clinics, surgeons, or facilitators (consistent with Volume 1 editorial position)
- Will not include before/after photographs from any specific clinic in the published book

## 16. Risks and unknowns

### Risks I am tracking

1. **The plastic surgeon reviewer may push back on Chapter Y ("procedures we recommend against").** If they want the language softened beyond what the data supports, we have an editorial decision to make. The fallback is to publish Chapter Y as a standalone editorial appendix with the surgeon's review covering only the rest of the book. This needs to be discussed with the reviewer up front.

2. **The body-image language may attract criticism regardless of how carefully it is written.** Cosmetic surgery is politically charged. Some readers will want stronger feminist critique; others will want stronger embrace of patient autonomy. The book should be clear about its position (consumer protection, not advocacy in either direction) and not try to please both camps.

3. **The BBL framing is the riskiest single editorial choice in the book.** If we under-warn, patients die. If we over-warn, we're labeled fearmongering. The defense is sourcing — every BBL claim should be cited to ASPS, peer-reviewed surgical literature, or CDC MMWR data, never to the book's own opinion.

4. **The Dominican Republic data is geographically specific and could be read as racially coded.** The CDC data is the CDC data, but the book should be explicit that the issue is regulatory and facility-specific, not destination-essentialist. Same logic the dental book applied to Turkey.

5. **The hair-transplant section may benefit from a separate volume eventually.** It's a different audience, different decision dynamic, different harm profile. Including it in Volume 2 is a commercial choice (broader market) and an editorial compromise. If sales data shows the cosmetic-surgery audience is buying but the hair-transplant audience is not, consider spinning hair restoration into Volume 5.

### Unknowns the brief does not resolve

- Whether the right reviewer budget secures top-tier reviewers in the timeline. Reviewer outreach should start NOW, not when the manuscript is done.
- Whether Tabiji has internal capacity to do a sensitivity-reader pass internally or needs to hire externally.
- Whether the JCI accreditation directory covers enough cosmetic surgery facilities to be a useful reader resource (the directory is heavily hospital-weighted, less ambulatory-clinic-weighted).
- Whether KDP's category structure will let us sit in MEDICAL > Plastic & Cosmetic Surgery as a primary category (some KDP categories are restricted by content type).
