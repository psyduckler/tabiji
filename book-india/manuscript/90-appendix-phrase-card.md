## The five phrases that matter most {-}

If you only memorize five, memorize these:

1. **Nahīṁ chāhiye** (nuh-HEE CHA-hi-yay) — *"I don't want it."* The single most useful phrase in this book. Walk away while saying it. Works on touts, scratch-card sellers, henna artists, fake guides, and chai-stall handlers.
2. **Bahut zyādā hai** (buh-HOOT ZYAA-daa hai) — *"That's too much."* Polite, firm, universally understood. Pair with a counter-offer or with Nahīṁ chāhiye.
3. **Meter chalāiye** (MEE-tar cha-LAA-ye) — *"Use the meter."* Auto-rickshaw and kaali peeli phrase number one.
4. **Mujhe police station le chaliye** (MOO-jay puh-LEES STAY-shun lay cha-LEE-yay) — *"Take me to the police station."* The phrase that ends a fare dispute, a captive boat ride, or a beach-shack chair shakedown — almost no scammer wants the police actually involved.
5. **Madad! Police!** (muh-DUD! puh-LEES!) — *"Help! Police!"* — at full volume, in a public space, when a situation is escalating.

## Numbers (1 to 1000) {-}

| English | Hindi script | Transliteration | Say it like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ten | दस | das | dus |
| Twenty | बीस | bīs | bees |
| Fifty | पचास | pachās | puh-CHAAS |
| Hundred (sau) | सौ | sau | saw |
| Two hundred | दो सौ | do sau | doh saw |
| Five hundred | पाँच सौ | pāñch sau | paanch saw |
| Thousand (hazaar) | हज़ार | hazār | huh-ZAAR |
| Two thousand | दो हज़ार | do hazār | doh huh-ZAAR |
| Lakh (100,000) | लाख | lākh | laakh |

The shorthand most Indian shopkeepers and drivers use: **sau** (100) and **hazaar** (1000). "Tin sau" = three hundred. "Char hazaar" = four thousand. "Ek lakh" = one hundred thousand.

## Regional add-ons {-}

For southern cities — particularly Chennai — most working-language interactions happen in **English** or **Tamil**, not Hindi. The basic Tamil phrase pair is:

- **Vēṇḍām** (VAY-num) — "I don't want it."
- **Nandri** (NUN-dree) — "Thank you."

For Kolkata, basic **Bengali**:

- **Lāgbe nā** (LAG-bay nah) — "Not needed."
- **Dhonyobad** (don-yoh-BAAD) — "Thank you."

For Goa, **Konkani** is the local language but English is universally used in tourist contexts. Hindi will work everywhere on this list except in the deepest village markets.
