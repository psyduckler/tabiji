# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

Read this chapter slowly. The sixty scams in this book differ in setting and amount — a saree shop in T. Nagar, a felucca-equivalent in Varanasi, a "tourist office" in Paharganj, a beach-shack chair in Baga — but underneath, they run on six repeating patterns. Memorise the six and you will spot a brand-new scam the moment it begins.

## 1. The Captive-Position Lever {-}

You are on a moving auto-rickshaw, in a boat in the middle of Lake Pichola, halfway up the elephant ramp at Amber Fort, or three deep into the Vishwanath lanes following a "shortcut." That is the moment the price reveal lands. Refusing now is socially and physically harder than refusing at the curb.

**The fingerprint:** the price was either never quoted, or quoted suspiciously low, or written in a way that produced an ambiguous "per person / per ride" trap. The reveal happens after you have given up your exit option.

**The defence:** every Indian transit decision is made at the curb. Confirm the total, in writing if possible, before the wheels turn or the boat leaves the dock. If the price changes mid-ride, the legal answer is to pay the originally agreed amount, walk to a busy area or a uniformed officer, and refuse to engage further.

## 2. The Religious or Authority Costume {-}

A saffron robe at Kalighat. A laminated "ASI Guide" badge at Golconda. A navy polo with a "Delhi Tourism" lanyard outside NDLS. A vest at Marina Beach demanding parking fees. The costume signals that the person has authority, knows the system, and is here to help. Almost none of them do.

**The fingerprint:** the costume mismatches the official channel. Real ASI guides are hired at the inside counter, not by men loitering at the entrance. Real India Tourism staff are inside the government building at 88 Janpath, not on the platform at NDLS. Real Mumbai parking is run through the Smart City app, not by men in fluorescent vests with handwritten "tickets."

**The defence:** the costume is the warning, not the credential. Verify the channel — the inside counter, the government address, the official app — before the interaction continues.

## 3. The Sub-Market Quote {-}

A ₹50 auto from CST when the meter would be ₹150. A ₹100 sunrise boat in Varanasi when the going rate is ₹300. A ₹600 elephant ride at Amber Fort when the regulated price is ₹900. A ₹500 prepaid taxi from RGIA when the official KIAL counter is ₹1,200. The quote is too good to walk away from — and that is exactly the point.

**The fingerprint:** the quote is a function of the *captive position* the operator is about to put you in. The cheaper the quote, the more dramatic the inflation will be once the trip is underway.

**The defence:** if the quote is meaningfully below market, it is the bait, not the deal. Pay the going rate at the official channel. Three hundred extra rupees for a metered Ola is the cheapest insurance in this book.

## 4. The Commission Detour {-}

The auto driver mentions his "cousin's shop." The Hawa Mahal rickshaw guide knows a "wholesale" emporium. The Mumbai Dhobhi Ghat "free guide" knows a chai stall. The Bangalore Commercial Street stranger has a "private back room." Every variant is the same: the journey from A to B routes through a third location where someone earns 30 to 40 percent on whatever you buy.

**The fingerprint:** the route deviates from your stated destination, and the deviation is framed as helpful. The shop owner greets the driver or guide by name. The prices have no tags. The chai is free.

**The defence:** state at the start of every ride and every walk: *no shops, direct to destination only*. Free chai is never free. Browse independently in the named bazaars (Sadar Bazaar in Agra, Bapu Bazaar in Jaipur, Charminar in Hyderabad, Colaba in Mumbai), and buy serious items only at government emporiums (Cottage Industries Exposition, Rajasthali, UP Handloom, Co-optex, Manjusha) where prices are posted and fixed.

## 5. The Made-Up Closure or Emergency {-}

The Red Fort is "closed for a government holiday." Your Paharganj guesthouse is "full tonight" or "closed for renovation." Your CCU airport hotel "had a fire last night." The Mathura toll plaza requires a "mandatory rest stop." The Charminar shop your auto driver knows is the *only* one with the "real" silk because the others are "tourist traps."

**The fingerprint:** the inconvenient truth is delivered by the person who happens to be best positioned to "help." The substitute is conveniently ready. You cannot verify the original claim from where you are standing.

**The defence:** call the source directly. Your hotel from your booking confirmation, the ASI website for monument hours, the Indian Railways IRCTC app for ticket-counter status. Almost without exception, the original claim was fabricated.

## 6. The Brand-Mimicry Storefront {-}

A "Government Tourist Information Office" near Jaipur Railway Station with maps on the wall and a "Ministry of Tourism" logo that has one letter different from the real one. A "Bawarchii" with two i's. A "Shahh Ghouse" with two h's. A "Karma Royal" scratch card promotion. A "ticketstajmahal.com" site that ranks above asi.nic.in on Google.

**The fingerprint:** the brand name is one letter, one syllable, one suffix off the real one. The storefront, the website, the lanyard — all polished enough to fool a glance.

**The defence:** Google the exact spelling, every time. The real RTDC (Rajasthan Tourism Development Corporation) office is inside or directly adjacent to the railway station, not in a shop nearby. The real Bawarchi is at Lalita Bagh in Begumpet. The real ASI ticket-booking site is at asi.nic.in or asi.payumoney.com — and nowhere else.

## Spend ten minutes here {-}

Before you read the city chapters, sit with the six patterns above for ten minutes. They will outlast every individual scam in this book. New variations appear every season — but they are always one of these six, in a new costume.
