# How to Use This Book {-}

India is extraordinary. The Taj Mahal at dawn, the Ganges Aarti at Dashashwamedh Ghat, the painted bazaars of Jaipur, the white marble of Udaipur's Lake Palace, the Charminar's four minarets above Laad Bazaar, the call to prayer over Hyderabad and the temple bells of Mylapore — there is nothing else like it on the planet. It is also, by a long way, one of the most scam-prone destinations a first-time traveler will visit. Auto-rickshaw drivers at Silk Board flip a meter that ticks at twice the legal rate. Men in polo shirts at New Delhi Railway Station claim your hotel has been "canceled" and walk you to a fake India Tourism office in Paharganj that charges $400 to rebook. A Lake Pichola boatman quotes ₹200 for a sunset ride, takes twenty photographs of you mid-ride, and demands ₹500 each. The Times of India reported in January 2025 that 500+ tourists were defrauded through fake Goa hotel listings in a single peak season.

We want you to come home with stories about the painted ceilings inside Amber Fort, the coracle rides on the backwaters, the silence at sunrise over the Ganga, and the taste of Hyderabadi biryani from the original Bawarchi — not about a ₹2,500 airport "prepaid" ride, a ₹50,000 worthless "investment" gem, or a Goan beach shack operator who blocked your car until you paid ten times the going rate.

That is why this book is short and practical. It documents sixty specific scams that trip up first-time visitors across twelve Indian cities, and it tells you the exact moves that stop them. Nothing in it is theoretical. Every scam has been reported by recent travelers on Reddit's r/India, r/IndiaTravel, r/IndianFoodIsBae, and city-specific subreddits, cross-referenced against published investigations from the Times of India, News18, Telangana Today, Deccan Herald, and Outlook Traveller, and confirmed against embassy advisories and consumer-protection databases. Where rupee amounts appear, they are the amounts the scam actually charges. Where official rates appear, they are what the metered, posted, or government-published price should be.

## How each scam is written

Every scam entry follows the same short format. First, the name of the scam — a landmark plus a mechanic, like "The Hawa Mahal Rickshaw Textile Detour" or "The CST Black-Taxi Meter Scam" — followed by the category it falls into (transport, overcharging, tourist trap, counterfeit, temple-beg, and so on), how serious and how common it is. Then two short sections: **How this scam works** — a plain narrative of how it unfolds in real time, with real Indian-rupee amounts where we have them — and **How to avoid it**, the specific moves that shut it down. Each entry ends with where it happens. If you can read one entry, you can read them all.

## The single most important chapter

The next chapter, **The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns**, is the most important part of this book. The sixty scams across these twelve cities may sound different from each other, but almost all of them are variations on six patterns: the captive-position lever (you on a moving rickshaw or a boat in the middle of Lake Pichola); the religious or authority costume (the saffron robe at Kalighat, the polo-shirt "Tourism" man at NDLS, the laminated "guide" badge at Golconda); the sub-market quote that buys captive time (the ₹50 auto, the ₹200 boat, the ₹600 elephant); the commission detour (the cousin's silk shop, the textile emporium in the suburbs); the made-up closure or emergency (your hotel "flooded," the Red Fort "closed for cleaning"); and the brand-mimicry storefront (the "India Tourism" sign, the look-alike Bawarchi, the Karma-Royal scratch card). Learn the six patterns and you will spot a new scam the moment it starts — even one we have not written down. Spend ten quiet minutes on that chapter before your trip. The rest of the book will then read as practical examples of patterns you already understand.

## The three bonuses at the back

After the city chapters, you will find three appendices that we hope you treasure more than the city chapters themselves.

The first is the **Hindi exit-phrase card**. Short phrases you can say, word for word, at the moments when your politeness would normally get you into trouble — the phrase for "no thank you," the phrase for "I have already paid," the phrase for "take me to the police station." Take a screenshot of it on your phone.

The second is the **recovery playbook**. A step-by-step flow of what to do in the first fifteen minutes, hour, day, and week after a scam. Most scam books only tell you how to avoid scams. If the worst happens — a Goa drug-sting "fine," a Mumbai dating-app bar bill, a Jaisalmer fake hotel listing whose address does not exist — you want this.

The third is the **emergency contacts appendix**. Tourist Police (1363), general police (100), the all-India emergency number (112), the cybercrime helpline (1930), the US Embassy in New Delhi (+91 11-2419-8000), and English-speaking medical care for each of the twelve cities, organized so you can find what you need in seconds.

## A suggested way to use the book

A week or two before your trip, read the first two chapters and skim the three appendices. Read the chapters for the cities you will be visiting. Take a screenshot of the phrase card and the emergency contacts on your phone. If anything in this book turns out to apply during your trip, open it again in the Uber or in your hotel room and check what to do.

You will almost certainly not need most of it. That is the point.

*Shubh yatra* — safe journey.
