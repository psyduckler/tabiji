# How to Use This Book {-}

Saudi Arabia is one of the last great destinations to open its doors. The sandstone tombs of Hegra carved into desert cliffs at AlUla, the coral-stone houses of Jeddah's Al Balad, the granite hairpins of the Al Hada road above Taif where baboons line the crash barriers, the green escarpment of Abha in the Asir mountains, the futurist skyline rising at NEOM, and — for the world's two billion Muslims — the Kaaba in Mecca and the Prophet's Mosque in Medina. Until 2019 almost none of this was reachable on a leisure visa. The Kingdom issued tourist visas to ordinary travelers for the first time that year, and under the Vision 2030 program it is now building a tourism industry from a standing start. That makes it thrilling. It also makes it a place where the infrastructure that protects visitors elsewhere — the metered taxi you can trust, the review you can rely on, the booking site you have used a hundred times — is either brand new or not yet there at all.

Here is the single most important thing to understand before you read another page. **In Saudi Arabia the risk to a tourist is almost entirely financial, not physical.** The Kingdom has one of the lowest violent-crime rates of any country you could visit; women walk safely alone at night in a way they cannot in most major Western cities, and the muggings, assaults, and drink-spikings that fill the scam books for other destinations barely register here. What you have to guard is your wallet, not your safety. The driver who claims the meter is broken and asks 250 SAR for a 60-SAR ride, the perfume seller offering "pure Taif rose oil" at 200 riyals when the genuine article runs 1,000 to 3,000 a tola, the WhatsApp message that knows your real Booking.com reference and wants you to "verify your card," the LinkedIn recruiter who needs a 15,000-SAR "processing fee" for a NEOM job that does not exist — these are the threats. They are real, they are common, and every one of them is preventable.

We want you to come home with stories about the silence inside a 2,000-year-old Nabataean tomb at sunrise, the call to prayer rolling across the Jeddah Corniche, the smell of damask roses in the Taif souq, and the first sight of the Kaaba — not about a 500-riyal airport taxi, a phantom rental-car scratch that ate your deposit, or a Hajj package whose hotel turned out not to exist.

That is why this book is short and practical. It documents sixty-four specific scams that trip up first-time visitors across ten Saudi cities, and it tells you the exact moves that stop them. Nothing in it is theoretical. Every scam has been reported by recent travelers on TripAdvisor's Saudi Arabia forum and Reddit, cross-referenced against Saudi and Gulf press (Arab News, the Saudi Gazette, Al Arabiya), consumer-protection and security investigations, and government and embassy advisories. Where riyal and dollar amounts appear, they are the amounts the scam actually charges, set against what the metered, posted, or official price should be.

## How each scam is written

Every scam entry follows the same short format. First, the name of the scam, the category it falls into, and how serious and how common it is. Then two short sections: **How this scam works** — a plain narrative of how it unfolds in real time, with real Saudi-riyal and US-dollar amounts where we have them — and **How to avoid it**, the specific moves that shut it down. Each entry ends with where it happens. If you can read one entry, you can read them all.

## The single most important chapter

The next chapter, **The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns**, is the most important part of this book. The sixty-four scams across these ten cities may sound different from each other, but almost all of them are variations on six patterns: the broken-meter fare overcharge; the stranger in an "official" costume; the online booking-and-visa phishing trap; the fake-goods authentication switch at the gold, perfume, honey, and date stalls; the commission and unlicensed-guide overcharge at the big sites; and the deposit-or-rental hostage. Learn the six patterns and you will spot a new scam the moment it starts — even one we have not written down. Spend ten quiet minutes on that chapter before your trip. The rest of the book will then read as practical examples of patterns you already understand.

## The three bonuses at the back

After the city chapters, you will find three appendices that we hope you treasure more than the city chapters themselves.

The first is the **Arabic exit-phrase card**. Short phrases you can say, word for word, at the moments when your politeness would normally get you into trouble — the phrase for "no thank you," the phrase for "how much?", the phrase for "I want a receipt." Take a screenshot of it on your phone.

The second is the **recovery playbook**. A step-by-step flow of what to do in the first fifteen minutes, hour, day, and week after a scam. Most scam books only tell you how to avoid scams. If the worst happens — a rental agency holding your deposit hostage over a scratch you did not make, a card number entered on a fake payment page, a deposit wired to a host whose apartment does not exist — you want this.

The third is the **emergency contacts appendix**. Saudi Arabia runs several emergency lines, and which one you dial depends on where you are. Across most of the country, **999** reaches the police, **997** the ambulance and Saudi Red Crescent, and **998** the fire and Civil Defense service. In the Mecca and Riyadh regions a single unified emergency number, **911**, now routes all of these. (For financial and online fraud — the bulk of what is in this book — the relevant hotlines are the Communications, Space & Technology Commission on **330330** and your card issuer, both covered in the appendix.) The appendix also lists embassy switchboards and English-speaking medical care for each of the ten cities, organized so you can find what you need in seconds.

## A suggested way to use the book

A week or two before your trip, read the first two chapters and skim the three appendices. Read the chapters for the cities you will be visiting. Take a screenshot of the phrase card and the emergency contacts on your phone. Install Careem and Uber before you fly, and bookmark the genuine government portals — **visa.visitsaudi.com** for the tourist eVisa and **Nusuk** for anything connected to Hajj or Umrah — so that when a slicker-looking copy appears in your search results, you already know which one is real. If anything in this book turns out to apply during your trip, open it again in the taxi or in your hotel room and check what to do.

You will almost certainly not need most of it. That is the point.

*Ma'a salama* — go in peace.
