# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The sixty-four scams in this book may sound different from each other, but almost all of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: The Broken Meter

The first pattern is the one you will meet first, often before you have left the airport: the taxi whose meter is suddenly "broken." Saudi cities are large, spread out, and — outside the ride-hailing apps — almost impossible to price if you do not already know the distance. Drivers know it. The meter is "broken," or "doesn't work up here on the mountain," or the quoted number turns out to have been "per person," and the fare lands at two, three, or four times what the ride should cost.

You will meet it at every major airport in this book. In Riyadh, the **Broken Meter Taxi Overcharge** quotes 150 SAR for a ride that should run 50 to 70, and the **Fake Taxi at Airport Interception** loads your bags into an unmarked sedan before quoting 250 SAR — four times the Careem rate. In Jeddah, **Airport Taxi Fare Inflation** opens at 500 SAR for an Al Balad ride that should cost 100 to 120. In Medina, **The Unlicensed Taxi Overcharge** quotes 150 SAR to the Haram and demands 300 on arrival, "because it was per person." The same script runs at **The Tabuk Airport & Street-Taxi Overcharge** (a six-kilometer, roughly $18 ride inflated to multiples), at the **Abha Airport & Mountain-Road Off-App Taxi Overcharge** (50 to 80 SAR doubled or tripled on the escarpment roads), at the **Dammam "Broken Meter" Taxi**, and at the **Dammam Airport "Broken Meter" Taxi to Khobar**, where a 90-to-150-SAR run to the seafront becomes a flat 250 to 350. In Taif, the broken meter has a mountain twist in the **Al Hada Cable Car and Mountain-Taxi Surcharge** and the **Airport and Street Taxi Overcharge**, where the genuine extra fuel cost of the hairpin road becomes cover for a made-up flat fare.

The pattern breaks the same way every time, and the fix is almost too simple: **use Careem or Uber.** Both operate reliably in every city in this book except AlUla (where you should pre-arrange a hotel transfer or use the official AlUla Taxi app), and both show the full fare before you confirm, on a route the app tracks. If you must take a street taxi, agree the full price out loud — or insist the meter is running — *before* the car moves, and if the driver refuses, walk to the next one. Never let anyone load your luggage into a trunk before the price and the driver are confirmed. Learn the benchmark for your ride (they are in the city chapters), photograph the plate, and follow your own GPS so you can challenge a "scenic" detour the moment it starts.

## Pattern 2: The Authority Costume

The second pattern is the stranger in an official role he has invented for himself.

This one matters in Saudi Arabia precisely because the real authorities are so trustworthy. Genuine Saudi police, immigration officers, and Ministry of Hajj guides do their jobs from marked counters, gates, and stations; a real immigration officer never approaches you in the arrivals hall to "verify" your passport, and a real officer never settles a "fine" in cash on the pavement. So almost any official-sounding approach inside a tourist or pilgrim zone is a costume.

You will see it in Dammam as **The Fake Airport Official**, who asks to "verify your entry," examines your passport, invents a "small problem with your paperwork," and steers you toward a waiting unlicensed car. You will see it across the Eastern Province as the **Fake "Plainclothes Officer" ID Shakedown**, where a man in local dress flashes a card, claims to be undercover police, and hints at a "fine" you can settle on the spot — a scam Arab News has tied to concrete arrests, including men running a fake checkpoint in Taif and impersonators with "perfect Saudi accents" in Jeddah. You will see a gentler version at the airports in Taif's **Airport and Street Taxi Overcharge**, where a tout poses as staff and even asks to see your passport before walking you to an inflated, unlicensed car. And you will see it inside the holy city as **The Haram Unlicensed Pilgrim-Guide Pitch**, where a man claiming to be a "licensed guide" shows a homemade ID card, collects his fee, then rushes or abandons you mid-ritual. A close cousin is the staged accident — the **Camera Drop Scam** in Jeddah, Abha, and Taif, where a friendly stranger hands you a pre-cracked camera, lets it "slip," and an accomplice arrives to "mediate" a 1,000-SAR payment for damage that was already there.

The pattern breaks on a single refusal. **Never hand your passport, your documents, or cash to anyone who is not behind an official counter or who will not clearly identify himself.** If someone claims a visa problem, walk to the airport information desk and verify it there. If a "plainclothes officer" wants an on-the-spot fine, decline it, insist on moving to a real police station, and call to verify. Real Ministry of Hajj guides wear an accreditation badge you can ask to see — arrange yours only through your licensed operator or hotel. And politely decline to hold a stranger's camera; offer to use your own phone instead. The unified emergency line in the Mecca and Riyadh regions is **911**; elsewhere the police line is **999**.

## Pattern 3: The Online Booking and Visa Trap

The third pattern never touches you on the street. It arrives by email, WhatsApp, LinkedIn, SMS, or a search result, and it is the most expensive cluster in this book — the scams here can cost thousands of dollars at a stroke, which is why several of them are rated high-severity. Because Saudi Arabia opened so recently and demand for beds, visas, and pilgrimage packages so far outruns supply, the online layer that fraudsters exploit is wide open.

The most painful version targets the faithful. **The Fake Hajj/Umrah Package Operator** in Mecca and Jeddah's **Hajj and Umrah Package Fraud** run polished websites, warm WhatsApp threads, and a £2,000 "deposit to hold your rooms" — then go dark three weeks before departure, leaving fake tickets and a hotel with no reservation in your name. Even a real booking can be hollowed out: **The Umrah Hotel Bait-and-Switch** drives you thirty minutes past the Haram to a worn apartment in Azizia, and Medina's **The Ghost Hotel Booking** promises a two-minute walk to the Prophet's Mosque and delivers a property that does not exist. The phishing variant is sharper still: AlUla's **Booking.com "Confirm Your Card" Phishing Trap** and Al Khobar's **Fake Booking & WhatsApp "Confirm Your Hotel" Fraud** message you with your *real* reservation reference and dates (harvested in a genuine data breach) and push you to a fake payment page before your room is "cancelled." Tabuk gathers the rest: **The Fake NEOM Job Offer** that charged one applicant 15,000 SAR in "processing fees," **The NEOM Vendor-Tender & "NEOM Crypto" Investment Fraud**, and **The Fake Saudi eVisa Site** (saudisevisa.com, visitssaudi.org, and lookalikes) that took £172 and delivered nothing. Abha's **Aseer Season Chalet & Istiraha Booking Fraud**, Dammam's **Haraj Phantom Car Deposit** (3,000 SAR by STC Pay for a car that vanishes), and the **Customs-Fee Delivery Text** that wants a trivial 17-SAR "clearance" before a far larger charge hits — all run the same machine.

The pattern breaks before you ever send money, on three hard rules. **Use only the genuine portals: visa.visitsaudi.com for the tourist eVisa, Nusuk for anything Hajj or Umrah, and careers.neom.com for any NEOM job** — and remember that a real employer never charges you a fee and NEOM has no cryptocurrency, ever. **Never enter card details, an OTP, or a "deposit" from a link in a message;** instead, open the platform's own app, or call the hotel's official number, and verify there. **Pay by credit card on a buyer-protected platform, never by wire transfer, STC Pay, or cash to a private account** — the chargeback is your safety net. Treat any "24 hours or you lose it" deadline as the tell it is, and run any unfamiliar travel or visa site through Trustpilot or Scamadviser first.

## Pattern 4: The Fake-Goods Authentication Trap

The fourth pattern lives at the souvenir stall. Saudi Arabia produces some of the world's most prized luxuries — 21-karat gold, Taif damask-rose oil, wild Sidr honey, Ajwa dates from Medina, aged oud — and every one of them is expensive enough that a convincing fake is hugely profitable against a visitor who cannot tell the difference. The seller swears the gold is solid, the oil is pure, the honey is wild, the dates are premium. Only one of those things is usually true: the price.

You will meet it at the gold souqs first. Jeddah's **Fake Gold and Jewelry at the Gold Souq** sells gold-plated brass as 21-karat for 800 SAR; Dammam's **The Gold Souk Switch** and Al Khobar's **Underweight & Mixed Gold in the Souqs** lean on weight manipulation and missing hallmarks that Arab News has reported on at length. You will meet it at the perfume stall as Taif's **Diluted or Synthetic "Pure Taif Rose Oil"** (200 SAR for what genuine oil sells at 1,000 to 3,000 a tola) and Tabuk's **Fake Oud & Inflated Souvenir Markup**. You will meet it at the honey jar as Taif's **Adulterated "Pure Mountain Honey"** and Abha's **Fake "Pure Asir" Sidr Honey** (genuine Asir Sidr runs SAR 350 to 500 a kilo). And you will meet it at the date bin and the pilgrim souvenir shop as Medina's **The Ajwa Date Mislabeling**, Mecca's **Counterfeit Zamzam Water** and **Ibrahim Al Khalil Souvenir Markup** (SAR 800 prayer beads worth SAR 15 to 25), and Riyadh's **Souk Counterfeit Designer Goods** in the Al Batha and Dira markets.

The pattern breaks on patience and provenance. **Buy nothing valuable from a street vendor, a hotel tout, or an informal back-room seller** — only from a named, licensed shop. **For gold, demand a certified invoice listing karat, weight, and the shop's commercial-registration number, watch it weighed against the day's posted rate, and check the hallmark.** For rose oil, honey, oud, and dates, **taste or smell-test before paying, compare prices across two or three sellers, and treat any "rare" product offered cheap as proof it is fake** — genuine Sidr honey and pure rose oil are never a bargain. Collect Zamzam water free from the official dispensers inside the Haram, and buy ordinary souvenirs at a supermarket like Bin Dawood. Never buy under the emotional pressure of having just completed a ritual.

## Pattern 5: The Commission and Unlicensed-Guide Overcharge

The fifth pattern sits between you and the country's headline sights. Getting into Hegra, up a cable car, into a desert wadi, or onto a Madain Saleh package means going through a person or a window — and that chokepoint is where the markup hides: a third-party reseller charging double for the identical ticket, a "private cabin" fare quoted when a per-head fare would do, an informal 4x4 driver at the end of the road, or a freelance operator padding a package by thousands.

The clearest case is AlUla. **The Lookalike Hegra Ticket Reseller Markup** sells the same coach-based Hegra Day Tour — official price about 95 SAR through experiencealula.com — for 250 to 300 SAR on third-party sites; **The Madain Saleh All-Inclusive Package Triple-Markup** inflates a roughly SR3,000 two-person, two-night trip toward SR6,500 to 7,000; and **The No-Show Rawi and Vanishing Refund** takes 250 euros for a private tour whose guide simply never appears. You will see the cable-car version in Abha as **The Cable Car "Per Head" vs "Whole Cabin" Switch** (a 2-adult, 1-child group quoted SAR 550 when the per-person round trip is SAR 50 to 90) and in Taif's **Al Hada Cable Car** surcharge. You will see the end-of-the-road version in Tabuk as **The Wadi Al Disah Informal 4x4 "End of the Road" Markup**, where a one-hour run opens at 300 SAR. And you will see the hospitality-as-pressure version in Riyadh's **High-Pressure Carpet and Antique Sales**, where coffee and a "museum" tour soften you up for a "rare Bedouin" carpet — usually a Pakistani or Indian import — whose price tumbles from 5,000 to 2,000 SAR with each refusal.

The pattern breaks on doing the booking yourself and pricing it line by line. **Buy AlUla tickets only at tickets.experiencealula.com and benchmark every quote against the ~95 SAR official Hegra Day Tour;** book the hotel directly, demand an itemized breakdown of any package, and walk away from a Madain Saleh bundle near SR6,500. **At a cable car, ask out loud "per person or per whole cabin?" and pay only after the rate for your exact party is stated.** For a wadi 4x4, agree the price *and* the duration before you climb in, and split the cost with other travelers. And enjoy the coffee in any souq without obligation — hospitality is genuine here and never creates a debt to buy.

## Pattern 6: The Deposit and Rental Hostage

The sixth pattern holds something of yours until you pay. Most often it is a rental car: because the best of AlUla, Taif, Abha, and Tabuk is reached by long mountain or desert drives with no public transport, many visitors rent — and the agency holds your deposit, then "discovers" a scratch, a dent, or an "undercarriage" problem on a steep-road-battered car and bills it before you fly out. The Saudi Gazette found that rental offices in Riyadh, Dammam, Al-Khobar, and Jeddah committed more than 2,000 violations and were fined over SR3 million; the Eastern Province in particular has a documented problem with unlicensed, uninsured offices.

You will see it nationwide. Riyadh's **Rental Car Damage Claim Fraud** and Jeddah's **Rental Car Damage and Deposit Hold** charge for a seat stain (one traveler was billed 57 SAR) and sit on a 2,000-SAR deposit for "weeks." AlUla's **Rental-Car Phantom Damage Charge** and Tabuk's **Rental-Car Damage & Deposit Trap** describe a customer chased down an hour after a clean return and told the car had "been in an accident," with comprehensive insurance declared void for lack of a police report. Abha's **Rental-Car Pre-Existing Damage Charge**, Taif's **Rental-Car Damage Charges**, and Al Khobar's **Car-Rental Office Without License or Insurance** complete the set. The same hostage logic drives the **Haraj Phantom Car Deposit** in Dammam, where a "departing expat" wants 3,000 SAR by STC Pay to "hold" a car that does not exist.

The pattern breaks at pickup, not at drop-off. **Before you drive off, film a dated, narrated walk-around video of every panel, wheel, mirror, seat, and the fuel gauge, and get every existing mark written onto the contract.** Rent from a licensed, well-known company with a real office; for off-road or wadi driving, buy explicit off-road cover first. **Pay the deposit by credit card, never debit or cash**, so a withheld deposit or a phantom charge can be reversed by chargeback rather than abandoned. Do a joint walk-around at return, get written confirmation the car is returned without new damage, keep your photos until the deposit clears, check for Saher traffic fines before you leave the country — and dispute any phantom bill through your card, not by paying to make it go away.
