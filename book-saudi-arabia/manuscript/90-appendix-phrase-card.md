# Appendix A: Saudi Arabic Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Saudi Arabia. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, followed by the **Saudi Arabic** in Latin transliteration, and a simple pronunciation guide in italics. Arabic script (right-to-left) is included after the transliteration where it helps; the transliteration is the primary form, because it is what you will actually be reading off your phone in the moment.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. You should also know, going in, that you will rarely *need* the Arabic. English is widely understood in Saudi hotels, malls, airports, and inside the Uber and Careem apps, and a great many vendors, drivers, and front-desk staff speak it comfortably. The phrases below are an insurance policy for the moments when English runs out — a street money-changer, a souq vendor, an off-app taxi driver in Abha or Taif — and for the simple fact that a foreigner who says even five words of Arabic is instantly read as someone paying attention, which is itself the single most effective scam deterrent in this book.

A note on dialect. Saudi Arabia is not one accent. **Hejazi** Arabic is spoken in the western cities — Jeddah, Mecca, Medina, and Taif. **Najdi** Arabic is the dialect of the central region around Riyadh. The eastern cities — Dammam and Al Khobar — lean toward the broader **Gulf** dialect shared with Bahrain and the UAE, and AlUla and Tabuk in the northwest blend Hejazi with northern-tribal speech. The differences are real, but they mostly affect long sentences and slang. The short, functional phrases in this appendix — *how much*, *no thank you*, *that wasn't the deal*, *I want a receipt*, *call the police* — are understood Kingdom-wide, in every city in this book, regardless of which dialect the listener speaks. Where a word changes meaningfully between regions, the note under the phrase says so.

A note on transliteration. Arabic vowels in these spellings are short and crisp — *a* is "ah," *e* is "eh," *i* is "ee," *o* is "oh," *u* is "oo." The combination *kh* is the throaty "ch" sound in the German *Bach* or Scottish *loch*. The combination *gh* is a gargled "r," like a French *r*. An apostrophe (*la'*) marks a glottal stop — a sharp pause, like the catch in the middle of "uh-oh." Two Arabic consonants have no exact English match: the letter ع (*ʿayn*) is a deep-throat sound closest to a strained "ah," and in the simplified spellings below it is left silent or marked with an apostrophe; the letter ح is a breathy *h* deeper in the throat than the English "h," written here as a plain *h*. None of this needs to be perfect. Saudis will forgive a strong foreign accent as long as the key word is clear. *Inshallah* — "God willing" — closes a great many conversational sentences and is a normal, gentle softener; *khalas* — "done / enough / that's it" — is the all-purpose word for closing a conversation and is worth memorizing on its own.

A note on register and respect. Saudi Arabia is a conservative country, and courtesy carries real weight. The default greeting is *as-salaamu alaykum* ("peace be upon you"), answered with *wa alaykum as-salaam*; opening with it, even badly pronounced, visibly softens almost any interaction. Politeness is not weakness here — a calm, firm *la shukran* delivered with a small smile will end more hassle than a raised voice ever will. The phrases below default to the polite register (*min fadlak*, "please"); escalate to the sharper forms only when polite has failed and someone is still in your space.

A note on gender. Arabic conjugates by gender, and Saudi Arabia observes a stronger separation of men's and women's social space than most countries in this series. Where the speaker's gender changes the phrase, both forms are given, with the shorthand `(M)` for a male speaker and `(F)` for a female speaker. "Please" also changes by the *listener's* gender: *min fadlak* to a man, *min fadlik* to a woman. When in doubt, use the form that matches you and you will be understood. A practical note for women travelers: in a dispute, asking to deal with a female staff member — at a hotel, a hospital, a mall information desk — is a completely normal request, and the phrase for it is included below.

## Asking before you commit

**How much?**
*Bikam?*  [Arabic: بكم؟]
*Bee-KAM?*

**How much is this?**
*Hatha bikam?*  [Arabic: هذا بكم؟]
*HAH-tha bee-KAM?*

The single most important question in Saudi Arabia. Ask it before you sit down, before the bags go in the trunk, before the gold leaves the case, before the cable-car cabin doors close. *Bikam* is short, clear, and impossible to mishear, and it is the same word in Hejazi, Najdi, and Gulf speech. The price you are quoted at this moment is the price you have agreed to — anything higher at the end is a scam, not a misunderstanding.

**Please show me the price.**
*Wassif li as-siʿir, min fadlak.*  [Arabic: وصّف لي السعر، من فضلك]
*WAH-sif lee as-SIH-ir, min FAD-lak.*

For souq stalls, perfume and oud sellers, honey and date vendors, and anywhere a quoted price feels improvised. A simpler version that always works is just *as-siʿir?* (*as-SIH-ir?*) — "the price?" A legitimate shop will name a figure without hesitating; a tourist-trap operation stalls and starts asking where you are from.

**Is this for the whole car or per person?**
*Hatha lil-sayyara kulha walla lil-shakhs?*  [Arabic: هذا للسيارة كلها ولا للشخص؟]
*HAH-tha lil-sigh-YAH-rah KUL-hah WAH-lah lil-SHAKHS?*

The pivot inside about half the taxi and cable-car scams in this book. A driver quotes "fifty" and at the destination claims it was fifty *per person*; an Abha cable-car tout quotes a cabin price and then charges per head. Asking this before the door closes — and asking the driver to nod or say it back — shuts the loophole.

**What's included?**
*Esh dakhil fi as-siʿir?*  [Arabic: ايش داخل في السعر؟]
*Esh DAH-khil fee as-SIH-ir?*

For tour packages, chalet and *istiraha* (rest-house) rentals, desert-safari bundles, and any "all-inclusive" price. Force them to itemize. *Esh* is the everyday Saudi word for "what." *Dakhil* (literally "entering") is "included."

**Where is the official ticket office?**
*Wein maktab at-tazaakir ar-rasmi?*  [Arabic: وين مكتب التذاكر الرسمي؟]
*Wayn MAK-tab at-tah-ZAH-kir ar-RAS-mee?*

Critical at AlUla's Hegra and Elephant Rock, the Abha and Taif cable cars, and any site where freelancers resell tickets at a markup. *Wein* is the universal Saudi "where." The word *rasmi* — official — is what distinguishes the real counter (or, for AlUla, the **experiencealula.com** platform) from the man with a clipboard and a markup.

## When you need to refuse politely

**No thank you.**
*La shukran.*  [Arabic: لا شكراً]
*Lah SHOOK-ran.*

Said firmly, kept walking, no elaboration. *La shukran* is understood everywhere in the Kingdom as a complete refusal. Repeating it louder does not strengthen it — repeating it the same way, once, does.

**I'm not interested.**
(F) *Ma abgha.*  [Arabic: ما أبغى]
*Mah AB-ghah.*
(M) *Ma abghi.*  [Arabic: ما أبغي]
*Mah AB-ghee.*

Stronger than *la shukran*. *Abgha / abghi* is the Saudi verb for "I want," and *ma* in front of it makes it "I don't want." Use it when a vendor or a tout keeps talking after the first refusal. (In the eastern Gulf cities you may also hear *ma areed* — *mah ah-REED* — for the same thing; both are understood.)

**Enough — that's it.**
*Khalas.*  [Arabic: خلاص]
*Khah-LAAS.*

The single most useful word in this appendix for closing a conversation. *Khalas* means "done / finished / enough," and said with a flat hand gesture it ends a sales pitch, a haggle, or a tail more cleanly than any full sentence. Pair it with *la shukran* — "*la shukran, khalas*" — and keep walking.

**I already have a guide / a ride.**
(F/M) *ʿEndi murshid khalas.*  [Arabic: عندي مرشد خلاص]
*AN-dee MUR-shid khah-LAAS.*

The best refusal for the unlicensed "pilgrim guide" near the Haram in Mecca, or the self-appointed guide at an AlUla or Abha viewpoint. *ʿEndi* is "I have"; *khalas* closes the door. For a ride, swap *mursid* for *tawseela* (*taw-SEE-lah*, "a lift / ride") — "*ʿendi tawseela khalas*," I already have a ride.

**Please leave me alone.**
(F) *Khallini li-halee, min fadlik.*  [Arabic: خلّيني لحالي، من فضلك]
*Khal-LEE-nee lee-HAH-lee, min FAD-lik.*
(M) *Khallini li-halee, min fadlak.*
*Khal-LEE-nee lee-HAH-lee, min FAD-lak.*

*Khallini li-halee* — "let me be by myself" — is firm without being rude. Remember that *min fadlik* (to a woman) and *min fadlak* (to a man) change by who you are speaking to.

**I don't need anything.**
(F) *Ma ahtaaj shei.*  [Arabic: ما أحتاج شي]
*Mah ah-TAAJ shay.*
(M) *Ma ahtaaj shei.*
*Same form — gender-neutral here.*

Use this against the "let me show you my shop," "free sample," "special price for you" pulls in the souqs of Jeddah's Al-Balad, Riyadh, and the Taif and Abha honey stalls. *Shei* is "anything / something." The phrase shuts the whole script.

## When something feels wrong

**I want a receipt.**
(F) *Abgha fatoora.*  [Arabic: أبغى فاتورة]
*AB-ghah fah-TOO-rah.*
(M) *Abghi fatoora.*  [Arabic: أبغي فاتورة]
*AB-ghee fah-TOO-rah.*

The single most useful escalation phrase in this entire appendix. *Fatoora* is the standard word for an invoice or receipt, and asking for one is a powerful, completely normal move in Saudi Arabia, where electronic invoicing is the legal norm. A legitimate shop, restaurant, or car-rental desk produces one without complaint. A scam operator stalls — and that stall is your signal to stop the transaction and, if needed, call the consumer line **1900** or the police on **999**.

**This is not what we agreed.**
*Hatha mu al-ittifaq.*  [Arabic: هذا مو الاتفاق]
*HAH-tha moo al-it-tee-FAAQ.*

For the taxi driver who "remembers" the price was per person, the seafood restaurant on the Khobar or Jeddah corniche that adds a "sea-view" charge, the car-rental clerk who invents pre-existing damage. *Mu* is the Saudi negation "not" (you will also hear *mush* in the Hejaz); *al-ittifaq* is "the agreement." Said calmly, in front of witnesses, it puts the burden back on the other side.

**Show me the damage.**
*Warrini ad-darar.*  [Arabic: ورّني الضرر]
*War-REE-nee ad-dah-RAR.*

Specific to the rental-car damage-claim scam documented in Riyadh, Taif, Tabuk, AlUla, and Al Khobar. *Warrini* is "show me"; *ad-darar* is "the damage." Pair it with your own time-stamped photos and videos of the car taken at pickup — the single best defense against a fabricated dent or scratch charge.

**I'll wait for the police.**
*Bantadhir ash-shurta.*  [Arabic: بانتظر الشرطة]
*Ban-TAH-dhir ash-SHUR-tah.*

The atmosphere-changer. Use it when a scam is in progress and the other party has not backed off. *Ash-shurta* (the police) is understood everywhere. Most opportunistic scams dissolve the moment a foreigner says this credibly and reaches for a phone — particularly because penalties for cheating tourists are taken seriously in the Kingdom.

**Do not touch me.**
*La tilmisni!*  [Arabic: لا تلمسني]
*Lah TIL-mis-nee!*

The disengagement phrase for a pickpocket or a too-close "helper" in the dense prayer crowds around the Masjid al-Haram in Mecca or the Prophet's Mosque in Medina, or in the press of Jeddah's Al-Balad. Said sharp, with a step back and a raised hand. A woman who needs to draw attention from a man getting too close can also say *ibʿid ʿanni* (*IB-id AN-nee*) — "get away from me."

**Where is the police station?**
*Wein markaz ash-shurta?*  [Arabic: وين مركز الشرطة؟]
*Wayn MAR-kaz ash-SHUR-tah?*

*Markaz ash-shurta* is the police station. In the holy mosques and their courtyards, security and police are heavily present; in the souqs and on the corniches, any mall security desk or hotel front desk can point you to the nearest station or call it for you.

**I'm calling my embassy.**
(F/M) *Bittasil bil-sifaara hag baladi.*  [Arabic: بتّصل بالسفارة حق بلدي]
*Bit-TAH-sil bil-sif-FAH-rah hag BAH-lah-dee.*

For disputes that escalate at counters and checkpoints. *As-sifaara* is the embassy; *hag baladi* — "of my country" — is the Gulf-flavored possessive. Mentioning the embassy raises the stakes for anyone improvising and signals there will be a formal record.

## Calling for help

**Help!**
*Saʿiduuni!*  [Arabic: ساعدوني]
*Sah-ʿee-DOO-nee!*

The shouted call for help — literally "help me [all of you]." Every Saudi adult understands it as "I am in trouble, intervene." In the crowds around the two holy mosques, in a mall, or on a corniche, it draws attention and bystanders fast.

**Thief!**
*Haraami!*  [Arabic: حرامي]
*Hah-RAH-mee!*

Shouted at a pickpocket fleeing the scene — relevant to the tawaf-crowd pickpocket rings in Mecca, the prayer-crowd thefts in Medina, and Al-Balad in Jeddah. Pair with *Saʿiduuni!* for maximum effect.

**Call the police.**
(F) *Ittasili bil-shurta.*  [Arabic: اتصلي بالشرطة]
*It-TAH-sih-lee bil-SHUR-tah.*
(M speaking to anyone) *Ittasil bil-shurta.*  [Arabic: اتصل بالشرطة]
*It-TAH-sil bil-SHUR-tah.*

Said to a bystander, a shopkeeper, a security guard, or a hotel clerk. The number to dial is **999** (police) anywhere in the Kingdom — and **911** also works in the Mecca and Riyadh regions. Both are free from any phone.

**Call an ambulance.**
(F) *Ittasili bil-isʿaaf.*  [Arabic: اتصلي بالإسعاف]
*It-TAH-sih-lee bil-is-ʿAAF.*
(M) *Ittasil bil-isʿaaf.*
*It-TAH-sil bil-is-ʿAAF.*

The ambulance / Red Crescent number is **997**. *Al-isʿaaf* is the standard word for ambulance and emergency medical service. (In the Mecca and Riyadh regions, **911** reaches the same dispatch.)

**I need a doctor.**
(F) *Ahtaaj duktoor.*  [Arabic: أحتاج دكتور]
*Ah-TAAJ duk-TOOR.*
(M) *Ahtaaj duktoor.*
*Same form — gender-neutral here.*

For anything short of an emergency. Hotel front desks routinely help guests find an English-speaking doctor; saying this to a concierge produces a referral within minutes. A woman who would prefer a female doctor can add *doktoora* (*duk-TOO-rah*), the feminine form — a completely ordinary request in Saudi hospitals.

**Is there a woman I can speak to?**
*Fee wahda agdar akallimha?*  [Arabic: في وحدة أقدر أكلّمها؟]
*Fee WAH-dah AG-dar ah-kal-LIM-hah?*

A practical, respectful phrase for women travelers who would rather handle a dispute, a medical issue, or a search with a female staff member. *Fee wahda* is "is there a woman"; this is a normal and well-understood request at hotels, hospitals, airports, and mall service desks.

## Money and money-changing

**I want a receipt.**
(F) *Abgha fatoora.*  [Arabic: أبغى فاتورة]
*AB-ghah fah-TOO-rah.*
(M) *Abghi fatoora.*
*AB-ghee fah-TOO-rah.*

Repeated from the previous section because it is the single most important phrase at any money-changer, gold shop, or rental desk. Licensed Saudi exchange houses — Al Rajhi, STC Pay outlets, the big mall *sarraf* counters — issue printed receipts as routine. The absence of one, or a "broken printer," at a street-corner changer is a scam signal, not a coincidence.

**What's the rate?**
*Esh siʿir at-tahweel?*  [Arabic: ايش سعر التحويل؟]
*Esh SIH-ir at-tah-WEEL?*

For money-changers. The cleaner phrase is *siʿir at-tahweel* (*SIH-ir at-tah-WEEL*) — "the exchange rate." Compare it to the rate on Google or the XE app before you change anything, and strongly prefer a licensed exchange house or simply your own card; the riyal is pegged at roughly 3.75 to the US dollar, so a quote far off that is a red flag.

**Count it again, please.**
*ʿiddha marra thaaniya, min fadlak.*  [Arabic: عدّها مرة ثانية، من فضلك]
*ID-hah MAR-rah THAH-nee-yah, min FAD-lak.*

The defensive move against the short-count documented at street changers and souvenir stalls in Riyadh, Dammam, and Medina. *ʿiddha* is "count it"; *marra thaaniya* is "a second time." Saudi banknotes (5, 10, 50, 100, 200, 500 riyals) are distinct in color, but a fast count of similar-sized notes is where the gap opens. Count it back, in front of the cashier, before you step away.

**Use the app price.**
*Bidfaʿ siʿir at-tatbeeq.*  [Arabic: بدفع سعر التطبيق]
*BID-fah SIH-ir at-tat-BEEQ.*

For the off-app taxi driver who waves you over and names a cash figure. *At-tatbeeq* is "the app." Saying you will pay the in-app price — and then booking an Uber or Careem on your phone in front of him — ends the negotiation, because the metered app price is set and visible.

## Taxi and transport

**I'm using Uber / Careem.**
(F/M) *Ana bastakhdim Uber.*  [Arabic: أنا بستخدم أوبر]
*AH-nah bas-TAKH-dim OO-ber.*

Replace *Uber* with *Careem* as needed — both apps work across Riyadh, Jeddah, Mecca, Medina, Dammam, Al Khobar, Taif, and (with thinner coverage) Abha, Tabuk, and AlUla. This is the single best defense against every taxi scam in this book. A driver who has flagged you down at the curb cannot compete with the fixed in-app fare, and saying this closes the conversation. In Mecca and Medina, note that app pickups have designated zones outside the central holy areas; let the app route you.

**Use the meter, please.**
*Shaghghil al-ʿaddaad, min fadlak.*  [Arabic: شغّل العدّاد، من فضلك]
*SHAGH-ghil al-ad-DAAD, min FAD-lak.*

For a street taxi when no app car is available — common around Abha's airport and mountain roads and parts of Taif. *Al-ʿaddaad* is the meter. This is mostly diagnostic: if the driver refuses the meter and will not name a fair fixed price up front, close the door and wait for an app car instead.

**Stop here, please.**
*Waggif hina, min fadlak.*  [Arabic: وقّف هنا، من فضلك]
*WAG-gif HEE-nah, min FAD-lak.*

For a ride going off-route or stretching the fare. *Waggif* is "stop"; *hina* is "here." Said calmly, it is the standard way to end a ride mid-trip. If a driver refuses to stop, the situation has escalated past haggling and you should be on the phone to **999**.

**I'll pay when we arrive.**
*Bidfaʿ lamma nuusal.*  [Arabic: بدفع لما نوصل]
*BID-fah LAM-mah NOO-sal.*

The answer to a driver who demands payment up front and then renegotiates during the ride. *Lamma nuusal* is "when we arrive." The agreed price is paid once, at the destination — and the apps remove this whole negotiation, which is why they are the better default.

**The price is too high.**
*As-siʿir ghaali marra.*  [Arabic: السعر غالي مرّة]
*As-SIH-ir GHAH-lee MAR-rah.*

The polite haggling opener at souqs and with street taxis. *Ghaali* is "expensive"; *marra* here means "very / really." This opens a real negotiation rather than a flat refusal — useful for souvenirs, oud, honey, and dates, where some bargaining is expected.

## Useful at sites and on pilgrimage

**I have my own ticket.**
*ʿEndi tazkara.*  [Arabic: عندي تذكرة]
*AN-dee taz-KAH-rah.*

For the freelance reseller at AlUla's Hegra or a cable-car queue who offers to "help" you buy a ticket at a markup. *ʿEndi tazkara* — "I have a ticket" — closes the door. AlUla's only official seller is **experiencealula.com**; buy there, on your phone, before you reach the site.

**I don't need a guide.**
(F) *Ma ahtaaj mursid.*  [Arabic: ما أحتاج مرشد]
*Mah ah-TAAJ MUR-shid.*
(M) *Ma ahtaaj mursid.*
*Same form — gender-neutral here.*

Said firmly to the unlicensed "guides" who attach themselves at viewpoints and near the holy sites. If you want a guide, book a licensed one through your hotel or the official site platform, never on the street.

**I won't pay extra.**
*Ma bidfaʿ ziyaada.*  [Arabic: ما بدفع زيادة]
*Mah BID-fah zee-YAH-dah.*

For the "tip" demanded after a service is complete, or a surprise add-on at a revolving restaurant or cable car. *Ziyaada* is "extra / increase." Said calmly, it draws a clear line.

**A respectful word at the holy mosques.** In Mecca and Medina, you are a guest in the two holiest sites in Islam, and the overwhelming majority of people around you are sincere pilgrims, not scammers. The handful of scams documented there — pickpocket rings in the tawaf crowd, unlicensed "guides," fake Zamzam water, the "sob story" beggar — are best handled quietly. A soft *la shukran* or *khalas*, a hand on your zipped bag, and a short walk toward a uniformed security officer resolves nearly all of it without a scene. Raising your voice or arguing loudly inside or just outside the mosques is both unnecessary and out of place. If something is genuinely wrong, the security presence in and around the Haram and the Prophet's Mosque is dense, professional, and used to helping foreign visitors — walk to the nearest officer rather than confronting the other person yourself.

## Reading Saudi riyal amounts

The Saudi riyal (SAR, *riyaal*, ريال) divides into 100 *halalas*, though prices for tourists are almost always in whole riyals. Banknotes come in 5, 10, 50, 100, 200, and 500 riyals, each a distinct color and clearly numbered in both Western (1 2 3) and Eastern Arabic (١ ٢ ٣) digits. The riyal is pegged at about **3.75 to the US dollar**, which makes mental math easy: divide a riyal figure by roughly four for a quick dollar estimate (100 SAR ≈ $27). Card and Apple Pay / mada are accepted almost everywhere, so you will carry far less cash than in many countries in this series — which itself removes most short-count opportunities.

| Amount | Spoken | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 SAR | riyaal | *ree-YAAL* |
| 5 SAR | khamsa riyaal | *KHAM-sah ree-YAAL* |
| 10 SAR | ʿashara riyaal | *ah-SHAH-rah ree-YAAL* |
| 20 SAR | ʿishreen riyaal | *ish-REEN ree-YAAL* |
| 50 SAR | khamseen riyaal | *kham-SEEN ree-YAAL* |
| 100 SAR | miyya riyaal | *MEE-yah ree-YAAL* |
| 200 SAR | miyyatein riyaal | *mee-yah-TAYN ree-YAAL* |
| 500 SAR | khamsumiyya riyaal | *kham-soo-MEE-yah ree-YAAL* |
| 1,000 SAR | alf riyaal | *ALF ree-YAAL* |

If a vendor or driver names a figure you cannot follow, ask them to write it down or show it on a calculator:

**Please write the number.**
*Iktib ar-raqam, min fadlak.*  [Arabic: اكتب الرقم، من فضلك]
*IK-tib ar-RAH-qam, min FAD-lak.*

In practice, most Saudi vendors will simply turn a phone calculator toward you — accept that, and you remove the entire "I said a different number" dispute.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive — and in Saudi Arabia that is not just good manners, it is the more effective strategy. A calm *la shukran* is already a complete refusal; *khalas* closes a conversation; *hatha mu al-ittifaq* ("that wasn't the deal") puts a disputed charge back on the other party without anyone raising their voice. Save the sharper phrases — *la tilmisni*, *bantadhir ash-shurta* — for the rare moment when polite has failed and someone is still in your space.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this, and in Saudi Arabia you will usually have English to fall back on. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Open with *as-salaamu alaykum*, close with *khalas* or *shukran*, and you will be read as a courteous visitor who is also paying attention — which, in the end, is the single most reliable way to never become the subject of a chapter in a book like this one.
