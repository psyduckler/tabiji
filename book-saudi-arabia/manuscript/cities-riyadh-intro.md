Riyadh is the Kingdom's capital and its scam profile is the most conventionally urban in this book: six documented scams, all tourist-trap variants, weighted toward the airport, the rental counter, and the old market district rather than any pilgrimage crowd. Two of the six are transport scams that bracket every arrival. The Broken Meter Taxi Overcharge quotes 150 SAR for a ride that should cost 50 to 70, claiming the meter is broken or padding the route through Riyadh's sprawling suburbs. The Fake Taxi at Airport Interception is its more aggressive twin — an unmarked sedan, a casual-clothed driver who loads your bags before any price is agreed, then a 250 SAR demand that is four times the Careem rate. The Al Batha market district anchors three more: Souk Counterfeit Designer Goods passes off fake handbags and watches as "first quality, same factory" at 500 SAR (and counterfeits can be seized at your home customs), High-Pressure Carpet and Antique Sales uses Arabic coffee and a sliding price to move mass-produced rugs as rare Bedouin pieces, and the Street Money Changer Short Change uses fast, distracting counting to hand back 500 SAR where 750 was owed. The Rental Car Damage Claim Fraud completes the set — a phantom scratch, a 200-to-500 SAR "administrative fee," and deposits that take over a month to return, if ever.

The risk is concentrated in three zones. The first is King Khalid International Airport arrivals, where both the broken-meter and fake-taxi pitches start, alongside the airport rental counters. The second is the historic market belt in southern and central Riyadh: the Al Batha market district, Dira Souq around the Chop Chop Square area, Al Owais Market, and the antique shops near Masmak Fortress and the Murabba historical area — this single corridor concentrates the counterfeit goods, the high-pressure carpet pitch, and the informal money changers all at once. The third is more diffuse: outside major hotels, shopping malls, and Riyadh Railway Station, where street taxis intercept visitors and unlicensed rental offices operate across the city. Al Batha, a budget district busy with workers and budget travelers, carries the densest cluster of informal money changers and the highest short-change risk.

A safe default for first-timers: order Careem or Uber from inside the terminal and walk only to the designated pickup zone — both show the fare and route upfront, both run reliably across Riyadh, and a glance at the estimate is your reference price against any street taxi. For money, skip the informal changers entirely and use ATMs at major banks like Al Rajhi, Saudi National Bank, or Riyad Bank; Saudi Arabia is increasingly cashless, so card and contactless payment cover almost everything anyway. The two habits that defeat most of the rest are to never let anyone load your luggage or take your cash before a price and plate are confirmed, and to film a timestamped walkaround of any rental car — every panel, wheel, mirror, and seat — before driving off and again at return, so a phantom-damage claim has nothing to stand on.
