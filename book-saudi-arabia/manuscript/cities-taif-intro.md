Taif is the Kingdom's mountain retreat — the cool, high "City of Roses" above Mecca — and its scam profile is the most distinctive in this book, built around the things people actually come to buy and see on the heights. Six documented scams, all tourist-trap variants, and two of them turn Taif's signature products against the visitor. Diluted or Synthetic "Pure Taif Rose Oil" and Attar is the defining one: a vendor waves a vial and names 200 riyals, but genuine pure oil runs roughly SAR 1,000 to 3,000 per tola because some 40,000 hand-picked damask roses go into each one — anything dramatically cheaper is cut, blended, or synthetic. Its twin is Adulterated "Pure Mountain Honey" from Roadside and Souq Vendors, where cheap rebottled syrup is sold as wild Sidr in a country whose honey output has collapsed against soaring demand. The one high-severity scam is the most physical: Baboon-Feeding Fruit Stalls on the Al Hada and Al Shafa Roads mark up a bunch of bananas for the captive tourist audience, and the feeding sets visitors up for the real danger of close contact with wild hamadryas baboons. The remaining three are Saudi-wide patterns that land squarely on Taif: the Al Hada Cable Car and Mountain-Taxi Surcharge (a "broken" meter and a flat fare two or three times what the trip should cost), the Airport and Street Taxi Overcharge from Jeddah and Within Taif (the most common Saudi scam there is, aimed at pilgrims combining Makkah with a Taif side-trip), and Rental-Car Damage Charges and the Camera-Drop Setup.

The risk is concentrated along the mountain roads and in the old town. The Al Hada and Al Shafa highland roads are the sharpest zone: the roadside fruit-and-snack stalls where the baboons gather at the crash barriers, the perfume and honey stalls along the route, and the Telefric Al Hada / Al Kar cable car at the Ramada Al Hada, where the overpriced mountain-taxi run ends. Downtown, the old souq and the perfume stalls near Souq Okaz concentrate the rose-oil and honey fakes, alongside the in-city taxi overcharge around the hotels. The transport scams reach all the way back to Jeddah: King Abdulaziz International Airport arrivals and the Makkah- and Jeddah-to-Taif road are where touts intercept visitors before the official rank, sometimes posing as staff and asking to see a passport before walking you to an unlicensed car. Rental-car damage claims attach at the airport drive-up and at scenic stops like Shubra Palace, the old souq, and the Al Hada viewpoints.

A safe default for first-timers: use Careem or Uber wherever they reach so the fare shows before you confirm, ignore any tout who approaches before the official taxi rank, and know the benchmarks — roughly SAR 200 to 300 for a sedan from Makkah or Jeddah, modest fares in town, and the official ~SAR 84 cable-car ticket bought only at the Telefric window. The habit that defeats the product scams is to refuse the bargain: buy rose oil only from a named farm or factory shop with a sealed bottle labeled Rosa damascena, expecting SAR 1,000 to 3,000 per tola, and buy honey only from licensed shops with labeled, dated jars — if you want a cheap keepsake, take home clearly labeled rosewater rather than "pure oil." On the mountain, the safest move is the simplest: do not buy or hand out "monkey food," keep the windows up and the doors shut as you pass the baboon troops, and never let children offer food to the animals.
