# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are ordered by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

A reassuring word before the steps. Saudi Arabia is, by international standards, a low-crime country with a strong, well-resourced state, and it takes the cheating of visitors seriously. Most of the scams in this book are commercial — an inflated taxi fare, a fabricated rental-car dent, an overweight gold sale, a fake hotel booking — rather than violent, and the Kingdom's consumer-protection and policing systems are genuinely responsive when you give them a clear record. English is widely spoken at hotels, hospitals, and the tourist-police level, and the reporting tools are largely app-based and modern. That works in your favor at every step below.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the situation. If you are physically inside the place where you were scammed — a souq shop, a parked taxi, a car-rental office, a "sea-view" restaurant on the corniche — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment to make the problem go away. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet and public: a mall, a hotel lobby, a coffee shop.

Do not sign anything you do not understand, including credit-card slips, car-rental "damage acknowledgements," and hand-written receipts. A signed document is far harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff physically block your exit or refuse to return your passport or card, that is a crime, and the correct response is to dial **999** (police; **911** also works in the Mecca and Riyadh regions) and stay on the line. Saudi emergency operators in the major cities handle English; if the dispatcher does not, say *"English, please"* clearly and wait, or ask a bystander or the nearest security guard to translate.

Screenshot and photograph everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill or quote, the shopfront and its name sign, the car and any disputed "damage" from several angles, the meter, the card terminal, the price card or menu, and — for a rental car — a wide shot showing the whole vehicle with a timestamp. The evidence you capture in these fifteen minutes is what every later step depends on. If you feel you are being followed or pressured, walk into any hotel lobby (a four-star or above will let you stand inside for a few minutes) before pulling out your phone.

If your bag, wallet, or phone was just taken — most likely in the prayer crowds around the Masjid al-Haram in Mecca or the Prophet's Mosque in Medina, or in Jeddah's Al-Balad — shout *"Haraami!"* (*"Thief!"*). Bystanders respond, and the security presence in and around the holy sites is dense and quick. Walk to the nearest uniformed officer rather than chasing anyone yourself.

## Within the first hour

Make the police report. For anything involving theft, a held passport, physical intimidation, or a significant amount of money, file with the police. There are three routes, and you can use whichever is fastest where you are:

- **Call 999** (or **911** in the Mecca and Riyadh regions) and ask for an officer. In the holy mosques, at airports, and in malls, there is usually an officer or security desk within sight.
- **The Kollona Amn app** ("We Are All Security," كلنا أمن), the Ministry of Interior's official reporting app, lets you file certain reports and reach police services from your phone. It is the modern front door to non-emergency police reporting.
- **Absher** (أبشر), the government services app many visitors already use for visa and entry services, is the hub that ties your identity to official services and is worth having installed before you travel.

Ask for a copy of the report or its reference number. You will need that reference for every later step — the card chargeback, the travel-insurance claim, the embassy if your passport was taken. You do not need fluent Arabic; in the major cities the police are used to assisting foreign visitors, and your hotel can call on your behalf and send a staff member with you if needed.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are in Appendix C; they work from Saudi Arabia and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready. If the scam was a restaurant overcharge, a taxi rip-off, a souq overcharge, or a fabricated rental-car charge run on your card, also call your own bank's fraud line — the number on the back of your card — because the issuer's fraud desk often freezes faster than the network lines and can flag a merchant directly.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File the consumer / commercial complaint. This is the step that is specific to Saudi Arabia and genuinely effective for the commercial scams that make up most of this book — overcharging, fake or adulterated goods (counterfeit gold, fake "pure" Taif rose oil or mountain honey, mislabeled Ajwa dates), refused receipts, and rental-car or booking disputes. Two channels matter:

- **The Ministry of Commerce hotline, 1900.** This is the national consumer-protection and commercial-fraud line. Operators can take a complaint against a named business and, in many cases, English-language support is available.
- **The "Balagh Tijari" commercial-complaint channel** (بلاغ تجاري, "commercial report"), run by the Ministry of Commerce and reachable through the Ministry's app and website. This is the right tool for reporting a shop that overcharged you, sold counterfeit or mislabeled goods, refused a receipt, or violated posted pricing. A complaint here creates an official commercial record against the business — which is what drives enforcement against repeat offenders and, in some cases, recovers your money.

When you file, give the business's full name and location (a Google Maps pin is ideal), the exact amount, the date and time, and your photos. The more specific the record, the more useful it is.

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the police reference or the commercial-complaint reference as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a markedly higher success rate than those filed a week later, and Visa and Mastercard rules give you strong rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges. This is the main reason this book recommends paying every formal charge — hotel, car rental, tour, tickets — by credit card rather than cash. The most common card-based scam in the Kingdom is the rental-car "damage" charge run on a deposit hold; your time-stamped pickup photos plus a chargeback are the way to fight it.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone — a hotel that copied it at check-in and then declined to return it without payment of an inflated bill, a "rental office" that kept it as collateral, anyone posing as an official — notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can resurface in fraud attempts later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. The US Embassy in Riyadh (+966-11-488-3800) handles US-citizen passport services, with consulates in Jeddah and Dhahran; other embassies are listed in Appendix C.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary, timestamps, the police or complaint reference, and the photographs. Even if you are unsure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position.

## Within the first week

Escalate any online or booking-platform fraud. Several scams in this book originate online before you ever land — the fake Saudi eVisa site, the "ghost hotel" booking, the fake AlUla / Hegra ticket reseller, the WhatsApp "confirm your hotel" message, the fake NEOM job or vendor-tender pitch in Tabuk. For these:

- Open a dispute on the legitimate platform if one was involved (Booking.com, Airbnb, GetYourGuide, Viator) as well as with your card issuer — platforms can sometimes recover deposits the card company cannot.
- For AlUla specifically, the only official ticket seller is the Royal Commission's **experiencealula.com**; report a lookalike reseller through that platform and the Ministry of Commerce.
- For a fraudulent investment or recruitment pitch (the NEOM "crypto" or job-fee scams), the relevant authority is the police and, for investment fraud, Saudi Arabia's Capital Market Authority — but at the visitor level, a police report plus your bank's fraud team is the practical first move.

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with everything gathered so far: the business name and location, the exact transaction details, the police or commercial-complaint reference, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts, and the date, without emotional language or accusations, and identify the business by its official name and location only — not individual staff. Reviews from recent visitors are one of the most reliable ways future travelers avoid the same operator, and several scams in this book (the AlUla reseller markup, the corniche "sea-view" overcharge, specific car-rental offices) are exactly the kind that a clear review helps the next person dodge.

## If the scam involved physical harm or you feel unsafe

Your health comes first. For any injury or medical emergency, dial **997** for an ambulance (the Saudi Red Crescent), or **911** in the Mecca and Riyadh regions, and say *"English, please."* The major Saudi cities have excellent, modern hospitals with English-speaking staff; specific options are listed by city in Appendix C, and your hotel front desk can call one and arrange transport.

Saudi Arabia is a low-violence environment for tourists, and the scams in this book are overwhelmingly financial rather than physical. But if you are ever intimidated, confined, or threatened — including a driver who will not stop the car or return your belongings — treat it as a police matter, not a haggle: call **999** (or **911** in the Mecca and Riyadh regions) immediately and stay on the line. Women travelers who feel unsafe can ask any hotel, hospital, mall, or airport for a female staff member to assist (the phrase is in Appendix A) — a normal and well-understood request in the Kingdom.

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, a replacement. Consular registration before you travel (STEP for US citizens, the FCDO's Travel Aware guidance for UK citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, and the equivalents for other nationalities) is worth doing precisely for situations like these — it is the difference between your embassy knowing you are in the country and not.
