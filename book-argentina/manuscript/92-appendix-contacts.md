# Appendix C: Emergency Contacts {-}

Every phone number in this appendix was verified current at the time of publication, but phone numbers do change, consulates move, and hospitals occasionally renumber their switchboards. If a number below fails to connect, search the organization's name plus *Argentina* or the city name, and you will almost always find the current line on the first page of results.

**Before you travel**, we strongly recommend you double-check your own embassy or consulate's current number directly on its official website — the US State Department's travel.state.gov, the UK's gov.uk, Canada's travel.gc.ca, Ireland's dfa.ie, Australia's smartraveller.gov.au. Save the main switchboard and the after-hours duty-officer line on your phone before departure, and screenshot this appendix so you can read it in a *colectivo* or a ferry where your signal cuts out. Even a verified number can become a wrong number between editions.

## The numbers that work everywhere in Argentina

These are the lines to dial from any phone — mobile or landline, Argentine SIM or roaming — anywhere in the country. All of them are free.

- **Unified emergency (all services):** **911**. Dispatchers speak basic English in Buenos Aires and most provincial capitals, Spanish elsewhere. This is the single number to call if you are not sure which service you need; the operator will route you to police, ambulance, or fire as appropriate. **911** works on any mobile phone in Argentina, even without a SIM and even on a locked screen.
- **Policía Federal Argentina:** **134**. The federal police, with national jurisdiction over crimes that cross provincial lines.
- **Policía de la Ciudad (Buenos Aires):** **911** or **147**. Also handles municipal tourist issues; tourists should call **147** (the CABA citizen-services line) for non-emergency reports and English-language help during office hours.
- **Gendarmería Nacional:** **911**. Covers border crossings, highways, and rural zones; also handles Iguazú and Patagonia border corridors.
- **Prefectura Naval Argentina:** at ports and at the border rivers (Paraná, Uruguay). For Tigre, Iguazú, and the Beagle Channel incidents, dial **106**.
- **Ambulance (SAME):** **107** in Buenos Aires; **911** routes the same dispatch provincially. English-speaking operators in CABA.
- **Fire (Bomberos):** **100**.
- **Consumer Affairs (Defensa del Consumidor) nationwide:** **0800-666-1518** (national hotline); each province also has its own line.
- **Toxicology hotline (envenenamientos):** **0800-444-8694**.
- **Central Bank (BCRA) financial-fraud complaint:** **0800-999-6663**.

## If you lose your passport

1. **Report it first** — go to the nearest comisaría and file a *denuncia* for a lost or stolen passport. You will leave with the signed and sealed report. This is the document your embassy wants to see before issuing an emergency replacement.
2. **Call your embassy's main switchboard** — or its after-hours line if it is a weekend — and request an emergency passport appointment. US, UK, Canadian, Irish, and Australian embassies in Buenos Aires all issue same-day or next-day emergency travel documents; the fee varies.
3. **Bring** the *denuncia*, any photo ID you still have (a driver's license photo on your phone counts), two passport photos (photo booths are common in Buenos Aires Subte stations and in the basement of Galerías Pacífico), and the payment method your embassy specifies.
4. **Border-control clearance for departure**: before flying out with an emergency travel document, airlines departing Ezeiza and Aeroparque see emergency documents regularly; border police know the format. For neighboring-country departures (Uruguay, Brazil, Chile via bus), your embassy will advise on any additional step.

## If your phone is stolen

1. **Freeze the SIM** by calling your carrier's customer-service line from any other phone. Argentine carriers: **Movistar** \*611; **Personal** \*111; **Claro** \*611. If you use a foreign SIM, call your home carrier's international line — most have a 24/7 theft-reporting number that forwards from Argentina. Tourist eSIMs (Airalo, Saily, Holafly) can be suspended in-app.
2. **File a *denuncia*** at the nearest comisaría with your device's IMEI (if you recorded it) and its serial number. Insurance claims require the sealed report.
3. **Lock and wipe remotely** via Apple's Find My or Google's Find My Device from any browser. Do this before filing the *denuncia* — a wiped phone cannot leak your email and banking apps while you wait in line.
4. **Change passwords immediately** for any account whose app was installed on the phone: email, banking, social media. Start with email; everything else resets off it.

## If your credit card is stolen or skimmed

1. **Freeze the card** by calling the number on the back (or use the issuer's app's "lock card" feature, which works in seconds). This matters more than filing a police report first — every minute you wait is a minute the thief can use the card.
2. **Report to police** with a *denuncia*. Insurance reimbursement and the card issuer's fraud-dispute process both want the sealed report.
3. **Dispute the charges** with your issuer. Most US, European, Australian, and Japanese card issuers reverse fraudulent charges fully; the *denuncia* speeds the process.
4. **Watch for follow-on phishing** — a skimmed card often leads to email and SMS phishing pretending to be your bank. Never click a link in one of these messages; go to your bank's app or website directly.

## A closing note

This appendix is meant to be read once on the plane, screenshot for offline use, and forgotten about for the rest of your trip. Phone numbers change. Consulates move. Hospitals renumber their switchboards. If any number in this list fails, **911** — the unified Argentine emergency line — will still work, and your embassy's website will still have the current line for your country.

Travel safely, travel calmly, and if the worst happens, remember: a stolen phone is an inconvenience, not a crisis. Filing a *denuncia*, freezing a card, and calling an embassy are ordinary procedures — Policía Federal officers and consular staff in Argentina handle these cases every day, and most travelers resolve everything within a few hours and return to their trip. Argentina is not a dangerous country for tourists; it is a practiced one. Help is close, and English is closer than you think.
