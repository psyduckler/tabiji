Tigre is the Paraná Delta's gateway town — thirty kilometers northwest of Buenos Aires by the Mitre railway line, a forty-minute journey from Retiro station. The town proper is small (roughly 380,000 in the greater metropolitan ring, but a much smaller downtown core), anchored by the Estación Fluvial (the riverfront boat terminal), Paseo Victorica (the restaurant promenade along the Río Luján), the Puerto de Frutos artisan market, and — on summer weekends — an enormous influx of porteño day-trippers heading out to the delta's island cabañas and wooden lanchas.

Two things define Tigre's scam ecosystem. First, the **Estación Fluvial unlicensed lanchero**: dozens of individuals offering "private Delta tours" via clipboards at the terminal entrance or WhatsApp bookings from Instagram. Many of these operators are unregistered, uninsured, and run non-GPS-tracked boats into delta channels where cell service fails. The municipal fiscalización (enforcement) is public about the problem and has been issuing citations since 2019; book only through the five regulated operators whose booths line the terminal concourse (Sturla Viajes, Cacciola, Interislas, Catamaranes Turísticos, Río Tur).

Second, the **Paseo Victorica restaurant bill-padding** pattern: cubiertos running ARS 5,000–8,000 per person unlisted on the menu; English-menu prices marked 40 % above the Spanish menu at the bar; and bread ("panera") arriving at the table and being billed at ARS 4,000–6,000 as if ordered. This pattern mirrors what happens at Av. Maipú in Ushuaia and Av. del Libertador in El Calafate; the defensive habit is the same.

Beyond those two clusters, Tigre is Buenos Aires's safest day-trip destination. The Mitre train is reliable and comfortable; the Puerto de Frutos market is a pleasant afternoon even for bargain-hunters; and the wooden-lancha commute between islands — done via the regulated operators — is one of the country's most charming experiences. Most first-time visitors leave Tigre with good memories.
