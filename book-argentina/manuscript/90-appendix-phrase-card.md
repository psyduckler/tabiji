# Appendix A: Rioplatense Spanish Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Argentina. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, followed by the **Spanish** text and a simple pronunciation guide in italics.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. Say the first three or four out loud a few times. You will feel much less awkward using them when the moment comes.

## A note on pronunciation

Argentine Spanish — especially the *Rioplatense* variety spoken in Buenos Aires, Rosario, Mendoza, and the Paraná basin — is unmistakable. It has three signatures that distinguish it from peninsular Spanish and from Mexican or Andean Spanish.

First, the **double-L (ll)** and the **y** are pronounced as a soft "zh" (like the "s" in *measure*) or sometimes a sharper "sh." *Calle* ("street") is pronounced "KAH-zheh" or "KAH-sheh," never "KAH-yeh." *Yo* ("I") is "zho." *Pollo* ("chicken") is "POH-zhoh."

Second, the **s at the end of a syllable** is often aspirated or dropped. *Mas o menos* sounds like "mah-oh-MEH-noh." *Dos* ("two") can sound like "doh." In Córdoba and Salta this softens further; in Buenos Aires it stays crisp enough to catch.

Third, Argentines use ***vos*** instead of ***tú*** ("you"), with its own verb conjugation. Where a Mexican says "*tú tienes*," a porteño says "*vos tenés*." Every verb in the second-person singular shifts accordingly: *hablás* not *hablas*, *sabés* not *sabes*, *tenés* not *tienes*. For tourist purposes you do not need to master *voseo* — the *tú* forms are also understood everywhere in Argentina — but hearing *vos* and not thinking a stranger is being ungrammatical is useful.

A few pointers that will get you 80 percent of the way:

- Argentine Spanish vowels are clean and short: *a* is "ah," *e* is "eh," *i* is "ee," *o* is "oh," *u* is "oo." Never a schwa.
- The letter *h* is silent — *hola* is "OH-la," never "HO-la."
- The letter *j* (and *g* before *e* or *i*) is a throaty English "h" — *jamón* is "ha-MOHN," *Gijón* is "hee-HOHN."
- The letter *ñ* is "ny" — *mañana* is "ma-NYAH-na."
- The letters *ll* and the letter *y* in Argentina are pronounced "zh" or "sh" — *llamo* is "ZHAH-moh," *playa* is "PLAH-zhah." This is the **yeísmo rehilado** and is the single most audible marker of Argentine speech.
- The letters *c* before *e* or *i* and the letter *z* are pronounced as English "s" — *cinco* ("SEEN-koh"), *Buenos Aires* ("BWEH-nohs EYE-rehs"). Argentina uses the **seseo**, not the peninsular-Spanish *ceceo*.

## A note on politeness

Argentine Spanish is warm and conversational. Porteños routinely add the filler *che* ("hey," "dude," but softer) to informal speech; you do not need to use *che* yourself, but hearing it is useful — it means the speaker is being friendly. *Por favor* ("please") and *gracias* ("thank you") at the start or end of a sentence make any refusal more comfortable, and a soft tone carries authority the way a raised voice does not.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**  
¿Cuánto es?  
*KWAN-toh ess?*

**How much does it cost?**  
¿Cuánto sale? (Argentine preference)  
*KWAN-toh SAH-leh?*

**Please turn on the meter.**  
El taxímetro, por favor.  
*El tahk-SEE-meh-troh, por fah-VOR.*

Say this the instant you get into a taxi. If the driver refuses, get out. Every licensed Argentine taxi has a meter and is legally required to use it.

**Can I see the menu in Spanish, please?**  
¿Me muestra la carta en español, por favor?  
*Meh MWES-trah la KAR-ta en es-pah-NYOL, por fah-VOR?*

In Argentina, "menu" is *la carta* — the full list with prices. If the waiter has handed you an English menu with markups, asking for the Spanish one is the single most effective push-back.

**Does this include the cubierto?**  
¿Está incluido el cubierto?  
*Es-TAH een-kloo-EE-doh el koo-BYER-toh?*

The cubierto (cover charge) is Argentina's signature tourist-overcharge. Ask this before you order, and confirm the stated amount per person matches the menu. A legitimate cubierto is ARS 1,500–3,500 and must be printed on the menu.

**Does this include service?**  
¿Está incluido el servicio?  
*Es-TAH een-kloo-EE-doh el ser-VEE-syoh?*

"Servicio incluido" added to the bill without disclosure on the menu is not legal. Ask this when the bill arrives.

**Do you accept credit cards?**  
¿Aceptan tarjeta?  
*Ah-THEP-tahn tar-HEH-tah?*

Card acceptance is near-universal in Buenos Aires, Córdoba, Mendoza, Rosario, and all major tourist zones; many Patagonian restaurants and some Salta bodegas are cash-only.

**What is the MEP rate today?**  
¿Cuál es el tipo de cambio MEP hoy?  
*Kwal ess el TEE-poh deh KAHM-byoh MEP oy?*

If you are paying with a foreign credit card, the **MEP rate** (roughly the US-dollar-equivalent tourist rate in 2026) is what your card is automatically billed at. Knowing the current MEP rate before you sit down is the single best defense against creative "peso-to-dollar" conversions at bill time.

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**  
No, gracias.  
*Noh, GRAH-syas.*

The single most useful phrase in this appendix. Said firmly, without slowing your pace. Walking away is not rude; it is Argentine-normal.

**I'm not interested.**  
No me interesa.  
*Noh meh een-teh-REH-sah.*

Stronger than *no, gracias*. Use this when a cambio tout, a remis driver, or a boat-tour pitch keeps talking after the first refusal.

**Please leave me alone.**  
Déjame en paz, por favor.  
*DEH-hah-meh en path, por fah-VOR.*

Literally "leave me in peace, please." Used firmly but without a raised voice. In Rioplatense, *déjame* (informal *vos* form) is more common than peninsular *déjeme*.

**Don't touch me.**  
No me toques.  
*Noh meh TOH-kehs.*

Say this clearly and step back. Most touts will disengage immediately rather than escalate.

**Excuse me, let me through.**  
Permiso, por favor.  
*Per-MEE-soh, por fah-VOR.*

For clearing a path when a pickpocket team is crowding you in a *colectivo* or on Florida Avenue.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I'm calling the police.**  
Voy a llamar a la policía.  
*Boy ah zha-MAR ah la poh-lee-SEE-ah.*

This is the firmest card in the deck. Argentina's unified emergency number is **911**; in Buenos Aires city you can also dial **911** or **147**. Say the phrase, hold up your phone, and start dialing. Most scam operators back down the moment they hear *policía*.

**I'll call my bank.**  
Voy a llamar al banco.  
*Boy ah zha-MAR al BAHN-koh.*

Say this if a restaurant, taxi driver, or tour operator disputes a credit-card transaction. Calling your card issuer from the table signals that the charge is going to be disputed.

**I was scammed. Please help.**  
Me estafaron. Ayudame, por favor.  
*Meh es-tah-FAH-rohn. Ah-zhoo-DAH-meh, por fah-VOR.*

The word *estafaron* ("they scammed me," in the impersonal they-form) is the specific Argentine Spanish construction for "I was scammed." Use this when speaking to a Policía officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, a fellow traveler, or any bystander you want to enlist. (Rioplatense uses *ayudame*, the *vos* form, instead of peninsular *ayúdeme*.)

**Please give me an itemized bill.**  
La cuenta detallada, por favor.  
*La KWEN-ta deh-tah-ZHAH-dah, por fah-VOR.*

Asking for *la cuenta detallada* forces the establishment to account for every item, and it is the most polite version of "I do not accept this charge." A legitimate place will hand it to you without complaint. A tourist trap will stall.

**I didn't order this.**  
No pedí esto.  
*Noh peh-DEE ES-toh.*

Say this calmly about any unrequested dish, bottle, bread basket, or "service" that appears on your bill. In Argentina you are not legally required to pay for food or drinks you did not order.

**This is not what we agreed.**  
Esto no es lo que acordamos.  
*ES-toh noh ess loh keh ah-kor-DAH-mohs.*

A short, firm phrase for taxi fares that exceed a quoted price, tour-operator price-creep, and rental-car "damage" disputes.

**These notes are counterfeit.**  
Estos billetes son falsos.  
*ES-tohs bee-ZHEH-tehs sohn FAL-sohs.*

If you receive a counterfeit peso bill at a cambio or as change, say this calmly to the cashier and request a replacement. A legitimate establishment will swap it immediately — counterfeit circulation is a problem they want to manage. A scam operator will stall.

## Getting help

**Where's the nearest police station?**  
¿Dónde está la comisaría más cercana?  
*DOHN-deh es-TAH la koh-mee-sa-REE-ah mahs ser-KAH-na?*

"Comisaría" is understood everywhere in Argentina.

**Where's the tourist police?**  
¿Dónde está la policía turística?  
*DOHN-deh es-TAH la poh-lee-SEE-ah too-REES-tee-kah?*

In Buenos Aires, the Comisaría del Turista is at Av. Corrientes 436, +54 11 4346 5748 — 24/7 English-speaking. Other cities have equivalents listed in Appendix C.

**I'd like to report a crime.**  
Quiero hacer una denuncia.  
*KYEH-roh ah-SER OO-na deh-NOON-syah.*

The word *denuncia* is the specific Argentine Spanish term for a criminal report. You will walk out of the station with a printed, stamped *denuncia* — this is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

**Is there someone who speaks English?**  
¿Hay alguien que hable inglés?  
*Eye AHL-gyen keh AH-bleh een-GLESS?*

Every Comisaría del Turista has at least one English-speaking officer on duty.

**Please call 911.**  
Llame al 911, por favor.  
*ZHAH-meh al NOH-beh-OO-noh-OO-noh, por fah-VOR.*

The unified emergency line **911** works on every mobile phone in Argentina — even without a SIM, even on a locked screen. Dispatchers speak basic English in Buenos Aires and in provincial capitals.

**Help! Thief!**  
¡Socorro! ¡Chorro!  
*Soh-KOH-rroh! CHOH-rroh!*

*Chorro* is Argentine slang for "thief" (replacing peninsular *ladrón* almost entirely in spoken Buenos Aires Spanish). Shouted in a crowded market or street, this is understood by every porteño bystander as "someone stole from me, help me stop them."

**Please help me.**  
Ayudame, por favor.  
*Ah-zhoo-DAH-meh, por fah-VOR.*

## Reading Argentine peso amounts

Argentine peso prices are written with the symbol **$** (yes, the same sign as US dollars — context disambiguates; when US dollars are meant, Argentines usually write **USD** or **U$S**). Decimal conventions are European — a comma is the decimal separator ($3,50 = three pesos fifty centavos) and a dot is the thousands separator ($1.500 = 1,500 pesos). This is the opposite of English conventions.

In 2026, the key denominations in circulation are:

- Coins: $1, $2, $5, $10 (increasingly rare due to inflation; mostly phased out of everyday use).
- Notes: $100, $200, $500, $1,000, $2,000, $10,000, $20,000 (the 20,000-peso note was introduced in 2024).
- One US dollar ≈ 1,400 Argentine pesos at the official rate, ≈ 1,350–1,500 at the MEP tourist-card rate, ≈ same-range blue-market rate.

| Amount | Written | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| 100 pesos | cien pesos | *syen PEH-sohs* |
| 500 pesos | quinientos pesos | *kee-NYEN-tohs PEH-sohs* |
| 1,000 pesos | mil pesos | *meel PEH-sohs* |
| 10,000 pesos | diez mil pesos | *dyess meel PEH-sohs* |
| 20,000 pesos | veinte mil pesos | *BEYN-teh meel PEH-sohs* |
| 45,000 pesos | cuarenta y cinco mil | *kwah-REN-tah ee SEEN-koh meel* |
| 100,000 pesos | cien mil pesos | *syen meel PEH-sohs* |
| One million | un millón | *oon mee-ZHOHN* |

If a vendor quotes a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it out:

**Please write the number down.**  
¿Me lo puede escribir, por favor?  
*Meh loh PWEH-deh es-kree-BEER, por fah-VOR?*

Almost every vendor will oblige, often on the back of a receipt or a paper napkin.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Argentina, polite firmness works better than raised voices — raising your voice reads as losing your composure and rarely helps your case. A calm *no, gracias* is already a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *déjame en paz* ("leave me in peace") and *no me toques* ("don't touch me") are both firm without being rude, and *voy a llamar a la policía* ("I'm calling the police") is the firmest card in the deck.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar — and a calm *no, gracias* with a *por favor* at the end is, in itself, proof you know enough Spanish that the interaction is going to be harder than they expected.
