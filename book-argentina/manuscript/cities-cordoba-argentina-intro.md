Córdoba is Argentina's second-largest city (population roughly 1.5 million), the colonial heart of the country's interior, and the capital of the Jesuit UNESCO heritage corridor. The Manzana Jesuítica — the Jesuit block that anchors Plaza San Martín — still holds the 1687 church, the Colegio Nacional de Monserrat, and the university cloisters that make Córdoba Argentina's oldest university town. An hour west, the Sierras de Córdoba pick up the country's summer mountain-holiday traffic, from the Germanic Villa General Belgrano (and its Oktoberfest, first weekend of October, non-negotiably booked out) to La Cumbrecita to the Che Guevara museum at Alta Gracia.

Tourists split into two groups here: day-trippers doing the Manzana Jesuítica and the Nueva Córdoba cafés, and Sierras-bound travelers transiting through the city to VGB, La Cumbrecita, or the Mina Clavero valley. Both groups pass through Ingeniero Aeropuerto Ambrosio Taravella (COR), which is the single worst airport-taxi ecosystem in Argentina in 2026. The COR taxi cooperative has an openly hostile relationship with the legal ride-hailing apps (Uber, Cabify, DiDi): drivers physically block the arrivals curb, throw stones at rideshare vehicles approaching the drop-off, and quote foreign-speakers USD 45 for a trip an Uber runs at USD 15–18. Local redditors and Clarín coverage confirm the workaround: walk to the main CPC Monseñor Pablo Cabrera entrance (five minutes from arrivals), request rideshare from there, and skip the rank entirely.

Inside the city, the scam profile is classic-Argentine: Güemes Saturday-market tourist-menu markup, Nueva Córdoba motochorro phone-snatchers on sidewalks near Plaza España, and the Sierras accommodation fraud (especially during Oktoberfest season, when legitimate VGB inventory sells out months ahead and last-minute WhatsApp-listed "rooms" materialize from nowhere). Córdoba's provincial **Policía Turística** (0351-434-2168) and the city tourist-information office both operate English-speaking channels for reporting.
