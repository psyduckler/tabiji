# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are organized by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — a cambio cueva, a tour-operator office, a restaurant with a suddenly inflated bill, a taxi that has detoured to a "friend's" leather shop — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. A signed credit-card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, dial **911** (Argentina's unified emergency line) or, in Buenos Aires city, **911** and **147**. For medical emergencies, **SAME** (ambulance) is **107**; for fire, **Bomberos** is **100**.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection (Google Maps dropped-pin is ideal), the shop's **AFIP** (federal tax) certificate (which Argentine law requires every retail establishment to display), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward. If a pickpocket team is still in the area, walk 100 meters in any direction before you pull out your phone to screenshot.

If your bag or wallet was just stolen in the street, shout — Argentine shopkeepers and passers-by will often help, and a visible commotion is the fastest way to draw attention. In a Buenos Aires Subte (metro) station, the *boletero* (ticket-booth attendant) is the closest authority; station staff can call Policía and hold security-camera footage for you.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest *comisaría* — the local police station — if one is within ten minutes; otherwise, to the nearest **Comisaría del Turista** or provincial equivalent. In Buenos Aires the Comisaría del Turista is at Av. Corrientes 436, +54 11 4346 5748, 24/7 English-speaking. Say *"Quiero hacer una denuncia"* ("I'd like to file a report"). You will walk out with a stamped *denuncia* (police report) that includes a reference number. This is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

Argentina does not have a mature online *denuncia telemática* system equivalent to Spain's, but the Policía de la Ciudad de Buenos Aires does operate a web-based pre-filing form at **denuncias.policia.gob.ar** that can shorten the in-person wait in CABA. Provincial police forces vary; in Córdoba and Mendoza, pre-filing is possible at the provincial police website; in Patagonia, in-person is still the norm.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from Argentina and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call. If the charge was a restaurant overcharge, a tour-operator "deposit," or a rental "damage" advance, also call your bank's fraud line directly — the number on the back of your card — because fraud desks sometimes freeze faster than the Visa or Mastercard emergency lines. If the card is Argentine-issued, the major banks (Galicia, Santander Río, BBVA Argentina, Macro, HSBC Argentina, ICBC Argentina) all run 24/7 card-loss lines at the numbers on the back of the card.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the *denuncia* reference number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Visa and Mastercard both offer strong chargeback rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend credit over debit for every Argentina trip.

For tourism-specific complaints against a registered business (a hotel that charged a phantom "tourist tax," a restaurant that padded a bill with an undisclosed cubierto, a tour operator that didn't deliver), file with the **Defensoría del Consumidor** — the Consumer Affairs office, which in Argentina operates at the level of each province. Buenos Aires city's is **Defensoría del Consumidor CABA** (+54 11 4323-9800, online at **buenosaires.gob.ar/defensoría**). Provinces like Córdoba, Santa Fe (Rosario), Mendoza, and Santa Cruz (El Calafate, Ushuaia) each have their own *Defensoría*. All accept complaints in Spanish and many now offer English-language intake; all publish online forms. Refusing to provide a receipt or to return an overcharge is itself a violation that *Defensoría del Consumidor* will sanction.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. US citizens should contact the US Embassy in Buenos Aires (Av. Colombia 4300, Palermo); UK, Canadian, Australian, Irish, French, and German citizens all have embassies in Buenos Aires; full list in Appendix C.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the *denuncia* reference number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position. The major dispatchers serving the Latin-American market — Allianz Global Assistance, AXA Partners, World Nomads, Chubb, Zurich, IMG Global — all operate 24/7 English-language lines for travelers and have local medical-provider networks throughout Argentina. Argentine private hospitals (Hospital Alemán, Hospital Británico, Hospital Italiano, Sanatorio Güemes in Buenos Aires; Hospital Italiano in Córdoba, Mendoza, Rosario) typically bill your insurer directly once a *Guarantee of Payment* is in place; public hospitals treat everyone in emergency cases regardless of nationality and bill insurance afterward.

If the scam involved an off-platform rental or a wire-fraud hotel booking, open the dispute on the booking platform (Booking.com, Airbnb, Expedia, Agoda) as well as with your card issuer. Platforms can sometimes recover deposits the card company cannot, and Airbnb in particular has a dedicated Argentina fraud team given how many bookings involve Argentine hosts.

## Within the first week

File a formal consumer-protection complaint with the national **Dirección Nacional de Defensa del Consumidor** — Argentina's federal consumer-affairs authority (online at **argentina.gob.ar/defensa-del-consumidor**). Parallel filings at the provincial *Defensoría* (Córdoba, Mendoza, Santa Cruz, Tierra del Fuego) speed regulatory attention. Regulators do not recover individual losses, but their regulatory actions against repeat-offender tour operators, rental shops, and hotels are one of the few effective pressures on the scam economy. Tourist-facing businesses know which complaints reach the regulator; a filed complaint sometimes produces a restitution offer that the shop refused to make on the day of the incident.

For overcharging by licensed taxis, file a parallel complaint with the **municipal taxi authority**. Buenos Aires city's is **SUBE Taxis CABA** (online at buenosaires.gob.ar/taxis, +54 11 4323-9800). Córdoba's is the Municipalidad de Córdoba Taxi division; Rosario's is the Municipalidad de Rosario. With the license number (the license number on the rear of every legal cab in Argentina, always photograph it before getting out), the authority can identify the driver, issue fines, and sometimes produce compensation.

For banking and payment disputes — a charge your Argentine bank refuses to reverse, an ATM skim you consider the bank should cover, a disputed wire-transfer fraud — escalate to **Banco Central de la República Argentina (BCRA)** at **bcra.gob.ar** (the Argentine financial-sector regulator) and to the **Centro de Defensa del Usuario Financiero**. For securities and investment-related fraud, the regulator is the **Comisión Nacional de Valores (CNV)**.

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business's name, the address, the exact transaction details, the *denuncia* reference number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name and location only. Argentine defamation law (both civil and criminal) is strict — stick to verifiable facts and receipts.

## If your phone or SIM was stolen

Call your carrier immediately to suspend the line. The four major Argentine carriers — **Movistar** (dial **\*611**), **Personal** (dial **\*111**), **Claro** (dial **\*611**), and **Flow Móvil** (the newer entrant) — all operate 24/7 stolen-device lines. Suspending the line prevents the thief from using your number to receive SMS two-factor-authentication codes and from running up international-roaming charges. If you are on a tourist eSIM (Airalo, Saily, Holafly, etc.), the app will let you suspend the line in a few taps; do that before the *denuncia*.

File a *denuncia* at the nearest police station and include your phone's **IMEI** number (the fifteen-digit serial; find it on the original box, in your Apple or Google account, or by dialing \*#06# *before* the theft). The IMEI lets police flag the device and is required for any insurance claim. If your SIM was the target — for example, a SIM-swap scam to intercept your bank's one-time codes — contact your bank's fraud desk before anything else; SIM-swap fraud moves faster than theft recovery, and the first hour is decisive.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking, drug-planting, or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of 24/7 hospitals with English-speaking staff in each city is in Appendix C. At the hospital, ask for a *análisis toxicológico*; Argentine private hospitals such as Hospital Alemán, Hospital Británico, and Hospital Italiano run these routinely and will print a report you can use as evidence. Argentine public hospitals treat every emergency case without up-front payment and will bill insurance afterward.

For drug-planting scams — where a stranger, a bar staffer, or occasionally someone in uniform claims to have "found" a substance in your possession — stay calm, do not resist, and dial **911** before any money changes hands. Real narcotics cases in Argentina go through official processes with written documentation, photographed evidence, and a *denuncia* you sign. Demands for cash to "make it go away" are the signature of a scam, not of legitimate enforcement.

In Argentina, the medical-emergency number is **107** (SAME); both **911** and **107** route to the same dispatch in Buenos Aires city. For fire, dial **100** (Bomberos). For any incident involving a foreign tourist, **911** is the most reliable fallback with English-speaking dispatchers in Buenos Aires and most provincial capitals. If you are inside a venue and drink-spiking is suspected, have a friend call for an ambulance *before* you leave, not after — sometimes the aggressive bill pressure escalates once the scammer realizes you are leaving.

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate a replacement. Consular registration — STEP for US citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, LOCATE for UK, ROCA for Canadians, and the Irish equivalent — is worth doing before you travel, specifically for situations like this.
