# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The sixty-six scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: A stranger who approaches you first is almost certainly running a script

This is the single most important habit you can take to Argentina. The instant someone steps into your path uninvited on Florida Avenue, near the Monumento Nacional a la Bandera in Rosario, at the Estación Fluvial in Tigre, or outside the Garganta del Diablo viewing platform at Iguazú, the interaction that follows is a rehearsed script. You are not being greeted. You are being sorted.

Argentina's scam ecosystem is unusually warm, and the openers almost always sound friendly or helpful. On Calle Florida a young porteño in a cream button-down steps from a doorway holding a clipboard with a hand-drawn exchange rate and calls "¡Cambio, cambio! Dollar, euro!" — the *arbolito* (little tree), named for the way these touts line the street like a row of trees. In Buenos Aires's Recoleta and Microcentro, the bird-dropping distraction pickpocket script still plays: a stranger points at a stain on your shoulder and, while "helping" to wipe it off, a second person lifts your phone from your other side. At the Estación Fluvial in Tigre, an unlicensed lanchero waves a flyer for a "private Delta tour" that is either a no-show or a substandard vessel. In Iguazú, downtown travel agencies push "Triple Frontier Day Trips" that cross you into Paraguay via drivers who skip migraciones stamps to save ten minutes — and leave you with a $100 overstay fine at your real border exit.

None of these interactions start with you asking for help. That is the tell. Argentines are famously warm, but they do not generally accost foreign tourists on the street to volunteer exchange rates, wipe stains off their clothing, or offer tours from the sidewalk.

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: when any stranger starts a conversation with you near a famous site, a bus station, or a pedestrian shopping street, reply "*no, gracias*" — firmly — and keep walking. Do not break stride. Do not engage. If the person follows you, repeat the phrase once, louder, and step into the nearest shop or café. Argentine politeness conventions do not require you to listen to a sales pitch, and walking away is not rude.

*You will see this pattern in the Florida Avenue "¡cambio!" touts (Buenos Aires), the bird-dropping pickpocket script (Buenos Aires), the EZE arrivals "Meet & Greet" remis tout, the Peatonal Sarmiento cambio touts (Mendoza), the Estación Fluvial unlicensed lanchero (Tigre), the Triple Frontier border-crossing pitch (Puerto Iguazú), and almost every approach scene in the city chapters that follow.*

## Pattern 2: "The meter is broken" means you are being overcharged

The second pattern is taxis. Argentina has strict taxi regulation — meters are mandatory in every licensed cab, and three ride-hailing apps (Uber, Cabify, DiDi) are now legal in Buenos Aires, Córdoba, Mendoza, Rosario, and most major cities. But a meaningful share of tourist rides begin with the driver refusing the meter or declining the app and quoting a flat price — always two to four times what the meter would charge.

In Buenos Aires, the official airport-taxi counter at Ezeiza (EZE) posts a flat rate of roughly ARS 35,000–45,000 to central Buenos Aires; arrivals-hall "Remis" touts quote ARS 80,000–150,000. In Córdoba, the COR airport taxi mafia physically blocks Uber and Cabify vehicles from the arrivals curb and has been documented throwing stones at rideshare cars — but local redditors confirm that walking to the main CPC Monseñor Pablo Cabrera entrance for a rideshare pickup is legal, safe, and less than half the taxi fare. In Rosario, the ROS Fisherton airport remis counter posts Zone 5 (Centro) at ARS 21,760; arrivals-hall taxis quote USD 45 flat, roughly double. In Ushuaia, the scam has its own local variant: Spanish-speakers are quoted ARS 6,500 to town, while foreign-sounding voices are quoted USD 50 for the same ride.

The "meter is broken" phrase — in Spanish, "*el taxímetro está roto*" — is the specific tell. Argentine meters are not broken. Refusing to use the meter is refusing to charge the legal price.

The defense is short and universal. In cities where Uber, Cabify, and DiDi operate, use an app. Pricing is transparent, payment runs through the app, and there is no "meter-is-broken" conversation on arrival. If you must take a street taxi, take it only from the official rank, where an inspector is often present. Before the car pulls away, say calmly: "*Taxímetro, por favor*" — "meter, please." If the driver refuses, get out and take the next cab. At airports, the published flat fare is on a printed card at the official taxi counter; insist on that. If a driver runs a tourist route (detouring through a long avenue just to add 3 km to a 2-km fare), use Google Maps in real time from the back seat and ask calmly why the route is not matching.

*You will see this pattern in the Ezeiza "Meet & Greet" remis overcharge (Buenos Aires), the Manuel Tienda León alternative, the COR airport taxi mafia rideshare-blocking (Córdoba), the ROS Fisherton "USD 45 flat" trick (Rosario), the USH dual-pricing scam (Ushuaia), the FTE airport taxi vs. VES shuttle decision (El Calafate), and the BRC arrivals tout script (Bariloche).*

## Pattern 3: Prices that aren't posted will surprise you later

The third pattern is the unposted price. Argentine restaurants along Paseo Victorica in Tigre, on Av. Maipú in Ushuaia, at Cafayate bodega tourist lunches, and on every tourist-facing strip in Buenos Aires, Mendoza, and Salta will sometimes — not always — quote you a price that is deliberately vague. "Precio especial." "Cubierto incluido." "Propina sugerida." "Ya hablamos al final." Each of these phrases hides a later surprise.

This pattern accounts for a meaningful share of the overcharging scams in this book. The **cubierto** is the key concept: a legitimate Argentine cover charge typically ARS 1,500–3,500 per person. It is legal and standard — provided it is printed on the menu before you order. Tourist-strip restaurants on Florida Avenue, Paseo Victorica, Av. del Libertador in El Calafate, and Av. Maipú in Ushuaia routinely add a cubierto of ARS 5,000–8,000 *per person* that was not on the menu. Add a "propina sugerida" (10 % suggested tip that quietly becomes "servicio incluido" 15 %) and a "panera especial" (bread you did not order, ARS 4,000–6,000) and a party of four can walk out paying ARS 25,000 in fees alone.

The English menu is the second trap. Many restaurants in San Telmo, Palermo, San Carlos de Bariloche, and Cafayate keep two menus: a Spanish-language menu at honest prices, and an English menu with the same items marked up 30 to 60 percent. The waiter hands you the English menu automatically.

The defense is a single habit, repeated before every transaction: ask to see the printed menu before you sit down, photograph the cubierto line with your phone, and — if there is any doubt — ask "*¿Puedo ver el menú en español, por favor?*" When the bill arrives, read it line by line. "Servicio incluido" added without menu disclosure is not legal under Argentine consumer-protection law and can be formally disputed. For ATM and exchange transactions, count all pesos in daylight before walking away — especially 1,000, 2,000, 10,000, and 20,000 notes. Pay every restaurant bill with a foreign credit card where possible: the MEP tourist-card rate is automatically applied in 2026, and you have chargeback rights if a fraudulent charge appears.

*You will see this pattern in the Florida Avenue restaurant cubierto scam (Buenos Aires), the Paseo Victorica bill-padding (Tigre), the Av. del Libertador tourist-menu double-pricing (El Calafate), the Av. Maipú "centolla special" USD-pricing gambit (Ushuaia), the Peatonal Sarmiento cubierto (Mendoza), the Salta Balcarce empanada markup, and the Cafayate wine-pairing "Torrontés reserve" upsell.*

## Pattern 4: The "scratch on the car," the "damage deposit," the "broken window"

The fourth pattern is the damage claim. You rent a car at Ezeiza, Bariloche, or Calafate airport, and when you return it, the operator points at an imaginary or pre-existing scratch and demands hundreds of dollars for "damage." Or worse: you leave your rental parked at a Patagonian viewpoint, come back to a smashed window and missing camera, and are billed both for the deductible *and* the replacement glass.

Bariloche's Circuito Chico is the epicenter of the smash-and-grab variant. The sixty-kilometer scenic loop around Cerro Catedral and Lake Nahuel Huapi has a dozen documented break-in spots — Cerro Campanario, Llao Llao, Punto Panorámico, Bahía López — where organized groups break rental-car windows while tourists are at the viewpoints. The rental company then collects both the comprehensive-insurance deductible (USD 300–500) and a separate glass-replacement fee (USD 150–300). El Calafate, Mendoza, and Iguazú airport rental agencies all run variants of the same "imaginary scratch" gambit at drop-off.

The defense is photographic, and it is mandatory. Before you accept the rental, take six to ten date-stamped photos of the car from every angle: front, back, left, right, wheels, the specific areas where scratches typically appear, the underside if the vehicle is high enough, and a close-up of the odometer and fuel gauge. Hand over a photocopy of your passport identification page rather than the real passport; keep the real passport in the hotel safe. Photograph the fuel gauge at pick-up and at return, and keep the final gas-station receipt. Pay every rental with a credit card, never a debit card — only a credit card gives you chargeback rights if a fraudulent "damage" charge appears. If a damage claim is made at return, produce the photos on the spot.

For Patagonian rentals, never leave anything visible in the car at any viewpoint: no backpack, no jacket, no shopping bag, not even an empty phone-charger cable. Take everything with you at every stop. If your window is broken despite the precautions, call **911** immediately and file a *denuncia* at the nearest police station within twenty-four hours; the police report is required for your rental company's insurance claim and for travel-insurance reimbursement.

*You will see this pattern in the Bariloche Circuito Chico smash-and-grab (the signature Patagonian rental scam), the FTE El Calafate rental "scratch" trick, the Mendoza MDZ airport drop-off dispute, the Iguazú "damage at return" variant, and the Ezeiza rental-car fuel-surcharge trick.*

## Pattern 5: An "official" is an official only at an official office

The fifth pattern is the stranger in authority. Real Argentine police — **Policía Federal** in dark-blue uniforms and peaked caps, **Policía de la Ciudad de Buenos Aires** in navy blue with "CABA" patches, **Gendarmería Nacional** in khaki, **Policía Provincial** in provincial liveries, and **Prefectura Naval** in navy blue at ports and airports — operate only from official stations, checkpoints, and their marked patrol cars. They do not demand to see the cash in your wallet on the street. They do not ask for a "fine" payable in cash on the spot. They do not conduct drug checks on sidewalks in tourist areas.

The *falsos policías* pattern runs across Argentine cities in two forms. The first, documented repeatedly in Buenos Aires and Rosario, is the "counterfeit check": two men in polo shirts flash unofficial laminated cards, claim they are checking for counterfeit currency after a nearby bank report, and ask to "see" the bills in your wallet — at which point they lift notes or swap a genuine 10,000-peso bill for a counterfeit one during the examination. The second, concentrated at Retiro bus station in Buenos Aires and at the Mendoza bus terminal, is the fake migraciones or fake tax-inspector check: a man with a high-visibility vest tells tourists their tourist tax hasn't been paid and a fine must be paid to avoid a problem with Migraciones. Argentine Migraciones does not do this. Argentine customs does not do this.

The defense is simple and universal. If anyone on the street identifies themselves as an official and wants to examine your wallet, passport, pocket contents, or phone, refuse and walk into the nearest well-lit shop or hotel lobby. Say calmly: "Vamos a la comisaría" — "Let's go to the station." Dial **911** from your phone and put the call on speaker if the person has not already left. Real officers have no problem with this. Fake ones leave immediately. In Buenos Aires, the **Comisaría del Turista** (Av. Corrientes 436, +54 11 4346 5748) has 24/7 English-speaking officers. Córdoba's **Policía Turística** is reachable at 0351-4342168. Contact details for every city are in the appendix.

*You will see this pattern in the Buenos Aires fake-currency-inspector approach, the Retiro bus terminal fake-Migraciones gambit, the Rosario Peatonal Córdoba fake-plainclothes script, the Mendoza Plaza Independencia "identification please" variant, and the general _falsos policías_ pattern across every major tourist city.*

## Pattern 6: If you pay before you have the thing, you probably won't get the thing

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch by reading the fine print after you have committed — so this pattern is really about what to pin down before you click *Confirm* or hand over cash.

You book a Perito Moreno "Big Ice" ice-trek online through a third-party reseller, pay full price by bank transfer, and the "operator" turns out to be a Facebook page with stolen photos; on arrival at Puerto Bandera, there is no booking in Hielo y Aventura's system and no tour. You book an Ushuaia Antarctic-cruise "last-minute deal" sight unseen from abroad, pay a USD 2,000 deposit, and the agency turns out not to exist. You agree to a Villa General Belgrano Oktoberfest hotel package and arrive in October to find the "hotel" was a private apartment that no longer accepts your booking. You hand USD 200 to a Florida Avenue street agent for a "VIP Teatro Colón tour" that was never purchased. You pay a deposit on a San Telmo apartment via WhatsApp to a "landlord" whose property turns out to be a Google-Street-View ghost.

All of these work because you committed before you knew the terms — and then the terms, the vendor, the venue, or the platform were not what you thought they were.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction you make in Argentina. Book accommodation only through platforms that offer pay-through-platform guarantees and dispute resolution (Booking.com, Airbnb, Expedia, Agoda) — never by Facebook, WhatsApp, or direct wire transfer to a private Argentine CBU or foreign IBAN. Verify any property on Google Street View before you depart — a legitimate Argentine apartment has visible signage and appears on at least one established booking platform. Pay every rental, excursion, or hotel by credit card (Visa, Mastercard, American Express) rather than debit, cash, or wire transfer — only a credit card gives you a chargeback right, and you can reverse a fraudulent charge within sixty days.

For **Perito Moreno ice treks** book only through **hieloyaventura.com** (the single concessionaire, with mandatory at-the-pier boarding at Puerto Bandera). For **Iguazú National Park entry** book only through **iguazuargentina.com**. For **Tren a las Nubes** book only through **trenalasnubes.com.ar**. For **Antarctic cruises** book only through established operators (Quark Expeditions, Hurtigruten, Oceanwide Expeditions, Silversea) via their own websites. For **long-distance buses** use **plataforma10.com** (a verified aggregator that cross-references Chevallier, Andesmar, Flecha Bus, and Vía Bariloche). For **domestic flights** use **aerolineas.com.ar**, **flybondi.com**, or **jetsmart.com** — never a third-party agent emailed to you unsolicited.

### One more: the "skip-the-line ticket" and fake-tour-guide scams

Argentina has its own set of ticketing traps. Private "skip-the-line" resellers charging USD 80 for a ticket that the monument's own website sells for ARS 45,000. Fake tour guides at Iguazú, the Recoleta Cemetery, and the Caminito offering "private tours" that include no actual entry ticket. Official entry is always through the monument's own website, clearly branded, with a named provincial or federal tourism authority behind it.

*You will see this pattern in the Perito Moreno ice-trek phantom-reseller (El Calafate), the Ushuaia Antarctic-cruise last-minute scam, the VGB Oktoberfest accommodation fraud (Córdoba), the Teatro Colón street-agent trap (Buenos Aires), the San Telmo WhatsApp-apartment fraud, the Florida Avenue "VIP tango-show ticket" pitch, and the general "wire a deposit before you arrive" scam that hits every Argentine destination.*
