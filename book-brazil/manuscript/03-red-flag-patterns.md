# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The seventy-two scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: A stranger who approaches you first is almost certainly running a script

This is the single most important habit you can take to Brazil. The instant a smiling local steps into your path uninvited at an airport arrivals hall, a famous beach promenade, a historic plaza, a church entrance, or a market gate, the interaction that follows is a rehearsed script. You are not being greeted. You are being sorted.

Brazil's scam ecosystem is unusually verbal, and the openers are almost always warm. At Rio's Galeão airport, a man in a fluorescent vest flashes a laminated "Taxi Especial" sign and says "my friend! Your hotel? R\$ 250" — the real Uber is fifty meters away at the Aplicativos pickup zone for R\$ 80. In Salvador's Pelourinho, a woman in a white Bahiana dress offers to tie a colorful *fita do Senhor do Bonfim* ribbon around your wrist "for three wishes — free, a gift" and then demands R\$ 50–R\$ 200 once it is knotted. On Fortaleza's Beira Mar orla, a motorbike pulls alongside while you take a phone photo of the sunset — the rider snatches the phone and is gone before you finish the word *pare*. In Rio's Lapa, a friendly local invites you to "Rio's best caipirinha bar" down a side alley; two drinks and an unmarked tab later, the bill is R\$ 10,000 and two bouncers are at the door. In Ouro Preto, a friendly vendor presses a soapstone figurine into your hand — "a gift, take a picture with it!" — and then demands R\$ 50 for "the artisan's time."

None of these interactions start with you asking for help. That is the tell. Brazilians are famously warm, but they do not generally accost foreign tourists at airports, plazas, or entrances to volunteer taxis, ribbons, tea invitations, or "special" bar recommendations.

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: when any stranger starts a conversation with you at an airport, a pier, or the entrance to a famous site, reply "*não, obrigado*" (no, thank you — pronounced *now, oh-bree-gah-doo*, feminine *obrigada*) and keep walking. Do not break stride. Do not engage. If the person follows you, repeat the phrase once, louder. Brazilian politeness conventions do not require you to listen to a sales pitch, and walking away is not rude.

*You will see this pattern in: the Galeão airport "Special Taxi" mafia (Rio), the Pelourinho Bonfim ribbon forced-tip (Salvador), the Avenida Paulista "clowns" distraction (São Paulo), the Beira Mar motorbike phone-snatch (Fortaleza), the Copacabana caipirinha-bar honeypot (Rio), the Manaus Centro Histórico "Teatro Amazonas guide" offer, and almost every tout scene in the 72 scam entries that follow.*

## Pattern 2: "The meter is broken" or "the app is broken" means you are being overcharged

The second pattern is Uber and taxis. Brazil has **Uber**, **99**, and **Cabify** nationwide, and they are cheap, metered, and transparent — you see the price before you book, and you pay through the app. But a meaningful share of tourist rides begin with a driver refusing the app and quoting a flat price — always 2× to 5× what the app would charge.

From Rio's Galeão airport (GIG), the real Uber fare to Ipanema or Copacabana is R\$ 90–R\$ 140. The flat-rate airport-hall quote is R\$ 250–R\$ 400. From São Paulo's Guarulhos (GRU) to Paulista or Jardins is R\$ 90–R\$ 180 on Uber; the "Special Taxi" flat quote is R\$ 250–R\$ 500. In Salvador (SSA), the airport flat quote runs to R\$ 200–R\$ 400 to Pelourinho when the Uber price is R\$ 60–R\$ 90. In Foz do Iguaçu (IGU), drivers will quote R\$ 150–R\$ 300 to Centro for an R\$ 45–R\$ 75 Uber route. In Brasília (BSB), the "Special Taxi" quote to Asa Sul is R\$ 200–R\$ 350 when Uber is R\$ 40–R\$ 65.

The "the meter is broken" or "the app cancelled — R\$ 100 cash please" phrase is the specific tell. Uber always works in Brazil's major cities. A refusal to open the app, or a mid-trip demand for cash on top of the in-app price, is a refusal to charge you the market price.

The defense is short and universal. Open Uber (or 99, or Cabify) and book the ride on the app; the price is shown up front, paid digitally, and there is no meter-is-broken conversation. If you must take a street taxi, look for the city's licensed cooperative (Coopertáxi in Rio, Radiotaxi in São Paulo, Cooptáxi / Coometas in Salvador) and insist on the meter or a *bandeira 2* receipt. Never hand a credit card to any taxi driver — the cloned-terminal manual-entry scam is documented nationwide. If a driver demands cash top-up for a "special surcharge" that doesn't exist in the app, refuse politely, exit the vehicle at the next public place, cancel the trip in-app, and report via the in-app safety button. For late-night arrivals at any airport, pre-book a transfer via your hotel rather than improvising with whoever is in the arrivals hall.

*You will see this pattern in: the GIG / GRU / SSA / REC / FOR / MAO / IGU / BSB / FLN "Special Taxi" kiosk mafia at every major airport, the cross-city Uber route-padding in Brasília's Plano Piloto grid, the Amazonas regional Uber coordinated scam (documented at Santarém and Alto do Chão), and the late-night Uber-supply-gap that unlicensed remis and mototaxis exploit.*

## Pattern 3: Prices that aren't posted will surprise you later — and "couvert" and "serviço" are the Brazilian specialties

The third pattern is the unposted price, and in Brazil it has three specific forms you need to know by name.

The first form is the classic tourist-trap unposted price. Brazilian beach kiosks, seafood restaurants, jet-ski operators, boat captains, photo stands, and market vendors will sometimes — not always — quote you a price that is deliberately vague. "*Preço de mercado*" (market price). "Special price for you." "Up to you." Each of these phrases hides a later surprise. On Praia do Futuro (Fortaleza) and Boa Viagem (Recife) barracas, a "*camarão ao alho e óleo preço de mercado*" turns into R\$ 280 for a 300g portion the same dish costs R\$ 80 two blocks inland. On Búzios's Rua das Pedras, seafood quoted "per kilo" arrives as 900g of fish billed at R\$ 850. In Manaus, a tambaqui "*preço de mercado*" returns R\$ 300 for a fillet that costs R\$ 120 at the Mercado Adolpho Lisboa two hundred meters away.

The second form is the **couvert artístico** — the "artistic cover charge." This is legitimate in Brazil: a live musician gets paid, the restaurant lists it on the menu, and a fair couvert is R\$ 15–R\$ 35 per person. The tourist-trap version is R\$ 60–R\$ 180 per person for a single guitarist, added silently, and shows up on the bill after dinner. In Paraty's Centro Histórico, in Salvador's Pelourinho, in Foz do Iguaçu's Av. Jorge Schimmelpfeng, and on São Paulo's Vila Madalena strip, the couvert is the overcharge vehicle.

The third form is the **serviço** — the "10% service charge." Here is the rule every traveler in Brazil must know: **Brazilian Federal Law 13.419/2017 makes the serviço explicitly OPTIONAL.** The restaurant may list "*serviço 10% opcional*" on the menu; many do not. You have the legal right to refuse the serviço, particularly for poor service. The manager knows this and will remove it if asked. A citation to the law ("*Lei treze-quatro-dezenove de dois-mil-e-dezessete torna o serviço opcional*") ends the conversation every time.

The defense across all three is a single habit, repeated before every transaction: ask for the price in writing, or point to a posted menu, *before* anything happens. For fish, prawns, or crab by weight, ask to see the scale and get the total in reais confirmed before it is cooked. For couvert, ask "*tem couvert?*" before you sit; if yes, ask the amount; if it is above R\$ 35 per person, stand up and leave. For serviço, photograph the menu page that shows the serviço policy; if the bill adds an unlisted or excessive serviço, refuse and cite the law.

*You will see this pattern in: every Brazilian restaurant chapter in this book — Rio Copacabana, Búzios Rua das Pedras, São Paulo Vila Madalena, Florianópolis Lagoa da Conceição, Salvador Pelourinho + Rio Vermelho, Recife Boa Viagem, Fortaleza Beira Mar + Praia do Futuro, Manaus Ponta Negra, Foz do Iguaçu Centro, Brasília "comida por kg" buffets, and Ouro Preto Centro Histórico.*

## Pattern 4: The damage claim, the cloned card, the "market price" weigh

The fourth pattern is the claim after the fact. You rent something, you buy something by weight, or you hand over a card — and the operator later points at an imaginary or pre-existing scratch, an inflated scale reading, or a cloned-terminal charge, and demands or has already taken money.

The rental-damage variant on Brazilian beach cities runs like this: a jet-ski operator at Praia da Tartaruga (Búzios), a buggy operator at Jericoacoara, or a bicycle rental in Paraty's Centro points at a pre-existing scratch and demands R\$ 800–R\$ 2,500. The defense is the same one that works everywhere in the world: take six to ten date-stamped photos of the item from every angle *before* you accept it, handover a photocopy of your passport rather than the original, keep the original in the hotel safe, and if a damage claim is made at return, produce the photos on the spot.

The **cloned credit-card terminal** variant is specifically Brazilian and deserves a name. Some unofficial taxi-cooperative counters, some beach-kiosk card machines, and some street-vendor stalls run a manual-entry card terminal that records the full card number plus CVV to a second device hidden behind the first. Two weeks later, a fraudulent charge appears from another city. The defense is specific: never let a card out of your sight, never accept a manual-entry terminal ("we'll type the number in" is the tell), pay with contactless or chip-insert only, and if the card "doesn't work on this machine" walk to the next vendor rather than allowing a second attempt on the same terminal.

The **INMETRO-scale weigh** variant is found at the "*comida por kg*" (pay-by-weight) buffets that dominate Brazilian weekday lunch. INMETRO is Brazil's metrology institute, and every commercial scale must carry a current INMETRO certification sticker on the scale itself (look for *Inmetro Calibração 2025* or *2026*). The tourist-trap variant is a scale without the sticker that pads 10–25%. The defense: verify the sticker is visible before you plate, and ask the price per kilogram before you start (legitimate Brasília and São Paulo buffets run R\$ 70–R\$ 110 per kg; tourist-strip buffets run R\$ 110–R\$ 180 per kg for the same food).

*You will see this pattern in: the Búzios and Fortaleza jet-ski and buggy damage claims, the Paraty bicycle-rental damage, the cloned-terminal taxi-counter scam at every major airport, the Praia do Futuro barraca scales, the Brasília "comida por kg" Setor Comercial Sul, and the Ouro Preto soapstone-and-gemstone shops.*

## Pattern 5: An "official" is an official only at an official office

The fifth pattern is the stranger in authority. Real Brazilian police — the Polícia Militar (PM) in blue uniforms, the Polícia Civil in plain clothes with formal ID, the Polícia Federal at airports and borders, and DEATUR tourist police in clearly-badged vests — operate only from official stations and posted checkpoints. They do not conduct on-the-spot "fines" against foreigners for nonexistent violations. They do not ask to search your wallet at a bar. They do not demand cash payable on the spot.

The *faux policier* pattern runs in three forms. The first is the São Paulo Paulista "*old lady blood-pressure*" gambit — a well-dressed older woman approaches a tourist, claims to feel faint, asks for help, and while the tourist's attention is on her, an accomplice lifts the wallet. No official is involved, but the emotional-authority dynamic is the same. The defense is a specific sentence: "*Eu vou chamar o SAMU — cento-noventa-e-dois*" (I will call SAMU — 192). This exposes the scam instantly; a real medical emergency *welcomes* an ambulance call. The second is the Brasília Esplanada dos Ministérios "official tour guide" on Praça dos Três Poderes — a man with a lanyard offers a "guided tour of the Congresso Nacional for R\$ 60." The real Congresso tour is FREE, bookable at *tours.congresso.leg.br*. The third is the Pelourinho "*cortesia*" street-performer who corners a tourist for a "capoeira demonstration" and then demands R\$ 50.

The defense is simple and universal. If anyone on the street identifies themselves as an official or a guide and wants to collect money from you, refuse and walk into the nearest well-lit shop or hotel lobby. For real emergencies, call **190** (Polícia Militar) or **192** (SAMU). For tourist-specific help, DEATUR direct-dial phones are listed in Appendix C for every city covered in this book. A real officer will have no problem with this. A fake one will leave immediately.

*You will see this pattern in: the Paulista "old lady blood-pressure" distraction (São Paulo), the Esplanada "official tour guide" scam (Brasília), the Pelourinho "mandatory guide" pressure (Salvador), the Olinda "mandatory guide" upsell (Recife), the Ouro Preto "mandatory guide" and fake "IPHAN preservation tax" demand, and the general Centro Histórico guide-pressure pattern in every colonial city.*

## Pattern 6: If you pay before you have the thing — and if you pay by PIX — you probably won't get the thing

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch by reading the fine print after you have committed — so this pattern is really about what to pin down before you click *Confirm* or hand over cash. And in Brazil, it has a specific and dangerous modern form: **the PIX transfer**.

**PIX is irreversible.** Brazil's instant-payment system is the fastest and most frictionless way to move money in Latin America — a QR code, a CPF number, or an email address can receive a payment in seconds. The Banco Central do Brasil's consumer-protection mechanism for PIX fraud is the **MED** (Mecanismo Especial de Devolução), which is narrow: it only covers clear fraud cases (identity theft, known-scam accounts), requires reporting within 80 days, and has no equivalent of the 30–60 day Visa / Mastercard chargeback right. In practice: if you PIX-transfer to a scammer, the money is gone.

The scam patterns that exploit this:

You book an "aluguel de temporada" (short-term rental) in Búzios for Réveillon via a Facebook or Instagram post at half the Airbnb rate; the "host" requires PIX deposit to "hold the booking"; the address is a vacant lot; the money is gone. You book a Rio Carnaval apartment at 40% off retail via a WhatsApp contact; the "owner" requires PIX for the deposit; the apartment is double-booked; the money is gone. You book an Amazon jungle lodge in Manaus via a Google ad from an unfamiliar operator; the deposit is via PIX; the lodge doesn't exist; the money is gone. You buy "Fortal Carnaval" front-row tickets from a Facebook Marketplace account at 60% off face value; PIX is the only payment method; the QR code at the gate shows "*inválido*."

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction you make in Brazil. Book accommodation only through platforms that offer pay-through-platform guarantees and dispute resolution (Booking.com, Airbnb, Expedia, Hoteis.com, Decolar) — never by Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, or direct PIX to an unknown CPF. When a host messages "PIX the deposit for a 20% discount," recognize it as the off-platform fraud signal and refuse. Verify any property on Google Street View before you depart. Pay every rental, excursion, tour, or hotel by **credit card** (Visa, Mastercard, American Express, Elo) rather than PIX, debit, or bank transfer — only a credit card gives you a chargeback right, and you can reverse a fraudulent charge within thirty to sixty days. For Carnaval, Réveillon, Rock in Rio, or any major event, book 9–12 months ahead through recognized platforms; the off-platform discount is always a scam, without exception.

### One more: the "commission stop" pattern on day tours

Brazil has its own set of tour-adjacent traps worth knowing. Any guided tour from Manaus that includes an unscheduled stop at a "coconut-sugar factory," a "pink dolphin feeding experience" (now illegal per IBAMA decree 2025), or an "indigenous tribe cultural exchange" (regulated by FUNAI, almost always staged at commercialized Amazônia villages) is almost certainly routing you to a commission-paying partner. Any Fortaleza day-trip to Jericoacoara or Canoa Quebrada that includes a "cachaça tasting stop" or a "*feira de artesanato*" stop has the same structure. This is not an emergency — you can simply decline to buy — but budget thirty extra minutes into your tour for it, and never feel obligated to purchase.

*You will see this pattern in: the Rio Carnaval / Réveillon aluguel-de-temporada fraud, the Búzios and Florianópolis peak-season cancel-and-relist Airbnb scam, the Manaus Amazon jungle-lodge booking fraud, the Ouro Preto Carnaval repúblicas booking fraud, the Fortaleza Réveillon + Fortal Carnaval booking fraud, and the Brasília Independência + Réveillon hotel-overbooking relocation gambit.*
