# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are organized by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — a restaurant with a suddenly inflated bill, a tailor's shop, a jet-ski operator demanding a "damage" deposit, a "Special Taxi" car that has driven you somewhere unexpected, a "Fita do Bonfim" seller escalating with accusations of bad luck — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. A signed credit-card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, dial **190** — the Polícia Militar emergency number. For medical emergencies, **192** reaches SAMU (ambulance dispatch); **193** reaches Corpo de Bombeiros (fire and rescue). For consumer-protection complaints, **151** is the nationwide PROCON hotline.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection (Google Maps dropped-pin is ideal), the business license (in Brazil the *Alvará de Funcionamento* is usually displayed near the entrance), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward. If a pickpocket team is still in the area, walk 100 meters in any direction before you pull out your phone to screenshot.

If your bag or wallet was just stolen in the street, shout — shopkeepers, motoboys, and passing drivers in Brazil will often help, and a visible commotion is the fastest way to draw attention. In Rio's Copacabana, Ipanema, and Lapa, the nearest DEATUR (Delegacia Especial de Atendimento ao Turista — tourist police) station is always within 10 blocks. In São Paulo's Centro Histórico and Avenida Paulista, the DEATUR office is at R. Sá e Souza, 99. In Salvador's Pelourinho, DEATUR is stationed directly in the Praça da Sé area. Tourist-police officers routinely handle foreign-visitor reports in English.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest *DEATUR* office if one is within ten minutes; otherwise, the nearest Polícia Civil station (*delegacia*). In Rio, DEATUR posts exist in Copacabana, Ipanema, Barra da Tijuca, Galeão airport, and Centro. In São Paulo, it's R. Sá e Souza 99 near Paulista. In Salvador, Pelourinho. In Recife, Boa Viagem. In Fortaleza, Beira Mar. In Manaus, Centro. In Brasília, Asa Sul. In Foz do Iguaçu, Centro. In Florianópolis, Centro. Say *"I want to file a report"* or use the Portuguese phrase *"quero registrar um Boletim de Ocorrência"* (KEH-roo heh-jees-TRAR oom boh-leh-CHEEM jee oh-koh-HEN-sya). You will walk out with a *Boletim de Ocorrência* (BO) — the official police-report document stamped with an official seal. This is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

Brazil also runs an **online pre-filing system** for non-violent incidents: *delegaciaeletronica.gov.br* (federal) and each state has its own variant (São Paulo at *delegaciaeletronica.policiacivil.sp.gov.br*; Rio at *delegaciaeletronica.pcivil.rj.gov.br*). Online filing works for stolen-object reports, credit-card fraud, and minor incidents. Serious incidents (assault, robbery with violence, major fraud) must be filed in person at a physical delegacia — and almost always on the same day as the incident; delayed reports raise suspicion and slow the paperwork.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from Brazil and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call. If the charge was a restaurant overcharge, a tailor "deposit," or a jet-ski damage fee, also call your bank's fraud line directly — the number on the back of your card — because fraud desks sometimes freeze faster than the Visa or Mastercard emergency lines. If the card is Brazilian-issued, the major banks (Itaú, Bradesco, Santander, Banco do Brasil, Caixa, Nubank, Inter) all run 24/7 card-loss lines.

**If the scam involved PIX**, the window for recovery is narrower and the mechanism is different. See the dedicated PIX section below.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the BO (Boletim de Ocorrência) reference number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Visa and Mastercard both offer strong chargeback rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend credit over debit and over PIX for every Brazil trip.

Call the PROCON consumer-protection hotline at **151** — Brazilian consumer protection is organized by state, but **151** routes you automatically to the state PROCON covering your current location. PROCON officers handle restaurant overcharging, rental-damage false claims, hotel overbooking, off-platform booking fraud, and "mandatory" guide / tax / tour demands. Filing a PROCON complaint in Brazil has genuine teeth — repeat-offender businesses face fines and operating-license suspensions, and established hotels and tour operators typically settle restitution quickly once a PROCON case is opened.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. US citizens should contact the US Embassy in Brasília or the US Consulates in Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo, Recife, or Porto Alegre. UK citizens can reach the British Embassy in Brasília or the Consulates in Rio and São Paulo. Canadian, Australian, and Irish citizens have embassies or high commissions in Brasília and consular networks in Rio and São Paulo.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the BO reference number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position. The major dispatchers serving the Latin America market — Allianz Global Assistance, AXA Partners, World Nomads, Chubb, Zurich — all operate 24/7 English-language lines for travelers and have local medical-provider networks throughout Brazil. Brazilian private hospitals will typically bill your insurer directly once a *Guarantee of Payment* is in place; public hospitals (SUS) require you to attend but may require you to pay up front and reclaim later if your case is not an emergency.

If the scam involved an off-platform rental or a wire-fraud hotel booking, open the dispute on the booking platform (Booking.com, Airbnb, Expedia, Hoteis.com, Decolar) as well as with your card issuer. Platforms can sometimes recover deposits the card company cannot, and Airbnb in particular runs a dedicated South America fraud team for the Rio Carnaval and Réveillon peak-booking windows.

## If the scam involved PIX

**PIX recovery is narrower than credit-card chargeback.** The Banco Central do Brasil runs a mechanism called **MED** (*Mecanismo Especial de Devolução*) for PIX fraud, but it covers a specific and limited set of cases.

Within the first 80 days, open a MED dispute by calling your bank's fraud line and explicitly requesting "*abertura de MED por fraude*" (opening an MED for fraud). The bank opens a case against the receiving account and — if the receiving account still has funds and meets MED criteria (identity theft, known-scam accounts, or clear misrepresentation) — can claw the money back in 7–10 business days.

MED criteria are specific:

- **Identity theft** — someone used your PIX credentials without authorization.
- **Known-scam account** — the Banco Central's fraud-pattern database has flagged the receiving account.
- **Clear misrepresentation** — the sender can document (screenshots, messages) that the receiving party represented themselves as a different entity (e.g., a fake "Airbnb host") than the actual account holder.

MED does NOT typically cover:

- **Regret** — you PIX'd voluntarily and want it back because the goods didn't arrive or weren't as described. (This requires a civil claim — *Ação de Cobrança* — and is rarely worth the effort for small amounts.)
- **Disputes between parties** — a genuine commercial disagreement.

If your PIX scam involves a clear fraud pattern (disappeared Airbnb, fake Amazon lodge, counterfeit Carnaval tickets, impostor Uber payment request), you have a reasonable MED case. Open it the same day. If the receiving account has already emptied, MED cannot recover what isn't there; file a BO with the Polícia Civil cybercrime unit (*Delegacia de Crimes Cibernéticos*) anyway, because the pattern helps prosecute the scammer even if your funds are gone.

For SIM-swap fraud (criminals transferring your phone number to their SIM to intercept bank codes and PIX authorizations), immediately call your carrier (Vivo, Claro, TIM, or Oi) and request a SIM suspension plus a port-out block. Then call your bank's fraud line before anything else — SIM-swap fraud moves faster than theft recovery, and the first hour is decisive.

## Within the first week

File a formal consumer-protection complaint with the appropriate state **PROCON** via *procon.gov.br* (national gateway) or the specific state portal (São Paulo PROCON at *procon.sp.gov.br*; Rio at *procon.rj.gov.br*; Bahia at *procon.ba.gov.br*). PROCON is the Brazilian analog of the UK's Trading Standards or France's DGCCRF. It does not recover individual losses automatically, but its regulatory actions against repeat-offender businesses are one of the few effective pressures on the scam economy. Tourist-facing businesses know which complaints reach PROCON; a filed complaint frequently produces a restitution offer that the shop refused to make on the day of the incident.

For tour-operator disputes, file a parallel complaint with **CADASTUR** (*cadastur.turismo.gov.br*) — the national tourism-operator registry. CADASTUR keeps the license records of every registered tour guide and operator in Brazil; with the license number or company name (always photograph a tour-office license before booking), CADASTUR can flag the operator, order compensation, and — for repeat offenders — revoke the license. For Amazon-region tours specifically, also file with **AMAZONASTUR** at *amazonastur.am.gov.br* (Amazonas state tourism board) at **+55 92 2123 8800**.

For banking and PIX disputes — a charge your Brazilian bank refuses to reverse, a PIX fraud that MED declined, an ATM skim you consider the bank should cover — escalate to the **Banco Central do Brasil Ouvidoria** at *bcb.gov.br/atendimento/registrardemandabc*, or to the banking-sector ombudsman at **0800 646 2492**. The Banco Central is the financial-sector regulator and its ouvidoria has a public complaint database that publishes bank performance; a recorded Banco Central complaint motivates banks to resolve where a direct-to-bank complaint would stall.

For aviation disputes, **ANAC** (the national civil-aviation agency) runs a passenger complaint portal at *anac.gov.br*. For hospitality disputes above PROCON's reach, the **Ministério do Turismo** runs a tourist ombudsman.

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business's name, the address, the exact transaction details, the BO reference number, the PROCON reference number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps, TripAdvisor, or Reclame Aqui (Brazil's largest consumer-complaint platform — *reclameaqui.com.br*). Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name and location only. Brazilian defamation law (both civil and criminal) can be strict — stick to verifiable facts and receipts.

## If your phone or SIM was stolen

Call your carrier immediately to suspend the line. The four major Brazilian carriers — **Vivo** (dial **1058**), **Claro** (dial **1052**), **TIM** (dial **1056**), and **Oi** (dial **10331**) — all operate 24/7 stolen-device lines. Suspending the line prevents the thief from using your number to receive SMS two-factor-authentication codes, from initiating PIX transfers on your phone, and from running up international-roaming charges. If you are on a tourist eSIM (Airalo, Saily, Nomad), the app will let you suspend the line in a few taps; do that before the BO.

File a BO at the nearest delegacia (or online at *delegaciaeletronica.gov.br*) and include your phone's **IMEI** number (the fifteen-digit serial; find it on the original box, in your Apple or Google account, or by dialing \*#06# *before* the theft). The IMEI lets police flag the device for the national *CEMI* (*Central de Gerenciamento de Móveis Inservíveis*) recovery system, and is required for any insurance claim.

If your SIM was swapped — criminals transferring your number to intercept bank codes and PIX authorizations — this is time-critical. Call the bank's fraud desk before anything else. Then file both the carrier-suspension and a *Boletim de Ocorrência* as cybercrime. Brazilian SIM-swap fraud peaked in 2023–2024 and has fallen since carriers added ID-verification requirements, but the attack vector remains active.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking, drug-planting, or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of 24/7 hospitals with English-speaking staff in each city is in Appendix C. At the hospital, ask for a toxicology screen (*"exame toxicológico, por favor"*); Brazilian private hospitals such as Hospital Albert Einstein (São Paulo), Hospital Sírio-Libanês (São Paulo / Brasília), Hospital Copa D'Or (Rio), Hospital Português (Salvador), Hospital Santa Joana (Recife), and Hospital Check-up (Manaus) all run these routinely and will print a report you can use as evidence.

For drug-planting scams — where a stranger, a bar staffer, or occasionally someone in a vest or uniform demands a roadside test or "finds" a substance in your possession — stay calm, do not resist, and call **190** (Polícia Militar) before any money changes hands. Real narcotics cases in Brazil go through official processes with written documentation, photographed evidence, a *flagrante delito* report you sign, and a mandatory visit to the delegacia. Demands for cash to "make it go away" are the signature of a scam, not of legitimate enforcement.

In Brazil, the medical-emergency number is **192** (SAMU, national ambulance dispatch). For fire, dial **193**. For police, dial **190**. For any incident involving a foreign tourist, the nearest DEATUR office is the first stop — direct-dial phones for all twelve cities in this book are listed in Appendix C.

If you are inside a venue and drink-spiking is suspected, have a friend call SAMU *before* you leave, not after — sometimes the aggressive bill-pressure escalates once the scammer realizes you are leaving.
