# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are organized by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — a tea house, an "art student" gallery, a jade shop, a taxi that has detoured, or a hotpot restaurant with a suddenly inflated bill — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including credit-card receipts. A signed slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, dial **110** (Public Security Bureau — the single emergency-police number, 24/7 across mainland China). The dispatcher may only speak Mandarin; be prepared to say "*Yīngyǔ, please*" — "English, please" — or to repeat "*wǒ bèi piàn le*" (我被骗了, "I was scammed"). If you are in a major city tourist area, the nearest PSB will dispatch an English-capable officer.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection (a WeChat-shared location or a screenshot of your phone's GPS is ideal), the shop's business-license certificate (营业执照, *yíngyè zhízhào* — every Chinese retail business is legally required to display this in a frame), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward.

If your bag or wallet was just stolen in the street, shout *xiǎotōu* (小偷, "thief"). Mainland Chinese bystanders will often intervene — pickpocketing is socially unacceptable and shopkeepers near tourist zones are trained to respond.

If you are in a subway station, the **station supervisor's office** (站长室, *zhànzhǎng shì*) is the closest on-site authority; they can call PSB and hold CCTV footage for you.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest **派出所** (*pàichūsuǒ*, neighborhood police station) — every hutong, scenic area, and major shopping street has one, with signage in Mandarin and usually English. Say *"wǒ yào bào'àn"* (我要报案, "I want to file a report"). You will walk out with a printed, stamped case-report document (案件回执单, *ànjiàn huízhí dān*) bearing a case number — this is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

For **tourism-specific complaints**, you can also call the **China National Tourism Administration's tourist-assistance hotline** at **12301**. This is a dedicated line staffed by English-speaking operators who can arbitrate tourism disputes, contact the scam venue on your behalf, and document the case in the national tourism complaint database. 12301 does not replace a PSB report but it is often faster for tourism-specific cases and can produce restitution from travel agencies, tour operators, and registered scenic-area businesses that PSB cannot.

**Freeze the card that was used.** International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from mainland China but note two caveats:

1. **The Great Firewall blocks many bank websites.** Your bank's **mobile app** (Chase, AmEx, Capital One, HSBC, Citi) usually works without a VPN; the website usually does not. Install and log in to your bank's app *before* you land in China.
2. **Some international toll-free numbers do not connect from mainland China.** Use the direct non-toll-free fallback number printed on the back of your card rather than the 1-800 collect line.

Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call. If the charge was a tea-house bill or a jade-shop "deposit," also call your bank's fraud line directly to freeze the card.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

**File a chargeback with your card issuer.** Use the PSB case-report number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Visa and Mastercard both offer strong chargeback rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend credit over debit for every mainland China trip.

Call **12301** (China National Tourism Administration) and **12345** (Chinese citizen-service hotline, which does take foreign complaints for scams outside tourism) to open a case. Both are English-capable and record case numbers. These are the two most effective regulatory escalation paths for scams by registered businesses in China.

**For scams in Shanghai**, the Shanghai Municipal Tourism complaint hotline is **+86 21 962 020**. **For Beijing**, the Beijing Municipal Bureau of Culture and Tourism complaint line is **+86 10 12301**.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. US, UK, Australian, Canadian, and Irish citizens have embassies in Beijing and consulates in Shanghai, Guangzhou, Chengdu, and Shenyang — full list in Appendix C.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the PSB case-report number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position. The major dispatchers serving the Asia-Pacific market — Allianz Global Assistance, AXA Partners, World Nomads, Chubb, Zurich — all operate 24/7 English-language lines for travelers and have local medical-provider networks throughout mainland China.

If the scam involved an off-platform booking (a WeChat image of a price, a direct Alipay transfer to an individual account for a tour or hotel), open the dispute on the original booking platform (Trip.com, Ctrip, Booking.com, Airbnb) as well as with your card issuer.

## Within the first week

**File a formal consumer-protection complaint** with the **State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR)** at **12315** — China's equivalent of the US FTC consumer-protection hotline. SAMR does not recover individual losses directly but its regulatory actions against repeat-offender tea houses, jade shops, tour operators, and "art galleries" are one of the few effective pressures on the scam economy. Tourist-facing businesses know which complaints reach SAMR; a filed complaint sometimes produces a restitution offer that the shop refused to make on the day of the incident. SAMR's website (**samr.gov.cn**) has an English complaint-filing form.

For **transport overcharging** — licensed taxis, tuk-tuks-equivalent three-wheelers, and registered ride-hail drivers — file a parallel complaint with the municipal Transportation Commission:

- Beijing: +86 10 12328
- Shanghai: +86 21 12345
- Guangzhou: +86 20 12345
- Other cities: dial **12328** (national transport-complaints hotline)

With the license-plate number (always photograph it before getting out), these commissions can identify the driver, issue fines, and sometimes produce compensation.

For **banking and payment disputes** — a charge your Chinese bank refuses to reverse, an ATM skim, a disputed Alipay or WeChat Pay transfer — escalate to the **People's Bank of China Financial Consumer Protection Bureau** at **+86 10 12363**. The PBOC is the financial-sector ombudsman.

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business's name, the address, the exact transaction details, the PSB case-report number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on **Dianping** (大众点评, the Chinese Yelp) and on TripAdvisor or Google Reviews if you can access them. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name and location only. Chinese defamation law is strict; stick to verifiable facts.

## If your phone or SIM was stolen

**Call your carrier immediately to suspend the line.** The three major mainland carriers: **China Mobile** (dial **10086**), **China Unicom** (dial **10010**), **China Telecom** (dial **10000**). All operate 24/7 stolen-device lines. If you are on a tourist eSIM (Airalo, Saily, Holafly, Nomad), the app will let you suspend the line in a few taps — do that before the PSB report.

**File a 报案 (case report)** at the nearest PSB with your device's **IMEI** number (the fifteen-digit serial; find it on the original box, in your Apple or Google account, or by dialing \*#06# *before* the theft). The IMEI lets police flag the device and is required for any insurance claim.

**Lock and wipe remotely** via Apple's Find My or Google's Find My Device — these Google/Apple services are blocked on the Great Firewall but work on your **home-country network** or over a VPN. Do this before filing the PSB report.

**Change passwords immediately** for any account whose app was installed on the phone: email, banking, social media. Start with email; everything else resets off it.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking or physical harm

**Go to a hospital before you go to the police.** Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of 24/7 hospitals with English-speaking staff in each city is in Appendix C. At the hospital, ask for a **toxicology screen** (毒理筛查, *dúlǐ shāichá*); Chinese international-capable hospitals (Beijing United Family, Shanghai United Family, Ruijin Hospital, Huashan Hospital, Peking Union Medical College Hospital) run these routinely and will print a report you can use as evidence.

For **drug-planting scams** — where a stranger claims to have "found" a substance in your possession — stay calm, do not resist, and dial **110** before any money changes hands. Real narcotics cases in China go through official processes with written documentation, photographed evidence, and a case-report document you sign. Demands for cash to "make it go away" are the signature of a scam, not of legitimate enforcement.

**Emergency numbers in mainland China:**
- **110** — Public Security Bureau (police)
- **120** — ambulance / medical emergency
- **119** — fire
- **122** — traffic accident
- **12301** — China National Tourism Administration tourist-help
- **12345** — citizen-service hotline (general complaints, English-capable in most cities)

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate a replacement. Consular registration — STEP for US citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, LOCATE for UK, ROCA for Canadians — is worth doing before you travel, specifically for situations like this.
