# Appendix A: Mandarin Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in mainland China. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, followed by the **simplified Chinese characters** (the written form used in all mainland signage and phones), a **Pinyin** romanization (including tone marks), and a practical pronunciation guide in italics.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. You can also copy the Chinese characters to a note on your phone and show them on screen when you cannot say the phrase aloud — this works reliably in every mainland interaction. Pointing at a phone screen is the single most effective communication tool a non-Mandarin-speaking traveler has.

## A note on pronunciation

Mandarin is a **tonal language**. Every syllable has one of four tones (plus a neutral tone), and the same sounds pronounced with different pitch contours mean different words — *mā* (mother, high level), *má* (hemp, rising), *mǎ* (horse, falling-rising), *mà* (scold, falling). Carrying every tone correctly is genuinely hard for adult learners. For defensive phrases, you don't need to — most Chinese listeners will infer meaning from context, and showing the characters on your phone is always the fallback.

A few pointers that will get you 80 percent of the way:

- **Pinyin vowels**: *a* is "ah," *e* is "uh" (schwa-like), *i* is usually "ee," *o* is "oh," *u* is "oo."
- **Pinyin "q"** is "ch," **Pinyin "x"** is "sh," **Pinyin "zh"** is "j," **Pinyin "c"** is "ts," **Pinyin "z"** is "dz."
- The tones on pronunciation marks: ˉ (high), ˊ (rising), ˇ (falling-rising), ˋ (falling).
- Stress is even — every syllable carries roughly equal weight, unlike English.
- A soft trailing *r* is a Beijing dialect feature (北京儿化音) — you can ignore it; Beijingers will understand you without it.

## A note on politeness

Mandarin politeness is shorter than Japanese or Thai. A single *qǐng* (请, please) at the start of a sentence, or *xièxie* (谢谢, thank you) at the end, is enough to flag you as polite. There is no grammatical politeness-particle system you must memorise.

For approaches you want to end: **"bù yào, xièxie"** (不要,谢谢) — "don't want, thanks" — is the single most useful phrase in this appendix. It is calm, firm, polite, and understood everywhere.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**  
多少钱?  
Duōshǎo qián?  
*Duo-shao chyen?*

**Please show me the menu.**  
请给我看菜单。  
Qǐng gěi wǒ kàn càidān.  
*Ching gay wo kahn tsai-dahn.*

**Do you have a Chinese-language menu?**  
有中文菜单吗?  
Yǒu zhōngwén càidān ma?  
*Yo jong-wun tsai-dahn ma?*

Critical in Yangshuo, West Street, and any tourist-menu restaurant where foreign-language menus carry a markup. Pointing at the Chinese menu typically brings prices down 30-50%.

**Please turn on the meter.**  
请打表。  
Qǐng dǎ biǎo.  
*Ching dah byow.*

Say this the instant you get into any taxi. If the driver refuses, get out. Alternatively, use DiDi — it is cheaper, meters itself, and has a built-in English interface.

**Is there a service fee?**  
有服务费吗?  
Yǒu fúwùfèi ma?  
*Yo foo-woo-fay ma?*

Crucial at tea houses, hotpot restaurants, and anywhere a bill arrives with unexpected line items. A legitimate venue will point at the posted menu where the fee is disclosed.

**What is the per-gram price for tea / pearls / jade?**  
每克多少钱?  
Měi kè duōshǎo qián?  
*May kuh duo-shao chyen?*

Use before any weighing begins. For tea: weigh dry, not after water is added. For pearls/jade: ask to see an independent certificate (独立鉴定证书, *dúlì jiàndìng zhèngshū*), not a shop-issued one.

**Do you accept foreign credit cards?**  
你们接受外国信用卡吗?  
Nǐmen jiēshòu wàiguó xìnyòngkǎ ma?  
*Nee-mun jee-show why-guo shin-yong-kah ma?*

In 2026, major Chinese retailers accept Visa/Mastercard through WeChat Pay or Alipay Tour mode. Small vendors may be cash-only.

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**  
不要,谢谢。  
Bù yào, xièxie.  
*Boo yow, shyeh-shyeh.*

The single most useful phrase. Firm, polite, understood everywhere. Walking away is not rude.

**I'm not interested.**  
我不感兴趣。  
Wǒ bù gǎn xìngqù.  
*Wo boo gan shing-chu.*

Stronger than *bù yào*. Use when an approach keeps talking after the first refusal.

**Please leave me alone.**  
请让我一个人。  
Qǐng ràng wǒ yí gè rén.  
*Ching rahng wo yee guh run.*

Used firmly but without raised voice. The word *ràng* (让) literally means "allow / let."

**Don't touch me.**  
别碰我。  
Bié pèng wǒ.  
*Byeh pung wo.*

Step back. Most touts disengage at this point.

**I'm waiting for my friend.**  
我在等朋友。  
Wǒ zài děng péngyǒu.  
*Wo dzai dung pung-yo.*

Useful when an English-speaking pair tries to walk with you — gives you a socially-acceptable reason to stand still and wait them out.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I'm calling the police.**  
我要报警。  
Wǒ yào bàojǐng.  
*Wo yow bow-jing.*

This is the firmest card in the deck. **110** is the single emergency-police number across mainland China, 24/7. Say the phrase, hold up your phone, and dial 110. Most scam operators back down the moment they see the call connect.

**I'll call my bank.**  
我要打电话给我的银行。  
Wǒ yào dǎ diànhuà gěi wǒ de yínháng.  
*Wo yow dah dyen-hwa gay wo duh yin-hahng.*

Say this if a tea house, shop, or taxi driver disputes a credit-card transaction. Calling your card issuer from the table signals that the charge is going to be disputed.

**I was scammed. Help me please.**  
我被骗了,请帮助我。  
Wǒ bèi piàn le, qǐng bāngzhù wǒ.  
*Wo bay pyen luh, ching bahng-joo wo.*

The verb *bèi piàn* (被骗) — "to be scammed, to be cheated" — is the specific Mandarin term. It communicates what happened with more urgency than any English translation. Use this with a PSB officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, or any bystander.

**I'd like an itemized bill, please.**  
请给我详细账单。  
Qǐng gěi wǒ xiángxì zhàngdān.  
*Ching gay wo shyang-shee jahng-dahn.*

Asking for *xiángxì zhàngdān* (详细账单, "detailed bill") forces the establishment to account for every item. A legitimate place will hand it to you without complaint. A tourist trap will stall.

**I didn't order this.**  
我没有点这个。  
Wǒ méi yǒu diǎn zhège.  
*Wo may yo dyen juh-guh.*

Use calmly about any unrequested dish, bottle, or "service" on your bill.

**This is not what we agreed.**  
这不是我们约好的。  
Zhè bú shì wǒmen yuē hǎo de.  
*Juh boo shuh wo-mun yweh how duh.*

For taxi fares over a quoted price, tour-operator price-creep, and "private boat" upcharges.

## Getting help

**Where is the nearest police station?**  
最近的派出所在哪里?  
Zuìjìn de pàichūsuǒ zài nǎlǐ?  
*Dzway-jin duh pie-choo-swor dzai na-lee?*

*Pàichūsuǒ* (派出所) is the neighborhood police station — the Public Security Bureau's local branch. Every hutong, street, and tourist scenic area has one.

**I want to file a report.**  
我要报案。  
Wǒ yào bào'àn.  
*Wo yow bow-ahn.*

The word *bào'àn* (报案) is the specific verb. You will walk out with a printed, stamped case report — your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand it.

**Is there an English speaker?**  
有会说英语的人吗?  
Yǒu huì shuō Yīngyǔ de rén ma?  
*Yo hway shwo ying-yu duh run ma?*

Most major PSB stations in tourist cities have at least one English-speaking officer; major hospitals have English-speaking patient coordinators.

**Please call 110.**  
请打110。  
Qǐng dǎ yāo yāo líng.  
*Ching dah yow-yow-ling.*

Numbers in emergency context: 110 is spoken "*yāo yāo líng*" (yāo is a variant of yī, "one"). Every Chinese adult recognises 110.

**Help! Thief!**  
救命!小偷!  
Jiùmìng! Xiǎotōu!  
*Jo-ming! Shyow-toe!*

Shouted in a crowded street, this is understood by every bystander. The word *xiǎotōu* (小偷) carries legal weight.

**I am lost.**  
我迷路了。  
Wǒ mílù le.  
*Wo mee-loo luh.*

Show the Chinese name of your destination on your phone. Most strangers will either point you the right way or walk with you.

## Reading Chinese yuan amounts

Chinese prices are written with "¥" (the Renminbi symbol) or "元" (yuán, the spoken unit). Chinese also uses "块" (kuài, colloquial for yuán — think "buck"). Decimal convention is a dot (3.50 元 = three yuán fifty fēn).

| Amount | Characters | Pinyin | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 yuan | 一块 / 一元 | yī kuài | *yee kwai* |
| 5 yuan | 五块 | wǔ kuài | *woo kwai* |
| 10 yuan | 十块 | shí kuài | *shur kwai* |
| 20 yuan | 二十块 | èrshí kuài | *urr-shur kwai* |
| 50 yuan | 五十块 | wǔshí kuài | *woo-shur kwai* |
| 100 yuan | 一百块 | yì bǎi kuài | *yee bai kwai* |
| 500 yuan | 五百块 | wǔ bǎi kuài | *woo bai kwai* |
| 1,000 yuan | 一千块 | yì qiān kuài | *yee chyen kwai* |

- Small-denomination coins are rarely used in 2026 — almost everything is paid digitally or with paper notes.
- Paper notes in circulation: 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 yuán. The largest note is 100 RMB, which means paying in cash at scale involves a lot of notes — another reason foreign travelers are pushed toward WeChat Pay or Alipay Tour mode.
- One US dollar has typically traded around 7.0-7.2 RMB in recent years; verify on xe.com in the week before you travel.

If a vendor quotes a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it:

**Please write the number down.**  
请写下数字。  
Qǐng xiě xià shùzì.  
*Ching shyeh shyah shoo-dzuh.*

## A note on tone (conversational, not Mandarin tones)

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In China, polite firmness works better than raised voices. Chinese conflict-resolution culture strongly rewards calm — raising your voice at a tea-house manager or a taxi driver often turns a recoverable situation into an unrecoverable one. A calm *bù yào, xièxie* is already a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *wǒ bèi piàn le* ("I was scammed") and *wǒ yào bào'àn* ("I will file a report") are both firm without being rude, and *wǒ yào bàojǐng* ("I'm calling the police") is the firmest card in the deck.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to point at your phone, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar — and a calm *bù yào, xièxie* with the Chinese characters on your screen is, in itself, proof you know enough Mandarin that the interaction is going to be harder than they expected.
