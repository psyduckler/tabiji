# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The ninety-eight scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: A friendly English-speaker who approaches you first is almost certainly running a script

This is the single most important habit you can take to mainland China. At scenic sites, most ordinary Chinese passersby will not start English conversations with foreign tourists — the language barrier alone makes it unusual. When someone with unexpectedly fluent English — often a pair or trio of young, well-dressed people — steps into your path near the Forbidden City, the Bund, Xi'an's Muslim Quarter, Chengdu's Jinli Street, or the Great Wall access villages and asks where you are from, the interaction that follows is a prepared script. You are not being greeted. You are being sorted.

The **Wangfujing art-student** version runs in Beijing: two young women who "studied art" invite you to a "free student gallery exhibition" around the corner; the "exhibition" is a commercial gallery where a patient salesperson will keep you for an hour until you buy a 3,000-RMB print of a "student piece." The **Shanghai Bund and Nanjing Road tea-ceremony** version sends a smiling pair to invite you "to practice English" at a traditional Chinese tea house nearby; the bill arrives at 3,000–10,000 RMB total for four tiny cups of tea each and you cannot leave without paying. The milder **Yu Garden** variant, clustered around the Shanghai Bazaar, typically runs 800–1,200 RMB per person rather than the Bund price. The **Xi'an Muslim Quarter "auntie"** version offers a guided tour of the lanes that ends at a single calligraphy or jade shop with a pre-negotiated kickback. The **Chengdu "panda-volunteer" invitation** sells a fake behind-the-scenes experience. The **Guilin "Li River private-boat"** variant quotes a price on the dock that doubles mid-trip. None of these interactions start with you asking for help. That is the tell.

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: when any stranger starts a conversation with you in English at a famous site in mainland China, reply "*bù yào, xiè xie*" — 不要,谢谢 — "no thanks" — and keep walking. Do not break stride. Do not let them walk with you. If they follow, step into the nearest hotel lobby or large chain store (Starbucks, KFC, a mainland-brand supermarket) and use the staff as a buffer. Walking away is not rude. The interaction was not a social invitation.

*You will see this pattern in: the Wangfujing art-student scam (Beijing), the Nanjing Road English-practice invitation (Shanghai), the Bund tea-ceremony ambush (Shanghai), the Muslim Quarter auntie-tour (Xi'an), the Jinli Street panda-volunteer sell (Chengdu), the Shamian Island calligraphy tour (Guangzhou), the Shilin Stone Forest "guide" (Kunming), the Zhangjiajie-park "student photographer" approach, and almost every tout scene in the 98 scam entries that follow.*

## Pattern 2: "The meter is broken" and the DiDi-that-cancels-for-cash

The second pattern is taxis. Mainland China has strict taxi regulation — meters are mandatory in every licensed city cab, and the DiDi (滴滴) ride-hail app is the de-facto replacement for street-hailing in every large city. But at airports, rail stations, and heavily-marketed tourist zones, a meaningful share of foreign-visitor rides begin with the driver refusing the meter and quoting a flat price, or a DiDi driver asking you to cancel the ride and pay cash instead.

At **Beijing Capital International Airport (PEK)** and **Beijing Daxing (PKX)**, the regulated flag-down is ~13 RMB and a typical run to central Beijing is 90–140 RMB metered — the flat-rate touts inside the terminal quote 300–400. At **Shanghai Pudong (PVG)**, the metered fare into the Bund is about 180 RMB; the "black car" flat-rate is 400–600. At **Xi'an North Railway Station**, DiDi is cheap and abundant; illegal minibus drivers lurking outside still ask for 200 RMB flat to hotels that would cost 35 metered. In Chengdu, Guangzhou, and Kunming the same pattern recurs at every major gateway.

The "meter is broken" phrase — in Mandarin, "*jìjiàqì huài le*" (计价器坏了) — is the specific tell. Meters are not broken. Refusing the meter is refusing to charge the legal price.

The **DiDi cash-out variant** is subtler: you book a DiDi, the driver calls and asks you to cancel because "the platform takes 30%" and offers to run it for the same price in cash. What actually happens is that you forfeit the app's GPS record, the legal receipt, the no-meter guarantee, and the post-ride complaint route — and the driver takes a scenic detour that doubles the fare.

The defense is short and universal. Take a taxi only from an **official rank** (at airports, railway stations, and any hotel with a doorman) where a supervisor is present. Say "*qǐng dǎ biǎo*" (请打表) — "please start the meter" — before the car moves. If the driver refuses, get out. Use **DiDi** in every city, and **never cancel to pay cash** — if the driver pressures you to cancel, cancel the ride and book again (the app flags drivers who repeatedly do this). DiDi's foreign-card mode works with Visa, Mastercard, and Amex; set it up before you land.

*You will see this pattern in: the PEK/PKX/PVG flat-rate quote (Beijing/Shanghai), the Guangzhou Baiyun Airport detour, the Chengdu Shuangliu freelance taxi, the Kunming Changshui rail-station tout, the Chongqing Longtousi 300-RMB "special rate," and the DiDi cash-out variant in every major city.*

## Pattern 3: Prices that aren't posted will surprise you later

The third pattern is the unposted price. Chinese "old street" scenic zones (*gǔ jiē*, 古街), tea houses, scenic-area ticket booths, and night-market seafood stalls will sometimes — not always — quote you a price that is deliberately vague. "Special." "Market price." "We'll see at the end." "Menu without prices." Each of these phrases hides a later surprise.

This pattern accounts for a meaningful share of the overcharging scams in this book. On Beijing's **Houhai** and **Wangfujing** night-food lanes, skewers priced "per stick" arrive as a single bill of 600 RMB because the "set" was priced separately. In Shanghai's **Yu Garden Bazaar**, tea canisters sold "by weight" turn out to be weighed after pressing water into the leaves to triple the mass. At **Xi'an's seafood-and-noodle** riverfront, the fish is priced *per jīn* (500 g); your "small" carp arrives at 1.8 jīn and 1,400 RMB. **Guilin and Yangshuo** cormorant-fishing "shows" on the river quote one price on the dock and add a "performance fee" on the water. **Lijiang** Naxi-style tea houses advertise "free entry" and then present a 400-RMB per-person minimum.

The defense is a single habit, repeated before every transaction: ask for the price in writing, or point to a posted menu with numbers. For fish by weight, watch it get weighed on a visible scale and get the total in RMB confirmed before it is cooked. For tea, ask the per-gram price and the weight *before* it is packaged. For any "show" or "experience" with an unposted price, agree the price in written characters on your phone screen — most hotel staff and police reports will accept a screenshot of your own WeChat conversation as evidence.

*You will see this pattern in: the Wangfujing food-street skewer surprise (Beijing), the Yu Garden tea-weight trick (Shanghai), the Xi'an riverside seafood per-jīn trap, the Houhai lantern-price escalation, the Chongqing hotpot "service charge" item, the Guilin private-boat mid-trip raise, the Lijiang tea-house minimum, and the Yangshuo cormorant-show upcharge.*

## Pattern 4: The "student gallery," the "tea ceremony," the "art dealer"

The fourth pattern is the **gallery-and-tea industrial complex** — the single largest scam category in mainland China by dollar value and the only one sophisticated enough to employ multi-person rehearsed scripts. It deserves its own red-flag pattern because it is so consistent across cities and so fast at extracting money from well-meaning visitors.

The choreography is almost identical in Beijing, Shanghai, Xi'an, Chengdu, and — more recently — Chongqing and Guangzhou. (1) A pair of well-dressed young people, almost always English-speaking and warm, approaches you at a major scenic site. (2) They introduce themselves as art or calligraphy or tea students, or as being "on the way home from class," and invite you to "a small gallery" or "a traditional tea ceremony" or "a student exhibition" nearby. (3) They walk you to a narrow side-street venue within ten minutes' walking distance. (4) Inside, a patient, older "teacher" or "tea master" serves you, explains symbolism, shows you pieces or cups, and builds rapport. (5) At the end, a soft pitch produces a catalog or price list with numbers that are two to ten times any reasonable retail equivalent — 3,000 RMB for a print, 800–1,200 RMB for four cups of tea, 5,000 RMB for a "hand-painted" scroll. (6) Card readers in the venue are "reliable" and "international," and once your card is charged, the chargeback process faces the language barrier and the absence of any signed dispute.

The defense is refusal at step one. If you did not ask to visit an art gallery or a tea house, do not follow anyone to one. It does not matter how warm or sincere the invitation feels. The entire script is designed to feel warm and sincere.

*You will see this pattern in: the Wangfujing art-student gallery (Beijing), the Nanluoguxiang and Liulichang calligraphy studios (Beijing), the Bund and Nanjing Road tea-ceremony scam (Shanghai), the Tianzifang art-student invitation (Shanghai), the Muslim-Quarter calligraphy shop circuit (Xi'an), the Jinli and Kuanzhai Alley tea-tasting (Chengdu), the Shamian Island and Beijing Road gallery pitch (Guangzhou), and the Lijiang Old Town "tea master" scene.*

## Pattern 5: A "certificate" is meaningful only at a real office

The fifth pattern is the fake-certificate scam. Real Chinese Public Security Bureau (PSB, 公安局) officers — in blue and black uniforms, with visible police identification — do not approach foreign tourists on the street to inspect visas or cash or phones. Real tourist-police booths exist at major scenic zones and at airports; they have permanent signage in English and Chinese. Real **jade**, **tea**, **silk**, and **medicinal** products sold to tourists at reputable shops carry proper registration documentation from China's Administration for Market Regulation (AMR), and any claims of "imperial palace certification" or "World Heritage workshop" are almost always fabrications.

The pattern runs in two forms. The first is the **fake-police check**: a man in a vaguely uniform-like jacket tells a solo traveler that his or her visa registration is "incomplete" and demands a cash "fine" to avoid a trip to the PSB. The second is the **fake-product certificate**: a shop produces laminated certificates "authenticating" a 20-RMB jade bracelet as an imperial-era piece at 8,000 RMB. Both work on the same instinct — the authority-of-a-stamp psychology that the real Chinese state apparatus can produce — and both collapse the instant you calmly say "*wǒ yào qù jǐngchá jú*" (我要去警察局) — "I will go to the police station with you."

The defense is simple and universal. If anyone on the street identifies themselves as an official and wants to examine your wallet, passport, phone, or visa, refuse and walk into the nearest large chain store (Starbucks, KFC, a branded supermarket), a hotel lobby, or a scenic-area tourist-service booth. Dial **110** (Public Security Bureau — the single emergency-police number across mainland China, 24/7) from your phone and put the call on speaker if the person has not already left. For "certified" jade, tea, silk, or calligraphy, never buy anything where the "certificate" comes from the shop itself — real certification comes from an independent appraisal institute and the shop will not have a physical certificate on premises for a specific piece.

*You will see this pattern in: the Tiananmen fake-visa-check script (Beijing), the Xi'an Muslim Quarter "imperial jade" certificate (Xi'an), the Shanghai Jiuguang antiques-district silver-stamp scam, the Chongqing Ciqikou "tea-master certification," the Guilin "Li River scenic-fee" fake officer, and the pervasive "government-approved workshop" shop banner across every tourist quarter.*

## Pattern 6: If you pay before you have the thing, you probably won't get the thing

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch by reading the fine print after you have committed — so this pattern is really about what to pin down before you click *Confirm* or hand over cash.

You book a **Great Wall tour** online, pay full price via a screenshot-heavy WeChat conversation, and the "operator" turns out to be a freelance WeChat account that intercepts you with a different driver for a different site. You book a **Huangshan or Zhangjiajie scenic-area package** on a Chinese travel site using Google Translate, and the package's "gondola fee," "scenic-area ticket," and "park bus" were not actually included. You hand 500 RMB to a street agent at **Xi'an Bell Tower** for a Terracotta Warriors tour that turns out to be a minibus with four kickback-shop stops. You agree to a **Guilin/Yangshuo Li River private-boat** on the pier, and the "private boat" becomes a group raft an hour into the trip.

All of these work because you committed before you knew the terms — and then the terms, the vendor, the venue, or the platform were not what you thought they were.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction you make in mainland China:

- **Book accommodation** only through platforms that offer pay-through-platform guarantees and English-language dispute resolution (Trip.com, Agoda, Booking.com, Expedia) — never by a WeChat-forwarded image of a price, never by bank transfer to a personal Alipay account. Many small Chinese inns are technically not permitted to accept foreign visitors; the OTA will filter these out for you.
- **Pay every rental, excursion, or hotel** by credit card (Visa, Mastercard, American Express) through an app that accepts foreign-card billing — DiDi for rides, WeChat's or Alipay's Tour/International mode for shops and food, a recognized OTA for hotels and trains. Only a credit card gives you a chargeback right that works from abroad.
- **For Great Wall, Forbidden City, Terracotta Warriors, and Zhangjiajie tickets**: buy only through the official booking platform of the site or a top-tier OTA (Trip.com, Klook, GetYourGuide). Street-tout prices are two to five times the official rate and the tour may not include what the tout said it did.
- **For high-speed rail**: use **12306** (the official site — 12306.cn) or Trip.com. Any street agent selling train tickets at a station is taking a 50–200 RMB markup per seat.
- **For domestic flights**: use Trip.com or Ctrip's English site; never buy a flight from a walking salesperson at an airport.
- **For Li River (Guilin) and Three Gorges (Chongqing) cruises**: buy only through the cruise company's official website or a top-tier OTA. Pier-side touts are selling downgraded or duplicated tickets.

### One more: the VPN-and-payment traps

Mainland China has its own digital-safety complications. If you cannot reach your bank's anti-fraud website because of the Great Firewall, freezing a stolen card takes longer; install your bank's app on your phone *before* you land (the apps work without a VPN, the websites generally don't). If you rely on a VPN and it drops, any foreign service you were mid-transaction on may hang — never pay a deposit while mid-VPN-drop. And the **AliPay/WeChat Pay "Tour" modes** occasionally glitch for foreign cards; have an alternative (cash in small denominations, a second foreign card) as backup.

*You will see this pattern in: the Great Wall fake-tour WeChat scam, the Huangshan "all-inclusive" hidden-fee package, the Xi'an Terracotta Warriors minibus kickback circuit, the Guilin private-boat bait-and-switch, the Zhangjiajie gondola-fee surprise, the Lijiang Old Town hotel-not-what-you-booked, and the all-platform "WeChat deposit" fraud that hits every mainland destination.*
