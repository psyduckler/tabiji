# Appendix C: Emergency Contacts {-}

Every phone number in this appendix was verified current at the time of publication, but phone numbers do change, consulates move, and hospitals occasionally renumber their switchboards. If a number below fails to connect, search the organization's name plus *中国* (Zhōngguó, China) or the city name, and you will almost always find the current line on the first page of results.

**Before you travel**, we strongly recommend you double-check your own embassy or consulate's current number directly on its official website — the US State Department's travel.state.gov, the UK's gov.uk, Canada's travel.gc.ca, Ireland's dfa.ie, Australia's smartraveller.gov.au. Save the main switchboard and the after-hours duty-officer line on your phone before departure, and screenshot this appendix so you can read it on the high-speed rail or in a hotel room where your signal drops. Even a verified number can become a wrong number between editions.

A note on **the Great Firewall**: Google, Gmail, Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, most Western news sites, and many foreign bank websites are not reachable on the public Chinese internet. Most of this appendix's numbers work without a VPN, but websites referenced here (consulate sites, tourism-administration portals) may require a VPN to reach from inside mainland China. Before you land: install your bank's mobile app (apps usually work without a VPN), save consular numbers as phone contacts, and bookmark official PSB and 12301 pages in an offline browser.

## The numbers that work everywhere in mainland China

These are the lines to dial from any phone — mobile or landline, Chinese SIM or roaming — anywhere in the country. All of them are free.

- **Public Security Bureau (police):** **110**. The single emergency-police number, 24/7 across mainland China. English-capable in all Tier-1 cities and at tourist sites in Tier-2 / Tier-3 cities. If the first operator does not speak English, repeat "*Yīngyǔ*" (English) or "*wǒ bèi piàn le*" (I was scammed).
- **Medical emergency / ambulance:** **120**. National ambulance dispatch. English available at major dispatch centers in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou; elsewhere the first operator may be Mandarin-only.
- **Fire:** **119**.
- **Traffic accident:** **122**.
- **China National Tourism Administration (CNTA) tourist-help hotline:** **12301**. 24/7, English-capable. For disputes with registered tour operators, hotels, ticket resellers, and scenic-area businesses. Files complaints in the national tourism complaint database.
- **Citizen service hotline:** **12345**. English-capable in most Tier-1 cities. For general complaints outside tourism (transportation, shops, consumer services).
- **Consumer protection (SAMR):** **12315**. State Administration for Market Regulation's consumer-complaint line. For fake goods, product-authenticity disputes, merchant misrepresentation.
- **Transportation complaints (nationwide):** **12328**.
- **People's Bank of China financial-consumer protection:** **+86 10 12363**.

## Card-issuer emergency lines

When you call, have your card number (or the last four digits), the approximate transaction amount, and the time of the fraudulent charge ready. **Most international toll-free numbers (starting 1-800) do not connect from mainland China.** Use the direct (non-toll-free) number — usually printed on the back of your card — and dial it as a standard international call.

- **Visa Global Customer Assistance (collect from China):** +1 303 967 1096. 24/7, English.
- **Mastercard Global Service (collect from China):** +1 636 722 7111. 24/7.
- **American Express International Service Center (collect):** +1 336 393 1111. 24/7. Amex China local line: +86 21 3864 3333.
- **Discover Global Customer Service:** +1 801 902 3100.

If your card was issued by a bank (not directly by Visa, Mastercard, or Amex), the fastest route is the number printed on the back of the card. Save this number in your phone *before* you travel — hunting for a number on a stolen card is impossible.

## If you lose your passport

1. **Report it first** — go to the nearest 派出所 (PSB neighborhood station) and file a 报案 for a lost or stolen passport. You will leave with the signed, stamped case-report document. This is the document your embassy wants to see before issuing an emergency replacement.
2. **Call your embassy's main switchboard** — or its after-hours line on weekends — and request an emergency passport appointment. US, UK, Canadian, Irish, and Australian embassies in Beijing and consulates in Shanghai/Guangzhou/Chengdu/Shenyang all issue same-day or next-day emergency travel documents.
3. **Bring** the PSB case report, any photo ID you still have (a driver's license photo on your phone counts), passport photos (photo booths are in most subway stations and at the basement of large department stores), and the payment method your embassy specifies.
4. **Visa-status regularisation**: before flying out with an emergency travel document, you must visit the **Exit and Entry Administration Bureau** (出入境管理处, *chūrùjìng guǎnlǐ chù*) in your city to record your exit status. Your embassy will provide the address.

## If your phone is stolen

1. **Freeze the SIM** by calling your carrier from any other phone: China Mobile **10086**, China Unicom **10010**, China Telecom **10000**. If you use a foreign SIM, call your home carrier's international line. Tourist eSIMs (Airalo, Saily, Holafly, Nomad) can be suspended in-app.
2. **File a 报案** at the nearest PSB with your device's IMEI (if you recorded it) and serial number.
3. **Lock and wipe remotely** via Apple Find My or Google Find My Device — requires a VPN or home-country network.
4. **Change passwords immediately** for any account whose app was installed on the phone. Start with email.

## If your credit card is stolen or skimmed

1. **Freeze the card** by calling the number on the back (or use the issuer's app's "lock card" feature). Every minute matters.
2. **Report to PSB** with a 报案. Insurance reimbursement and the card issuer's fraud-dispute process both want the case-report number.
3. **Dispute the charges** with your issuer. The PSB case report speeds the process.
4. **Watch for follow-on phishing** — a skimmed card often leads to SMS and WeChat phishing pretending to be your bank. Never click a link in these messages; go to your bank's app directly.

## A closing note

This appendix is meant to be read once on the plane, screenshot for offline use, and forgotten about for the rest of your trip. Phone numbers change. Consulates move. Hospitals renumber their switchboards. If any number in this list fails, **110** — the Public Security Bureau — will still work, and your embassy's website will still have the current line for your country (reachable via VPN or from your home network before you travel).

Travel safely, travel calmly, and if the worst happens, remember: a 2,000-RMB tea-house bill is an inconvenience, not a crisis. Filing a 报案, freezing a card, and calling an embassy are ordinary procedures — PSB officers and consular staff in mainland China handle these cases every day, and most travelers resolve everything within a few hours and return to their trip. China is not a dangerous country. Help is close, and English is closer than you think.
