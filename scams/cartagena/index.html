The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Rose Seller Pressure Scam
🔶 Medium
📍 Plaza Santo Domingo, Plaza de Bolivar, Torre del Reloj, restaurants in the Walled City, Getsemaní neighborhood

Rose sellers in Plaza Santo Domingo and Plaza de Bolívar place a flower in your companion's hand or on your dinner table without invitation, then turn back and demand 10,000–30,000 pesos ($3–8) — refusing causes a loud scene, and team-based versions also lift unattended phones during the distraction.

The hunting ground is the Walled City's restaurant plazas during dinner hours: Plaza Santo Domingo, Plaza de Bolívar, the area around Torre del Reloj, and the Getsemaní bar street. A woman approaches your outdoor table holding a small bunch of roses. Before you can say anything, she places one on the table or hands it directly to your companion, smiles warmly, says it is a gift, and starts to walk away. Three steps later she turns back and asks for payment. The vendors operate in the same plazas day after day and are well-known to restaurant staff, but few establishments actively intervene.

The mechanic is engineered social pressure. Once a rose is in your partner's hand or on your dinner table, refusing requires you to be the one creating a public scene — and most diners pay 10,000–30,000 pesos rather than cause one. The team-based variant adds a second layer: while the rose seller occupies you, an accomplice walks past the table and lifts an unattended phone, sunglasses, or small bag. Aggressive versions involve following diners for blocks after they decline, or escalating with shouting that draws additional vendors to your table.

Say "no, gracias" firmly and clearly before any rose can touch your table or your companion's hand — and do not accept the flower even briefly to "look at it." If a rose is already on your table, set it back in the seller's hand or on the ground; you are not obligated to pay for an item you did not agree to buy. Keep phones and wallets off the tabletop in any Walled City plaza, especially when a vendor approaches. Choose restaurants with indoor seating or those known to keep vendors out of their dining areas. If a seller becomes aggressive, alert restaurant staff or call 123 — the Policía de Turismo patrols the Walled City and responds quickly to incidents in the dining plazas.

Red Flags

  • A vendor approaches your table and places a flower without being asked
  • The seller hands the flower directly to your companion rather than asking if you want to buy
  • No price is mentioned until after the flower has changed hands
  • The seller becomes loud, aggressive, or refuses to take the item back when you decline
  • A second vendor or person appears nearby while your attention is on the rose seller

How to Avoid

  • Say no firmly and immediately before any flower touches your table or hands; do not accept it even briefly.
  • If a flower is placed on your table, put it back in the seller's hand or on the ground; do not feel obligated.
  • Keep your phone and wallet off the table in restaurants, especially when approached by vendors.
  • Choose restaurants with indoor seating or those known to keep vendors away from dining areas.
  • If a seller becomes aggressive, alert restaurant staff or nearby police; Policia de Turismo patrol the Walled City.
Scam #2
Fake Police Shakedown
⚠️ High
📍 Getsemaní neighborhood, side streets off the Walled City, areas near nightclubs in the Walled City, Bocagrande at night

Men in plain clothes in Getsemaní and on Walled City side streets flash badges, claim to be undercover narcotics police, and demand to inspect your wallet for "counterfeit currency" — they pocket bills during the count or stage a drug-offer setup minutes earlier to demand a cash "fine," and the US Embassy explicitly warns about this scam.

The encounter typically happens after dark on Getsemaní's quieter blocks, on side streets just off the Walled City, around the nightclub strip in the Walled City, or in Bocagrande at night. Two men in civilian clothes step in front of you, flash a laminated badge too quickly to inspect, and announce they are undercover narcotics police running counterfeit-currency checks or drug screenings. When you hesitate, they escalate the authority — "we can detain you at the station if you don't cooperate." The US Embassy in Colombia explicitly flags this pattern and advises travelers to insist on going to a real station if stopped.

The mechanic is the wallet hand-off. The standard play: they examine your wallet to "verify" your cash, palm a few large bills during the count, and return the wallet a few hundred dollars lighter with a fake "receipt." The aggressive variant claims to find something suspicious and demands an on-the-spot cash fine to avoid arrest. The most sophisticated variant is the staged drug offer — a street vendor offers you drugs first, you decline or hesitate, and the "police" arrive thirty seconds later accusing you of attempted purchase and demanding payment to make the matter go away. The vendor and the officers are the same operation.

Real Colombian police never inspect tourists' wallets on the street — any officer who asks is running a scam, and the right response is "I will go with you to the nearest station to verify." Genuine Policía de Turismo wear clearly labeled vests, will produce ID for inspection, and will never demand on-the-spot cash. Carry only a passport photocopy on the street; keep the original and the bulk of your cash in the hotel safe. If a staged drug offer happens, do not panic — you have done nothing wrong by declining a stranger's offer. Dial 123 (Colombia emergency) on your phone in clear view of the men; fake ones will leave. For losses, file at any CAI station or at policia.gov.co.

Red Flags

  • Men in plainclothes flash badges and claim to be undercover police on a quiet street
  • They ask to see your wallet and want to inspect your cash, not just your identification
  • A street vendor offered you drugs or something suspicious moments before the police appeared
  • The officers demand an immediate cash fine and refuse to issue a written citation
  • They become threatening when you suggest going to the nearest police station

How to Avoid

  • Never hand your wallet to anyone on the street; you may show identification but keep physical control of it.
  • Insist on going to the nearest police station to verify their identity; real officers will agree without hesitation.
  • Carry a photocopy of your passport and leave the original in your hotel safe; this satisfies most legitimate ID checks.
  • If approached after a staged drug offer, do not panic; the entire encounter is scripted and you have done nothing wrong.
  • Call 123 (Colombia emergency) or contact the tourist police if you feel threatened.
Scam #3
Scopolamine Drink Spiking
⚠️ High
📍 Nightclubs and bars in the Walled City, Getsemaní bar scene, Bocagrande nightlife area, dating app meetups throughout the city

Scopolamine ("burundanga" or "Devil's Breath") spiked into drinks at Walled City and Getsemaní bars or at dating-app-arranged meetups produces memory loss and extreme compliance — victims hand over ATM PINs and hotel safe codes voluntarily, then wake hours later with everything gone, and the US Embassy specifically warns tourists about this threat.

The drug is the same scopolamine running through Bogotá's bar-spike scene; in Cartagena, the hotspots are nightclubs in the Walled City, the Getsemaní bar street, and the Bocagrande nightlife strip, plus dating-app meetups that move to specific bars. Organized rings use attractive women on Tinder and Bumble who target male tourists with expensive-looking profiles. One documented case: a traveler matched on Tinder, met for drinks at a bar she chose, and woke the next morning in his hotel room with the safe emptied, his phone gone, and no memory after the second drink. The Cartagena Explorer site and multiple traveler forums document the same template repeatedly.

The mechanic is the drug's pharmacology. Scopolamine causes anterograde amnesia and extreme suggestibility — under its influence, victims cooperate with requests to enter PINs, unlock phones, open safes, and authorize banking-app transfers without resistance and without later memory of doing so. The dose can be delivered in a drink, on the rim of a glass, on a business card, or blown as a powder during a handshake. The accomplice's job is to be sufficiently warm and credible that you accept moving venues, accept a second drink, and accept her presence in your room. Colombian authorities treat these crimes seriously but prosecution is difficult precisely because victims cannot identify their attackers.

Watch every drink poured from the bottle to the glass, never accept a drink out of your sight, and never bring a dating-app match to your hotel room on a first meeting. Be especially cautious of attractive strangers who steer the night to a specific bar they "know." Keep your hotel safe code and ATM PIN to yourself regardless of how warm the conversation has become. If you suddenly feel disoriented after light alcohol, tell bar staff immediately, dial 123, and get to a hospital — scopolamine has a treatment window. For losses, freeze cards via your issuer's 24-hour fraud line, file at the nearest CAI, and contact the US Embassy at +(57)(1) 275-2000 (or your home country's embassy in Bogotá).

Red Flags

  • An unusually attractive stranger is overly eager to meet you through a dating app or approaches you aggressively at a bar
  • Someone you just met insists on ordering drinks for you or handles your drink when you are not looking
  • A new acquaintance suggests moving to a specific bar or location they choose rather than a popular public venue
  • You suddenly feel extremely disoriented, confused, or compliant after consuming only one or two drinks
  • Your date is paying unusual attention to your phone, wallet, or hotel room details early in the conversation

How to Avoid

  • Never leave your drink unattended and never accept a drink that you did not watch being prepared and poured.
  • Be extremely cautious with dating apps; meet only in well-known public venues and tell someone your plans.
  • Do not bring dates or new acquaintances to your hotel room, especially on the first meeting.
  • Keep your hotel safe code and ATM PIN to yourself; never share these with anyone regardless of the situation.
  • If you suddenly feel unusually intoxicated after little alcohol, immediately alert bar staff, call 123, and get to a hospital.

Scam #4
Taxi Overcharging Without Meters
🔶 Medium
📍 Rafael Núñez International Airport, Walled City exits, cruise port area, Bocagrande hotel zone, outside nightclubs

Cartagena taxis don't use meters, which makes every fare a negotiation — drivers at Rafael Núñez Airport quote 60,000 pesos for runs that should cost 15,000–25,000, and outside nightclubs after midnight the overcharge climbs to 3–5× the standard rate, with cruise-port taxis mixing unlicensed cars into the official rank.

The overcharge zones are Rafael Núñez International Airport arrivals, the Walled City exits at Torre del Reloj and India Catalina, the cruise port at Manga, the Bocagrande hotel zone, and outside Walled City and Getsemaní nightclubs after 11 PM. The minimum legal fare for 2025 was set at 10,800 COP. The standard rates: Walled City to Bocagrande, 10,000–15,000 COP; airport to Old Town, 15,000–25,000 COP; cruise port to Walled City, 12,000–18,000 COP. A driver at the airport curb quoting 60,000 for the airport-to-Walled-City run is asking for triple the standard fare and counting on tourist ignorance of the legal rate.

The mechanic is structural. With no meter, the entire price is verbal, which means the driver has total control over the dispute at the destination. Three variants run consistently. First, the upfront overquote: he simply names a price triple the standard rate and shrugs at negotiation — take it or leave it — counting on your luggage and time pressure. Second, the bait-and-switch: he agrees to 20,000 pesos before departure, then claims at arrival the price was 40,000 and you misunderstood due to the language barrier. Third, the long-route detour: he takes an unfamiliar back-route through neighborhoods you don't know, claiming traffic, and the bill grows with the ride length.

Open Uber, DiDi, or InDriver in the airport arrivals hall before you exit the terminal — every app shows GPS-tracked, fixed pricing in advance, removing every meter variable. If you must take a taxi from the rank, agree on the fare verbally before you load luggage and confirm the number twice ("dos cero mil pesos" — "20,000 pesos"). Save the standard rates as a screenshot on your phone: airport to Old Town 15,000–25,000, Walled City to Bocagrande 10,000–15,000. Carry small bills so you can pay the exact agreed amount. Have your hotel call a registered taxi for evening returns rather than hailing on the street. For overcharges, photograph the taxi number and report at policia.gov.co or call 123.

Red Flags

  • The driver does not agree to a fare before you get in or quotes a price far above standard rates
  • The taxi has no visible license, taxi number, or official markings
  • The driver claims the fare is higher upon arrival than what was agreed before departure
  • Someone aggressively solicits you for a taxi ride inside the airport or cruise terminal rather than at the official stand
  • The driver takes an unfamiliar route that seems longer than necessary for the distance

How to Avoid

  • Always agree on the fare before entering the taxi and confirm it verbally in front of a witness if possible.
  • Know standard rates in advance: Walled City to Bocagrande is 10,000 to 15,000 COP; airport to Old Town is 15,000 to 25,000 COP.
  • Use ride-hailing apps like Uber or InDriver which show upfront pricing and GPS-tracked routes.
  • Ask your hotel to arrange airport transfers or recommend trusted taxi drivers with set rates.
  • Carry small bills so you can pay the exact agreed fare; drivers may claim they have no change for large bills.
Scam #5
Beach Service Ambush
🔶 Medium
📍 Playa Blanca (Isla Barú), Bocagrande beach, Castillogrande, beaches on Isla del Rosario day trips

Beach vendors at Playa Blanca and Isla del Rosario open with a "free" ceviche sample and a sip of rum, then escalate through massages, hair braids, and cocktails without quoting prices — the bill at departure runs 300,000+ pesos ($75) for services you never explicitly ordered, and the islands give you no escape because boats leave on a schedule.

The trap zones are Playa Blanca on Isla Barú, Bocagrande beach, the beaches around Castillogrande, and the Isla del Rosario day-trip beaches. The classic case: a couple lounges on rented chairs at Playa Blanca; a vendor approaches with a free sample of ceviche and a taste of local rum framed as friendly hospitality. Within an hour, more food has arrived, a massage has happened, hair-braid accessories have been added — none of it priced before delivery. The vendors operate as a coordinated group across the same beach, so the food vendor, the massage vendor, and the accessory vendor are all part of the same ecosystem. Cartagena's beach-vendor problem is the single most-cited scam on TripAdvisor's Cartagena forum.

The mechanic is the anchoring sequence and the captivity. Start with something framed as free, escalate gradually through services the buyer would normally refuse if priced upfront, and present the total only after consumption — when refusing to pay creates a public confrontation and a real risk of physical pressure. On Playa Blanca and the Rosario islands the captivity is structural: the boat back to Cartagena leaves on a schedule, there is nowhere to go in the meantime, and disputed beach bills happen in the vendor's home territory with no police presence. Some couples have reported $100+ bills for cocktails and snacks they never explicitly ordered.

Decline every "free" sample, taste, and unsolicited service on Cartagena beaches firmly and immediately — nothing on the beach is free, and accepting one item opens the door to the entire sequence. Before accepting any food, drink, massage, or service, ask "cuánto cuesta?" and get a verbal price you both confirm out loud. Bring your own snacks and drinks for beach days at Playa Blanca and the Rosarios. Book island day trips through reputable operators (Bona Vida, Las Islas Hotel) who include lunch in the package and brief you on vendor pressure. If presented with an outrageous bill, negotiate firmly to roughly half — the prices are inflated and bargaining is expected — and report aggressive vendors via the Policía de Turismo at +57 5 660 0011.

Red Flags

  • A vendor offers a free sample, a complimentary taste, or a free massage on the beach
  • Services or food keep arriving without prices being discussed or agreed upon
  • You are on an island beach with limited ability to leave if a dispute arises
  • Multiple vendors approach in sequence as if coordinated, offering different products and services
  • The vendor only mentions the total price after all services have been provided

How to Avoid

  • Decline all free samples and unsolicited services firmly and immediately; nothing on the beach is truly free.
  • Before accepting any food, drink, massage, or service, explicitly ask for the price and agree to it verbally.
  • Bring your own snacks, drinks, and supplies to beach outings to reduce dependence on vendors.
  • Book Isla del Rosario and Playa Blanca trips through reputable tour operators who include lunch in the package.
  • If confronted with an outrageous bill, negotiate firmly; the prices are inflated and vendors expect bargaining.
Scam #6
Friendship Bracelet and Wrist Grab
🔶 Medium
📍 Plaza Santo Domingo, Torre del Reloj (Clock Tower), old city walls, Getsemaní neighborhood streets, outside major churches in the Walled City

Friendly young men near Torre del Reloj and Plaza Santo Domingo grab your wrist while talking about "Colombian friendship tradition," tie a woven bracelet on in under three seconds, then demand 20,000–50,000 pesos ($5–12) — variants include henna tattoos started before any price is agreed and "free" landmark photos that come with payment demands.

The hotspots are Torre del Reloj (the Clock Tower), Plaza Santo Domingo, the area along the old city walls, the Getsemaní bar streets, and outside the major churches in the Walled City — all places where tourists naturally pause and pose for photos. A young man approaches with a warm smile, opens with a friendly comment about Colombian friendship traditions, and reaches for your wrist while still talking. The bracelet is secured in under three seconds. The same vendors operate the same spots day after day; travel forums describe them as a fixture of the Walled City pause-points.

The mechanic is speed plus social discomfort. The tie is deliberately practiced — by the time you fully process what is happening, the bracelet is already on. The price demand follows immediately: 20,000 to 50,000 pesos, with aggression if you refuse. The scam exploits the natural reluctance to refuse someone who has already given you something physical, and the difficulty of removing a tightly woven bracelet without scissors. Variants include henna tattoos started on your hand before a price is agreed (you cannot remove the partial drawing, so payment for the "completed" design is demanded), and "let me take your photo with the wall" framings where the photographer demands payment after the shot.

Keep both hands at your sides or in your pockets when walking through Torre del Reloj, Plaza Santo Domingo, and the Walled City pause-points — physical distance is the entire defense. Say "no, gracias" loudly and clearly the moment a stranger reaches toward your wrist; do not engage in conversation or slow down, because the sellers rely on stopping you. If a bracelet has been tied on against your will, you owe nothing — cut it off and walk away. Do not let strangers take your phone "to help with a photo" near the Clock Tower. Travel in pairs in these zones so one person can watch for the wrist grab. For aggressive incidents, dial 123 or contact the Policía de Turismo on +57 5 660 0011.

Red Flags

  • A stranger reaches for your wrist or hand while talking about friendship or local culture
  • The person begins tying or attaching something to you before you agree to anything
  • No price is discussed upfront; money is only mentioned after the bracelet is secured
  • The seller singles you out from a crowd and makes strong physical contact quickly
  • The seller becomes aggressive, loud, or follows you when you try to walk away without paying

How to Avoid

  • Keep your hands at your sides or in your pockets when walking through areas known for bracelet sellers.
  • Say no clearly and firmly before anyone can touch your wrist; physical distance is your best defense.
  • If a bracelet is tied on you against your will, you are not legally obligated to pay; remove it and walk away.
  • Do not engage in conversation or slow down when approached; these sellers rely on getting you to stop moving.
  • Travel in pairs when walking through the Walled City so you can watch out for each other.
Scam #7
Fake Emerald Jewelry Swindle
🔶 Medium
📍 Jewelry shops in the Walled City, Centro Comercial Getsemaní, street vendors near San Pedro Claver Church, shops near the cruise terminal

Walled City and cruise-port jewelry shops sell synthetic, treated, or lacquered stones as natural Colombian emeralds at fine-stone prices — one TripAdvisor buyer paid $800 for a "Colombian emerald" ring that appraised at under $100, and sophisticated bait-and-switch versions swap the stone you saw for a lower-grade replacement during the boxing process.

The shops cluster inside the Walled City near San Pedro Claver Church, in the Centro Comercial Getsemaní, and around the cruise terminal at Manga, where stepping-off-ship tourists are easy marks. Colombia is the world's largest emerald producer and shops know visitors want to take home a piece of that heritage. The problem is that many tourist-area shops sell synthetic, color-treated, or low-grade stones at prices that suggest they are fine natural emeralds, with impressive storefronts and pushy sales tactics masking the actual quality. The TripAdvisor case file: an $800 ring that appraised at home for under $100.

The mechanic comes in two flavors. The simpler version is straight quality misrepresentation — the stone is synthetic, oiled, fracture-filled, or lacquered (a thin coating of green lacquer on the underside dramatically deepens apparent saturation), but you cannot tell without a loupe and gemological training. The sophisticated version is the bait-and-switch during boxing: the dealer shows you a genuine high-quality stone, you negotiate, you pay, and during the moment of distraction at packaging he swaps the stone you saw for a lower-quality or synthetic equivalent. By the time the appraisal happens at home, the shop owner claims you must have swapped it yourself. A perfectly clear emerald without visible inclusions ("the garden") is almost always synthetic.

Buy emeralds in Cartagena only from shops that provide independent third-party certification from a recognized lab (GIA, Gubélin, or Colombia's CDTEC) — and watch the entire boxing process so the stone never leaves your sight. Inspect the stone under magnification before you pay; a real emerald should have visible inclusions (the garden), and a perfectly clear stone is a red flag. Research price floors before shopping: a fine one-carat Colombian emerald starts at $1,500+. Decline shops that street touts or guides "lead you to" — they earn commission and the markup is on you. For losses, dispute the charge with your card issuer if you paid by card; cash purchases are unrecoverable but report the shop at policia.gov.co.

Red Flags

  • A shop pushes you to buy immediately, creating urgency with claims about special prices or limited availability
  • The emerald appears perfectly clear without any natural inclusions or imperfections
  • The shop cannot provide independent third-party certification (GIA, Gubélin, or CDTEC)
  • A street vendor or guide led you to a specific shop and seems to be earning a commission
  • The price seems too good for the supposed quality, or the dealer dramatically reduces the price during bargaining

How to Avoid

  • Only purchase emeralds from shops that provide independent certification from recognized laboratories like GIA or CDTEC.
  • Inspect the stone under magnification before and after purchase; natural emeralds should have visible inclusions (called the garden).
  • Never allow the stone out of your sight during packaging; watch the entire boxing process carefully.
  • Research standard prices for Colombian emeralds of specific carat weights and quality grades before shopping.
  • Consider purchasing from government-regulated establishments recommended by your hotel or the Cartagena tourism board.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Policía Nacional de Colombia / Policía de Turismo station. Call 123 (emergency) / 112 (national line). Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at ADenunciar (Colombian National Police Online Report - Spanish only).

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

File a police report (denuncia) at the nearest CAI (police station) or through ADenunciar online. Contact your embassy: the U.S. Embassy in Bogotá (+57-601-275-2000) can coordinate with the honorary consular agent in Cartagena. Bring a passport photo and any identification for an emergency travel document. The process typically takes one to three business days.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

The tourist areas of Cartagena, including the Walled City, Getsemaní, and Bocagrande, are generally safe during the day and into the early evening. Tourist police (Policia de Turismo) patrol the main areas and are identifiable by their labeled vests. The primary risks are petty scams (overcharging, bracelet sellers, vendor pressure), drink spiking in nightlife settings, and opportunistic theft. Violent crime against tourists is rare but not unheard of. Most visitors who exercise normal precautions have a safe and enjoyable trip.
Stay within the Walled City, Getsemaní, Bocagrande, Manga, and Castillogrande. Avoid the neighborhoods of Nelson Mandela, Olaya Herrera, and El Pozzón, which are residential areas with higher crime rates and no tourist infrastructure. Even within safe zones, be cautious on quiet side streets in Getsemaní after midnight. The area immediately outside the Walled City walls can be sketchy at night, particularly near the India Catalina statue and along the road toward La Boquilla.
Uber operates in Cartagena and is generally the safest transportation option because it provides GPS tracking, upfront pricing, and a digital record of your trip. InDriver is another popular app where you can negotiate fares. Both are safer than hailing random taxis on the street. Note that Uber operates in a legal gray area in Colombia, so drivers may ask you to sit in the front seat and may not display an Uber sticker.
If you suspect drugging, call 123 (Colombia emergency line) immediately and get to the nearest hospital. Clínica Boca Grande and Hospital Universitario del Caribe are the main facilities. Do not go back to your hotel alone. Alert someone you trust. File a police report (denuncia) even if you do not remember details, as toxicology tests can confirm the substance. Contact your embassy for assistance, especially if your passport or important documents were stolen.
Cartagena taxis do not use meters; fares are negotiated. Standard rates: within the Walled City or Getsemaní, 8,000 to 12,000 COP; Walled City to Bocagrande, 10,000 to 15,000 COP; airport to Walled City, 15,000 to 25,000 COP; Walled City to La Boquilla, 20,000 to 30,000 COP. Late-night fares (after midnight) are typically 20 to 30 percent higher. Always agree on the fare before entering the vehicle and have the exact amount ready in small bills.
