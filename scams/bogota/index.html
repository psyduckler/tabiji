The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Scopolamine Drink Spiking
🟢 Low
📍 Zona T (Zona Rosa) in Chapinero, bars in Teusaquillo, La Candelaria nightlife, Parque 93 area

Female accomplices in Chapinero's Zona T bars dose tourists' drinks with scopolamine ("burundanga"), then walk them through ATM withdrawals while they stay disoriented and compliant — Bogotá logged 1,409 scopolamine poisoning episodes in 2023, a decade record, and the "La 57" gang running this template was dismantled in 2024.

The hunt zone is Zona T (Zona Rosa) in Chapinero, the Teusaquillo nightlife strip, La Candelaria after dark, and the bars around Parque 93. The classic case: two Brazilian tourists met a friendly English-speaking woman at a Zona T bar, she suggested moving to a second bar she knew, drinks arrived, and within 30 minutes both men were disoriented and compliant. They woke up in their hotel the next morning with watches, phones, laptops, wallets, and $8,000 in cash gone, and bank records showing ATM withdrawals at four different machines through the night. The Colombian National Police dismantled the "La 57" gang in 2024 — the operation used female accomplices and corrupted waitstaff to dose drinks across cheap Chapinero and Teusaquillo bars.

The mechanic is the drug. Scopolamine, locally called burundanga or "Devil's Breath," produces memory loss and extreme suggestibility — victims walk to the ATM, enter their PIN, and authorize transfers without resistance and without later memory of doing so. The dose is delivered in a drink, on the rim of a glass, or as a powder blown across a face during a handshake. The accomplice's job is to be sufficiently warm and credible that you accept moving venues, accept a second drink, and accept her presence in your hotel room. Bogotá's 2023 total of 1,409 documented poisoning episodes is almost certainly an undercount because many victims have no memory of the event and report only the loss of property the next day.

Never accept a drink poured out of your sight, and watch the bartender prepare every drink you order from the bottle to the glass. Decline invitations from strangers in bars to move to a second venue "they know," especially in Chapinero and Teusaquillo. Stay in well-reviewed Zona T and Parque 93 bars rather than the cheaper side-street venues where La-57-style operations have run. Travel in groups, designate a sober watcher, and never leave a friend alone with a new acquaintance. If you suspect dosing, dial 123 (Colombian emergency) immediately and go to a hospital — scopolamine has antidotes if administered within hours. File at policia.gov.co and contact your embassy.

Red Flags

  • An attractive stranger is unusually eager to join you or invites you to a specific bar they choose
  • Your drink tastes slightly off, bitter, or different from what you ordered
  • You feel suddenly disoriented, confused, or excessively compliant after just one or two drinks
  • The person suggests leaving your current venue for somewhere 'better' that they know
  • A waiter or bartender seems to be coordinating with the person who approached you

How to Avoid

  • Never accept drinks from strangers — buy your own and watch the bartender prepare them the entire time.
  • Never leave your drink unattended, even to go to the bathroom — order a fresh one when you return.
  • Be extremely cautious with people who approach you in bars and want to take you to a different venue.
  • Stick to established, well-reviewed bars in Zona T and Parque 93 rather than unknown or cheap bars.
  • Go out in groups and designate one person to stay alert — never leave a friend alone with a stranger.
Scam #2
Fake Police Wallet Inspection
⚠️ High
📍 La Candelaria, Avenida Jiménez, around Transmilenio stations, Chapinero side streets

Two men in civilian clothes flash police-looking badges in La Candelaria, claim they're checking for counterfeit currency, and demand to inspect your passport and wallet — they return the wallet $300 lighter with a fake "receipt" telling you to visit a police station to exchange "suspect" bills.

The encounter typically happens on the side streets of La Candelaria, on Avenida Jiménez between the Emerald Trade Center and the Gold Museum, around TransMilenio stations after dark, and on quieter Chapinero blocks. Two men in civilian clothes approach, flash badges that look official but won't bear close inspection, and announce they're plainclothes officers running a counterfeit-currency check. World Nomads and other safety guides flag this as one of Bogotá's most reported tourist scams. Variations include fake "fines" issued on the spot for fabricated infractions and demands to inspect your backpack contents.

The mechanic is the wallet hand-off. They demand to see your passport and your wallet simultaneously to "check for marked bills." One man counts your cash while the other holds you with questions; the count includes a few bills disappearing into his sleeve. They return the wallet with a hand-written "receipt" and tell you to visit a police station to exchange the "suspect" bills — the receipt buys time before you actually count what's left. By the time you check at your hotel, $300 is missing. The variant with backpack inspection works the same way: one officer keeps you talking while the other rummages through your daypack and pockets a phone or camera.

Colombian police never ask to inspect tourists' wallets or cash on the street — any "officer" who asks is running a scam. If plainclothes men flash a badge and demand financial inspection, say in Spanish or English: "I will go with you to the nearest CAI to verify," and dial 123 on your phone. Real officers will agree; fake ones will leave. Carry only a photocopy of your passport's photo page (the original belongs in the hotel safe), keep the bulk of your cash in a money belt, and walk toward a busy commercial street if you feel pressured. For losses, file at any CAI station or at policia.gov.co.

Red Flags

  • Plainclothes individuals claim to be police and flash badges without letting you examine them closely
  • They ask to inspect your wallet, cash, or credit cards for 'counterfeit money'
  • They issue a 'receipt' and tell you to visit a police station — creating a delay before you check your money
  • The encounter happens on a quiet street with few witnesses
  • They become aggressive or threatening if you hesitate or ask questions

How to Avoid

  • Colombian police never ask to inspect your wallet or cash on the street — this is always a scam.
  • Carry a photocopy of your passport and leave the original in your hotel safe.
  • If stopped by plainclothes people claiming to be police, insist on going to the nearest CAI (police station) to verify.
  • Call 123 (police emergency) on your phone while saying 'I want to verify your identity at the police station.'
  • Walk toward a busy commercial area if you feel threatened — scammers avoid scenes with many witnesses.
Scam #3
Phone and Bag Snatching ('No Dar Papaya')
⚠️ High
📍 TransMilenio buses and stations, La Candelaria streets, Carrera Séptima, Usaquén market area

Phone-snatchers at TransMilenio stations grab phones from texting tourists during the door-chime crowd surge; bicycle thieves slash crossbody bags on Carrera Séptima — Colombians call this lifestyle "no dar papaya" (don't give papaya, don't make yourself an easy target), and Bogotá robbery rates have risen sharply since 2020.

The hotspots are TransMilenio stations during peak hours, the length of Carrera Séptima, the gathering points around La Candelaria and Plaza Bolívar, and the Usaquén Sunday market. The phone snatch is the most common version: a tourist at chest height texting on the platform, the bus doors open, the crowd surges, and the phone is gone in under two seconds into the boarding mass. The bag slash is the second version: a bicycle thief on Carrera Séptima rides up parallel to a pedestrian, slashes the bottom of a crossbody bag with a small blade, and pedals off with whatever falls out. Both are documented persistently and increasing in frequency since 2020 according to official Bogotá crime data.

The mechanic is exposure plus crowd cover. The thief needs a visible target (a phone in your hand, a bag without a chest strap, a wallet in a back pocket) and a moving crowd to disappear into within four seconds. TransMilenio gives both — the door chime is a built-in signal that crowd density is about to spike. Carrera Séptima gives the bicycle equivalent: a pedestrianized stretch where a slow-rolling bike attracts no attention and can pull away through a parallel side street within ten seconds. The expression "no dar papaya" captures the local discipline: don't display the thing that can be taken.

Keep your phone in a deep front pocket on every Bogotá street and at every TransMilenio platform — never use it while walking and never hold it at chest height waiting for a bus. Wear a bag across your chest, zipper side facing your body, ideally under a jacket or cardigan. On TransMilenio, hold bags in front of your stomach and avoid 7–9 AM and 5–7 PM peak. Do not place phones on outdoor café tables along Carrera Séptima or near Parque Bolívar. If you must check directions, step inside a shop or a bank lobby first. For losses, file at the nearest CAI within 24 hours for insurance purposes; emergency dispatch is 123.

Red Flags

  • You are using your phone visibly on the street or near a TransMilenio station
  • Someone on a bicycle or motorbike is riding slowly close to the sidewalk, watching pedestrians
  • You are in a crowded area where a thief could grab and disappear into the mass of people
  • Your bag is hanging loosely on one shoulder or behind you where you cannot see it
  • You are sitting at an outdoor cafe with your phone on the table near the street edge

How to Avoid

  • Keep your phone in a deep front pocket — never use it while walking on the street or standing at bus stops.
  • Wear your bag across your chest with the zipper facing your body, ideally under a jacket.
  • On TransMilenio, keep bags in front of you and avoid peak hours when crowding is extreme.
  • Never place your phone on a restaurant table — keep it in your pocket or lap.
  • If you must check your phone outdoors, step inside a shop or building first.

Scam #4
Express Taxi Kidnapping (Paseo Millonario)
🟢 Low
📍 Street-hailed taxis anywhere in Bogotá, especially after dark in Chapinero, La Candelaria, Zona T

Street-hailed taxis in Chapinero, La Candelaria, and Zona T pick up tourists, then stop at a red light to admit two accomplices who demand phone passcodes and bank PINs and drive the victim to multiple ATMs over three hours — Colombians call this the "paseo millonario" (millionaire ride), one of Bogotá's most feared crimes.

The setup is hailing on the street, especially after dark in Chapinero, on the edges of La Candelaria, or leaving a restaurant in Zona T. The taxi looks legitimate, the driver speaks pleasantly, and the ride starts normally. A few minutes in, the driver slows for a red light or a manufactured stop, and two additional men enter through the rear doors — usually with the central locks already engaged on the child-lock setting. The classic case file: a tourist was held for three hours, driven to four different ATMs, forced to withdraw the daily maximum at each (over $2,000 total), and additional money moved through his banking apps before he was dropped in an unfamiliar barrio at 3 AM.

The mechanic is the manufactured pickup. The driver is part of the operation; the "red light stop" was where the accomplices were already waiting. Once they are in, your phone passcode, banking app login, and PIN come out under direct threat. The duration matters — they have to time the ATM tour to your bank's daily withdrawal cap, which usually means three to four machines spaced through the night. Recent cases include physical violence (beatings to enforce compliance) and digital-app drains via Nequi, Daviplata, and other Colombian payment apps in addition to ATM cash.

Never hail a taxi on the street in Bogotá — book every ride through Uber, DiDi, or InDriver, where the driver is registered and the route is GPS-tracked. If you must take a yellow taxi, have your hotel or restaurant call a registered radio-taxi company and verify the license plate matches the number painted on the doors. Set your bank's daily ATM withdrawal limit to the lowest practical number ($200–$300) before you fly to Bogotá and keep the bulk of your cash in a separate account requiring a code from your home phone. Share your live location with a friend whenever you take any ride. For incidents, dial 123 immediately and file at any CAI.

Red Flags

  • You are hailing a taxi on the street rather than using an app or hotel-arranged car
  • The taxi driver seems to be communicating with someone by phone during the ride
  • The taxi slows down or stops where additional passengers could enter
  • The driver takes an unexpected route or the doors seem to have child locks engaged
  • There is already a passenger in the taxi when it stops for you — never enter

How to Avoid

  • Never hail a taxi on the street in Bogotá — always use Uber, DiDi, or InDriver apps.
  • If you must take a taxi, have your hotel or restaurant call a registered radio taxi company.
  • Check that the taxi's license plate matches the numbers painted on the doors and displayed inside.
  • Share your live location with a friend or family member whenever you take any ride.
  • Set low daily ATM withdrawal limits and keep minimal cash in your primary checking account.
Scam #5
Fake Emerald Street Sale
🔶 Medium
📍 Avenida Jiménez between Calle 6 and 7, outside the Emerald Trade Center, La Candelaria tourist streets

Self-described "miners" outside the Emerald Trade Center on Avenida Jiménez open velvet pouches of brilliant green stones at $200 a piece — a fine emerald starts at $1,500 per carat from a licensed dealer, and the painted-lacquer stone you bought is worth about $5.

The pitch happens on Avenida Jiménez between Calle 6 and 7 (the informal emerald-trading strip outside the Emerald Trade Center), in La Candelaria's tourist streets, and around the Gold Museum. A friendly man approaches with a small velvet pouch, opens it carefully so you can see five or six brilliant green stones, and explains he is a miner from Boyacá selling direct to bypass the middleman markup. The price he names — $200 for what looks like a fine premium stone — feels like a bargain because Colombia is the world's largest emerald producer and your mental anchor is "thousands of dollars at home." World Nomads has flagged the pattern repeatedly; the strip exists precisely because buyers expect informal trading there.

The mechanic is a chemistry trick. The stone is a low-grade or synthetic green stone whose underside has been painted with a thin layer of green lacquer, which dramatically deepens the apparent saturation when light passes through. To a tourist holding it up to the sun, the stone looks like a $3,000 emerald. To a jeweler with a loupe, the lacquer is visible immediately and the underlying stone is worth about $5. By the time you have it appraised at home, the seller is back on Avenida Jiménez with a fresh pouch. Variants include synthetic Russian emeralds (lab-grown, real chemistry but worth a fraction of natural), oiled or filled stones with hidden fractures, and "wholesale" tanzanite passed off as emerald.

Never buy emeralds from street vendors anywhere in Colombia — purchase only from licensed dealers with a physical storefront, and ask for a certificate from a recognized lab (CDTEC, GIA, or a Colombian gemological institute). Reputable Bogotá dealers include Schumacher, Glauser, and Bauer on Carrera 6, and the Emerald Trade Center's licensed shops (not the street outside it). Insist on independent appraisal before paying serious money — a legitimate seller will allow it. Understand the price floor: a one-carat fine emerald starts at $1,500 from a real dealer; anything substantially cheaper is treated, synthetic, or fake. For losses, file at policia.gov.co and dispute the card charge if you paid by card.

Red Flags

  • A stranger approaches you on the street with emeralds for sale from a pouch or pocket
  • The price seems too good to be true — fine emeralds start at $1,500+ per carat, not $50-200
  • The seller claims to be a miner or wholesaler selling 'direct' to bypass middlemen
  • You are near Avenida Jiménez where informal emerald trading is known to attract scam artists
  • The seller discourages you from having the stone independently appraised before buying

How to Avoid

  • Never buy emeralds from street vendors — always purchase from established, licensed dealers.
  • Reputable dealers include Emerald Museum shops, and jewelers like Schumacher, Glauser, and Bauer on Carrera 6.
  • Ask for a certificate of authenticity and a receipt with the dealer's full business information.
  • If interested, have any stone independently appraised before paying — legitimate sellers will allow this.
  • Understand that a genuine fine emerald cannot be purchased for a few hundred dollars — if the price seems low, the stone is fake or low quality.
Scam #6
La Candelaria Guided Tour Pickpocketing
🔶 Medium
📍 La Candelaria historic district, Plaza Bolívar, Botero Museum area, Chorro de Quevedo

Pickpockets join "free walking tours" in La Candelaria and work the group from behind during stops at Chorro de Quevedo and Plaza Bolívar — a 30-person tour all facing the guide is the ideal cover, and a wallet lifted from a daypack on someone's back is invisible until 20 minutes later.

La Candelaria's free walking tours typically launch at Plaza Bolívar or Chorro de Quevedo and run two-hour loops through the historic center. Groups of 20–40 are normal. The pickpocket version of the trip works alongside the tour: a thief joins the group at the start, blends into the back rows, and works individual targets during the longer stops where the guide is delivering history at Chorro de Quevedo, the Botero Museum exterior, or Plaza Bolívar. The narrow lanes and crowded gathering points around these stops make brief, deliberate contact look like normal jostling.

The mechanic is sustained attention misdirection. A tour group focusing on a guide for five-to-ten minutes at a time is one of the cleanest pickpocket setups available. Bags are on backs (because everyone is facing forward), pockets are unattended, and the brief bump from behind reads as crowd pressure. The thief lifts a wallet, a phone, or a backup credit card and either melts out of the tour at the next intersection or stays for another stop. Twenty minutes later, the victim reaches into her daypack and finds the wallet missing — too late to identify the thief, who has been gone since the previous block.

Wear your daypack on your front during every La Candelaria walking tour, and position yourself at the edge of the group facing outward rather than in the dense middle. Leave your passport, primary credit card, and any cash beyond $50 in the hotel safe; carry only what you need for the tour. Use a hidden money belt under your shirt for backup cash. Zippers should face your body. If someone bumps you during a stop, immediately check pockets and bag — most theft is reported only after the tour has dispersed and the trail is cold. For losses, file at the nearest CAI within 24 hours and contact your card issuer's fraud line.

Red Flags

  • You are in a large group all focused in the same direction — backs exposed to the street
  • Someone bumps into you or stands unusually close during a stop on the tour
  • Your bag is on your back where you cannot see or feel someone accessing it
  • The tour gathers in a narrow street or crowded plaza where movement is restricted
  • Unfamiliar faces seem to appear and disappear within the tour group at stops

How to Avoid

  • Wear your bag on your front during walking tours and keep zippers facing your body.
  • Position yourself at the edge of the group facing outward rather than in the middle of the crowd.
  • Leave unnecessary valuables and extra cards at the hotel — carry only what you need for the tour.
  • Use a hidden money belt under clothing for passport and backup cash.
  • If someone bumps you during a stop, immediately check all your pockets and bag compartments.
Scam #7
Dating App Robbery Setup
🟢 Low
📍 Hotels in Chapinero, apartments in Zona T, short-term rentals across Bogotá

Tinder, Bumble, and Hinge matches in Bogotá steer solo male tourists to a specific Chapinero bar, then to the tourist's hotel — once inside the room, scopolamine in a drink incapacitates the victim and the room is stripped of laptop, phone, watch, and any cash before bank transfers are made through banking apps.

The pattern targets solo male visitors in Chapinero hotels, Zona T short-term rentals, and Airbnbs across north Bogotá. The classic case file: a solo traveler matches with an attractive woman on a dating app, she suggests meeting at a bar she knows in Chapinero, drinks happen, and she pivots to going back to his hotel. ASIS International has reported the pattern as a significant security concern for tourists in Colombia; The Bogotá Post documented Colombian police dismantling gangs that used dating apps as their primary recruitment tool. The drug used is the same scopolamine running through Zona T's bar-spike scam — once dosed, the victim is suggestible enough to authorize transfers from his banking app without later memory of doing so.

The mechanic is the move from public to private. In a busy bar she cannot dose you discreetly enough; in your hotel room, she has uninterrupted access to your drink and your devices. She excuses herself to the bathroom shortly after the second drink arrives; you wake up hours later, groggy, confused, and missing laptop, phone, camera, wallet, and watch. The bank account shows wire transfers, Nequi or Daviplata movements, and ATM withdrawals you have no memory of authorizing. The relationship she built across two hours of conversation makes the room invitation feel routine — that warm-up time is the entire point of the dating-app channel versus a bar pickup.

Never bring a stranger from a dating app to your hotel room, no matter how warm the conversation has been — meet only in busy, well-lit public venues for the first three meetings. Watch every drink poured from the bottle to the glass and never accept one out of your sight. Do not share your hotel name or room number until you have met someone multiple times in person. Use video calls before meeting to verify the profile is real and tell a friend your location with a check-in time. If you wake up disoriented with property missing, call 123 immediately, get to a hospital for scopolamine testing, freeze your bank cards, and file at the nearest CAI.

Red Flags

  • A dating app match is unusually eager to meet quickly and steers you to a specific venue
  • The person insists on going to your hotel or private accommodation rather than staying in public
  • You begin feeling unexpectedly drowsy or disoriented after just one or two drinks
  • The person is evasive about their full name, workplace, or verifiable personal details
  • After arriving at a private location, the person excuses themselves or makes a phone call

How to Avoid

  • Never bring someone you just met to your hotel room — meet only in busy, well-lit public places.
  • Always buy and watch the preparation of your own drinks — never leave a drink unattended.
  • Tell a friend your location and set up regular check-in times when going on a date.
  • Do not share your hotel name or room number until you have met the person multiple times.
  • Use video calls before meeting in person and verify their identity matches their profile.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Colombian National Police (Policía Nacional) station. Call 123 (Emergency) or 112. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at policia.gov.co.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact the US Embassy in Bogotá at Carrera 45 No. 24B-27, Bogotá. Phone: +(57)(1) 275-2000. After-hours emergencies: +(57)(1) 275-4021. From the US: +1-202-501-4444 (24/7). The Embassy can issue emergency travel documents.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
