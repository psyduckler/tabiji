The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Fake Parking Ticket QR Code
⚠️ High
📍 Georgetown streets, National Mall parking areas, Dupont Circle metered spots, any street with out-of-state plates

Scammers place realistic fake parking citations on windshields of out-of-state plates throughout Georgetown and the National Mall area, directing tourists via QR code to fraudulent payment sites that charge $75–$150 and harvest credit card details for further unauthorized use.

DC parking enforcement produces official-looking citations with city logos, department names, and payment instructions — and scammers have reverse-engineered that format precisely. Handheld printers produce tickets that look genuine to someone unfamiliar with the local document. Out-of-state plates are the deliberate target: visitors are unfamiliar with DC parking rules and more likely to pay a ticket without scrutinizing the issuing agency or the payment URL.

The ticket's QR code points to a .com website rather than a .gov domain — but an unfamiliar tourist won't notice the difference. The payment portal accepts full credit card details, charges $75–$150 for the fake fine, and the card information is then used for additional unauthorized purchases. The DC DMV issued an official warning clarifying they do not issue parking tickets; all legitimate citations come from DC Department of Transportation or police and are only payable through official .gov domains.

The payment URL is the clearest tell. Never scan a QR code on a parking ticket — go directly to dmv.dc.gov or ddot.dc.gov and search for any alleged violation by license plate number to verify it exists in the official system. Legitimate DC citations list the issuing agency as DC Department of Transportation or Metropolitan Police, never "DC DMV." If the violation is real, it will appear in the official database; if it does not, the ticket is fraudulent.

Red Flags

  • The ticket has a QR code directing you to a website ending in.com instead of.gov
  • The citation lists the DC DMV as the issuing agency, which never issues parking tickets
  • The payment website asks for full credit card details rather than offering multiple payment options
  • The ticket appears on your windshield in an area where you are confident you parked legally
  • The paper quality or font looks slightly different from official government documents

How to Avoid

  • Only pay DC parking tickets through the official DC DMV website at dmv.dc.gov or by mail to the address listed on legitimate tickets.
  • Check the issuing agency: legitimate DC tickets come from the DC Department of Transportation (DDOT) or Metropolitan Police, never from 'DC DMV.'
  • Never scan a QR code on a parking ticket; instead, go directly to the official website and search for your violation by plate number.
  • Take a photo of the ticket and compare it with official DC citation examples available on the DC government website.
  • Report suspicious tickets to the DC DMV at (202) 737-4404 and file a complaint with the Better Business Bureau.
Scam #2
Metro Station Change Scam
🔶 Medium
📍 Smithsonian Metro station, Gallery Place-Chinatown Metro, Union Station entrance, L'Enfant Plaza Metro concourse

A scammer in a DC Metro station holds up two $10 bills and asks to exchange them for a $20, then palms the $20 and claims the tourist handed over only a $1 — a misdirection play that costs $19–$45 at Smithsonian, Gallery Place, and Union Station.

The DC Metro carries millions of tourists between the National Mall and downtown, and the busy station concourses create the right mix of distraction and social pressure. The scammer targets tourists who look like they are in a hurry: consulting a map, looking at a phone, or visibly unfamiliar with the system. They approach with two $10 bills held up as apparent proof of what they claim to need.

"Could you exchange two tens for a twenty?" You hand over the $20. In the handover, the scammer palms it and holds up a $1 bill: "You only gave me a dollar." The swap happens in a motion so practiced it is invisible under station lighting. A variant breaks a $50: the scammer returns five bills, but two of the $10s are $1s folded to look like $10s. The loss — $19 to $45 — is small enough that most victims don't file a report, which is exactly what the scammer counts on.

The scam depends on you handing over a bill before checking what comes back. Never make change for a stranger in a Metro station — if someone needs fare help, point them to the station manager booth rather than giving cash. If you are mid-exchange and something feels wrong, pause and count everything in your hand before the other person walks away. Walk toward the station manager or a Metro Transit Police officer at any sign of pressure.

Red Flags

  • A stranger approaches you specifically inside a Metro station asking to make change for bills
  • The person is overly friendly and creates a sense of urgency about needing change quickly
  • A second person appears nearby and creates a distraction while the exchange happens
  • The scammer insists on handling your bills rather than letting you count the exchange yourself
  • The person targets tourists who are visibly consulting maps or looking at their phones

How to Avoid

  • Never make change for strangers in Metro stations or on the street; politely say 'no' and keep walking.
  • If someone needs Metro fare help, direct them to the station manager booth rather than giving cash.
  • Keep large bills in a secure wallet and avoid displaying cash in crowded transit areas.
  • Be especially alert at Smithsonian, Gallery Place, and Union Station Metro stops, which are prime hunting grounds.
  • If you feel pressured, walk toward the station manager booth or a Metro Transit Police officer.
Scam #3
Unlicensed Pedicab Price Gouging
🔶 Medium
📍 National Mall between the Capitol and Lincoln Memorial, around the Washington Monument, Jefferson Memorial area, Tidal Basin during cherry blossom season

Unlicensed pedicab operators on the National Mall quote no price before the ride, then demand $50–$120 after dropping tourists at a destination — an especially common trap during cherry blossom season when tired crowds are easy to solicit along the 2-mile Mall.

The National Mall stretches roughly two miles from the Capitol to the Lincoln Memorial, and on hot or crowded days — cherry blossom season especially — pedicabs look like a reasonable option. Unlicensed operators position themselves along the Mall on National Park Service property where they are not authorized to make commercial transactions, approaching families and older visitors who have been walking for hours.

No price is quoted before the ride. The driver is friendly, the distance seems short, and the ride takes ten minutes. Then the driver demands $80–$120 at the destination, claiming a per-minute rate of $5–$8 that was never disclosed. The Tourmobile bus holds the exclusive NPS commercial contract for Mall transportation — any pedicab picking up passengers on the Mall itself is operating illegally and has no accountability for what they charge after the ride.

The price discussion must happen before you board. Ask for the exact total fare for your specific destination before getting in, and walk away from any driver who will not name a fixed price upfront — a per-minute rate without a distance cap is not a price. Licensed operators like Nonpartisan Pedicab display rate cards; unlicensed operators refuse to name a price because the surprise at the destination is the mechanism. The DC Circulator bus ($1) and free National Mall shuttle cover the same routes with no fare ambiguity.

Red Flags

  • The pedicab driver does not display a posted rate card or National Park Service authorization
  • No price is quoted before you get in, or the driver says 'we'll work it out after'
  • The driver is parked on park service property rather than at a designated pickup zone
  • The pedicab lacks visible business branding, licensing numbers, or insurance information
  • The driver aggressively solicits you rather than waiting at a designated stand

How to Avoid

  • Only use pedicab services that display clear per-hour pricing and are licensed by the National Park Service, such as Nonpartisan Pedicab.
  • Always confirm the total fare, whether it is per trip or per hour, and get a verbal agreement before boarding.
  • Take a photo of the rate card and the pedicab's license number before the ride begins.
  • Walk the National Mall instead; the distance from the Capitol to the Lincoln Memorial is about 2 miles and manageable for most visitors.
  • Use the free National Mall shuttle or DC Circulator bus ($1) as cheaper legitimate alternatives.

Scam #4
Friendship Bracelet and CD Hustle
🟢 Low
📍 National Mall near the Washington Monument, outside Smithsonian museums, Georgetown M Street, Adams Morgan on weekend nights

Street vendors near the National Mall and Smithsonian museums tie bracelets on tourists' wrists or press CDs into their hands as "free gifts," then demand $10–$20 and become hostile when refused — sometimes as cover for a pickpocket working the same crowd.

The National Mall and the area outside Smithsonian museums draw the highest concentration of tourists in Washington DC, and the street operations here are calibrated to that foot traffic. A person approaches with a wide smile — open, casual, friendly — and either ties a colorful bracelet on your wrist while making small talk, or presses a CD into your hands while explaining they are a local artist looking to share their music.

Once the item is on your wrist or in your hands, the social dynamic shifts. The person demands a $10–$20 "donation" and becomes aggressive or guilt-tripping if you try to return it or walk away. The bracelet version is particularly effective because removing it requires a deliberate act that the scammer turns into a confrontation. The secondary risk, documented in traveler reports, is a partner working the crowd: while one person engages you with the bracelet or CD, another is positioned near your bag.

The moment to stop the interaction is before anything touches your body or hands. Keep your hands at your sides or in your pockets and say "no thank you" without breaking stride — once a bracelet is on your wrist, the social pressure to pay has already been engineered into the situation. If a bracelet is tied on before you can refuse, remove it and place it on the ground; you are not obligated to pay for an unsolicited item. Keep your bag zipped and in front of you during any street interaction near the Mall.

Red Flags

  • Someone approaches you on the street and immediately tries to put something in your hand or on your wrist
  • The person claims the item is 'free' but then asks for a donation or payment
  • A second person lingers unusually close during the interaction
  • The person becomes aggressive or guilt-trips you when you try to decline or walk away
  • You are targeted specifically because you are taking photos, consulting a map, or carrying shopping bags

How to Avoid

  • Keep your hands in your pockets or at your sides and firmly say 'No thank you' without breaking stride.
  • Never allow a stranger to place anything on your body; physically step back if someone reaches for your wrist.
  • If a bracelet is tied on, remove it and place it on the ground; you are not obligated to pay for unsolicited items.
  • Keep your bag zipped and in front of you during any street interaction, especially near the National Mall.
  • If someone becomes aggressive, walk toward the nearest Park Police officer or into a Smithsonian museum entrance.
Scam #5
Three-Card Monte Gambling Hustle
🔶 Medium
📍 Near Union Station, Gallery Place-Chinatown sidewalks, busy intersections along Pennsylvania Avenue, downtown street corners

A dealer shuffles three cards on a cardboard-box table near Union Station and Pennsylvania Avenue while planted shills win repeatedly to lure tourists into betting $50–$300 — a game the Metropolitan Police confirm tourists cannot win because the dealer controls the outcome through sleight of hand.

Three-Card Monte tables appear near busy tourist transit points — Union Station, Gallery Place, Pennsylvania Avenue — where foot traffic between the Mall and downtown creates a steady audience. The setup is a cardboard box, three cards, and a dealer whose hands are very fast. The crowd watching appears to be ordinary passersby who have stopped out of curiosity; most of them are not.

Several of those apparent bystanders are planted shills who win small bets easily, making the game look beatable. The dealer lets a new tourist win a first bet of $10–$20 to build confidence. Then bets escalate to $50–$100, and the tourist loses every time because the dealer uses sleight of hand to control which card is which regardless of what appears to have happened. The Metropolitan Police maintain an official warning on this game: "The only time you win is when the suspect lets you — and the only reason they let you is to make you think you can beat them."

The crowd itself is the mechanism — stopping to watch makes you a target, and the planted winners create the social proof that the game is winnable. Walk past without stopping: even standing as a spectator puts you in range of pickpockets who often work Three-Card Monte crowds independently of the main operation. Street gambling is illegal in DC and participants can be cited as well as victims. Report active games to the Metropolitan Police at (202) 727-9099.

Red Flags

  • A small crowd gathers around a person shuffling cards on a box or makeshift table on the sidewalk
  • Several 'spectators' seem to be winning easily and encourage you to try your luck
  • The dealer lets you win your first small bet to build your confidence
  • The game is set up near busy tourist walking routes between the Mall and downtown attractions
  • Lookouts are positioned at nearby corners watching for police

How to Avoid

  • Never participate in street gambling games; the game is mathematically rigged through sleight of hand and you cannot win.
  • Walk past without stopping to watch, as even spectators can be targeted by pickpockets working with the group.
  • Understand that the people 'winning' are planted accomplices, not random lucky tourists.
  • Report Three-Card Monte operations to the Metropolitan Police at (202) 727-9099 or 911.
  • Remember that street gambling is illegal in DC and participants can be cited as well as victims.
Scam #6
Fake Tour Guide Solicitation
🟢 Low
📍 Outside the White House on Pennsylvania Avenue, near the Capitol steps, around the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool, Arlington Cemetery entrance

Self-appointed guides approach tourists outside the White House, Capitol, and Lincoln Memorial offering personalized tours for $20–$50 per person, provide 15 minutes of publicly available facts mixed with invented anecdotes, then disappear — sometimes steering groups through commission-paying souvenir shops along the route.

Washington DC's major landmarks are among the most-visited in the United States, and the outside approaches to the White House, Capitol steps, and Lincoln Memorial draw a steady stream of unofficial guides. They approach families with children or older visitors who look uncertain at the entrance, offering a "personalized tour" that sounds more engaging than consulting a phone. They appear knowledgeable and may claim credentials or affiliations that cannot be verified on the spot.

The guide collects $20–$50 per person upfront in cash, then provides a 15-to-20-minute walk covering facts available on any NPS pamphlet or government website, mixed with invented historical anecdotes. Some cut the tour short claiming a "schedule conflict" and disappear; others complete the loop but spend a disproportionate share of the time walking tourists through specific souvenir shops where they earn a commission. A common angle is claiming to have "reserved spots" at the Capitol, Bureau of Engraving, or Smithsonian — all of which are free or bookable directly through official .gov sites.

Free official alternatives make paid street guides unnecessary at every DC landmark. Book tours only through companies with verifiable TripAdvisor or Viator profiles, and check in advance whether the attraction requires booking — the National Park Service runs free ranger-led walks and talks at every Mall memorial and monument. Capitol tours, White House tours, and Bureau of Engraving tours are all free through congressional representatives or official .gov booking; all Smithsonian museums are free with no premium access tier. Anyone claiming to sell access to these attractions is misrepresenting the situation.

Red Flags

  • Someone approaches you unsolicited outside a major landmark offering a personalized tour
  • The guide demands cash payment upfront before the tour begins
  • The guide promises access to places that are actually free to enter, like the Capitol or Smithsonian museums
  • The person cannot show any form of licensing, business card, or affiliation with a known tour company
  • The tour route includes stops at specific souvenir shops presented as 'local favorites'

How to Avoid

  • Book tours only through established companies with verifiable websites, TripAdvisor profiles, and posted reviews.
  • Take advantage of free National Park Service ranger-led walks and talks at all Mall memorials and monuments.
  • Reserve free Capitol, White House, and Bureau of Engraving tours through official.gov websites well in advance.
  • All Smithsonian museums are free; never pay anyone for 'skip the line' access to these institutions.
  • If you want a paid tour, book through platforms like Viator or GetYourGuide where reviews and refund policies are transparent.
Scam #7
Distraction Pickpocketing on the Metro
⚠️ High
📍 Gallery Place-Chinatown station, Metro Center transfer platform, crowded Orange/Blue/Silver line trains, Smithsonian station escalators

Two-person teams on DC Metro trains and station escalators — especially at Gallery Place, Smithsonian, and Metro Center — use bumps, dropped items, and sudden questions to distract tourists while a partner lifts wallets, phones, and passports from bags and pockets.

DC's Metro carries a high volume of tourists between the National Mall stops and downtown transfer hubs, creating predictable crowds on escalators and station platforms. Gallery Place during Capitals and Wizards games and the Smithsonian station during cherry blossom season are among the densest pinch points. Tourists are visibly distracted — consulting maps, looking at phones, managing children and luggage — and often unfamiliar with the local crowd dynamics.

The team works in pairs: one person bumps you at an escalator or doorway and immediately apologizes, creating a few seconds of social engagement; a partner behind or alongside reaches into your bag or pocket in that window. Variants include someone dropping something near your feet while you are in a tight space, or a question asked while standing unusually close. The "sandwich" technique presses you between two people on a crowded escalator. Victims typically don't notice until they try to pay for something after leaving the station.

The gap between a distraction and realizing your pocket was picked is the entire operation. Keep your wallet in a front pocket and your bag zipped and held in front of you at all times on the Metro — rear pockets and back-slung bags are where the losses happen. Be specifically alert on Smithsonian, Gallery Place, and Metro Center escalators during peak tourist periods. Report pickpocketing to Metro Transit Police at (202) 962-2121 immediately and cancel any stolen cards before you leave the station area.

Red Flags

  • Someone bumps into you unnecessarily hard in a Metro station or on a train
  • A stranger drops something near you and makes a production of picking it up while you are in a tight space
  • You feel someone pressing unusually close to your bag or pockets in a crowd that is not dense enough to justify it
  • Someone asks you an overly complicated question while standing very close on a platform or escalator
  • A group of people suddenly surrounds you during boarding or exiting the train

How to Avoid

  • Carry your wallet in a front pocket and keep your bag zipped and positioned in front of your body at all times on the Metro.
  • Use a money belt or neck wallet for your passport, large cash, and backup credit card when touring the city.
  • Be especially vigilant on escalators at Smithsonian, Metro Center, and Gallery Place stations during peak tourist season.
  • Keep your phone secured and avoid using it while walking through crowded station corridors.
  • Report pickpocketing immediately to Metro Transit Police at (202) 962-2121 and cancel stolen cards right away.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Metropolitan Police Department (MPDC) station. Call (202) 727-9099. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at MPDC Online Reporting.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your embassy on Embassy Row along Massachusetts Avenue NW; most embassies are located between Dupont Circle and the Naval Observatory

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.

DC Metro stations are generally safe, especially during daytime hours. Metro Transit Police patrol stations, but tourists should remain alert for pickpockets during rush hour and large events. Avoid empty rail cars late at night and stay near other passengers.
