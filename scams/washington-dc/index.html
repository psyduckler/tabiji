The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Fake Parking Ticket QR Code
⚠️ High
📍 Georgetown streets, National Mall parking areas, Dupont Circle metered spots, any street with out-of-state plates

Scammers print realistic DC Department of Transportation parking citations — complete with official logos — and place them on out-of-state vehicles in Georgetown and around the National Mall; the QR code links to a fraudulent .com payment site where victims are charged $75–$150 and their card details used for further fraud.

Georgetown's residential streets, the National Mall parking lots, and Dupont Circle metered spots see dense out-of-state tourist traffic from March through October. Scammers use handheld printers to produce citations that closely replicate official DC Department of Transportation tickets, complete with DDOT logos, officer badge numbers, and the city seal. Tourists with unfamiliar plates are the preferred target: they don't know local parking enforcement conventions and are motivated to pay quickly to avoid a tow.

The fake ticket carries a QR code directing you to a payment website. The legitimate domain for DC parking violations is .gov; the fraudulent sites use .com domains with convincing government-style layouts. The site processes a $75–$150 payment and captures your full card details. Some victims discover additional unauthorized charges days later — the parking "fine" was the smallest part of the loss.

Only pay DC parking citations through dc.gov — type the URL directly into your browser and look up your plate number; never scan a QR code from a windshield ticket. Legitimate DC citations are issued by the DC Department of Transportation (DDOT) or Metropolitan Police, never by the "DC DMV" — a common tell on fake tickets since the DMV does not issue parking citations. If the ticket shows a DMV issuer or a .com payment link, photograph it and call DDOT's Revenue Division at (202) 737-4404 to verify whether a genuine violation exists before paying anything.

Red Flags

  • The ticket has a QR code directing you to a website ending in.com instead of.gov
  • The citation lists the DC DMV as the issuing agency, which never issues parking tickets
  • The payment website asks for full credit card details rather than offering multiple payment options
  • The ticket appears on your windshield in an area where you are confident you parked legally
  • The paper quality or font looks slightly different from official government documents

How to Avoid

  • Only pay DC parking tickets through the official DC DMV website at dmv.dc.gov or by mail to the address listed on legitimate tickets.
  • Check the issuing agency: legitimate DC tickets come from the DC Department of Transportation (DDOT) or Metropolitan Police, never from 'DC DMV.'
  • Never scan a QR code on a parking ticket; instead, go directly to the official website and search for your violation by plate number.
  • Take a photo of the ticket and compare it with official DC citation examples available on the DC government website.
  • Report suspicious tickets to the DC DMV at (202) 737-4404 and file a complaint with the Better Business Bureau.
Scam #2
Metro Station Change Scam
🔶 Medium
📍 Smithsonian Metro station, Gallery Place-Chinatown Metro, Union Station entrance, L'Enfant Plaza Metro concourse

Scammers inside DC Metro stations approach tourists holding two $10 bills and ask to swap them for a $20, then palm your $20 and display a $1 claiming you made a mistake — losses run $19–$45 per encounter, concentrated at Smithsonian, Gallery Place, and Union Station.

DC Metro's busiest tourist stations — Smithsonian, Gallery Place-Chinatown, and Union Station — run near-constant foot traffic of first-time visitors navigating the fare system, checking phones, and carrying backpacks. The change scam targets that distraction with a request designed to feel like helping rather than transacting: a person approaches holding two $10 bills and asks if you can swap them for a $20. The social script is designed to feel harmless — which is why it works.

You reach for a $20. The moment it changes hands, the scammer palms it and substitutes a $1 or a $5 folded to look thicker. He holds up the smaller bill: "You only gave me one dollar." The pressure is immediate and public — other commuters are walking past, a train is coming, and arguing over $20 on a platform feels disproportionate. A second person often creates a brief distraction during the swap. A variant involves asking to break a $50; the outcome is always the same: the scammer walks away with more than he arrived with.

The rule is simple: never make change for a stranger in a Metro station or on a platform — DC Metro's SmarTrip and phone tap-to-pay system means nobody legitimately needs cash-change inside the system anymore. Say "no" without breaking stride. If the approach continues, walk toward the station manager booth at the fare gate. Smithsonian and Gallery Place are the highest-density spots; stay especially alert at those two during peak tourist hours between 10 AM and 3 PM.

Red Flags

  • A stranger approaches you specifically inside a Metro station asking to make change for bills
  • The person is overly friendly and creates a sense of urgency about needing change quickly
  • A second person appears nearby and creates a distraction while the exchange happens
  • The scammer insists on handling your bills rather than letting you count the exchange yourself
  • The person targets tourists who are visibly consulting maps or looking at their phones

How to Avoid

  • Never make change for strangers in Metro stations or on the street; politely say 'no' and keep walking.
  • If someone needs Metro fare help, direct them to the station manager booth rather than giving cash.
  • Keep large bills in a secure wallet and avoid displaying cash in crowded transit areas.
  • Be especially alert at Smithsonian, Gallery Place, and Union Station Metro stops, which are prime hunting grounds.
  • If you feel pressured, walk toward the station manager booth or a Metro Transit Police officer.
Scam #3
Unlicensed Pedicab Price Gouging
🔶 Medium
📍 National Mall between the Capitol and Lincoln Memorial, around the Washington Monument, Jefferson Memorial area, Tidal Basin during cherry blossom season

Unlicensed pedicab operators on the National Mall quote no price before the ride, then demand $50–$120 for a 10-minute trip after it's done — the Tourmobile bus holds an exclusive NPS contract for Mall transportation, meaning most pedicabs there are operating illegally to begin with.

The National Mall stretches roughly 2 miles from the Capitol to the Lincoln Memorial, and unlicensed pedicab operators position themselves at the high-traffic nodes — the Washington Monument, the Jefferson Memorial during cherry blossom season, and the Lincoln Memorial steps — where tired visitors and families with young children are most tempted by a ride. These operators have no posted rate cards and approach aggressively, particularly during the spring cherry blossom peak when the Mall is crowded enough to make an unlicensed operation blend in.

The driver offers a friendly "I'll take you around the Mall" with a wave and a smile, and when you ask the price, deflects: "We'll work it out, don't worry about it." You get in. Ten minutes and a few monuments later, he stops and hands you a bill: $80–$120, calculated at $5–$8 per minute with per-passenger surcharges and a tip. When you push back, he doesn't move the pedicab. On National Park Service property with no fixed establishment to walk away from, the standoff is uncomfortable — most tourists pay to end it.

Insist on a written total fare before anyone boards — a licensed operator will provide one without hesitation (Nonpartisan Pedicab, one of DC's legitimate operators, posts $84–$90 per hour clearly). If a driver won't put a price in writing, walk away. The DC Circulator bus runs along Mall-adjacent routes for $1, and the free National Mall shuttle operates during peak season — both are scam-proof. Walking the full Capitol-to-Lincoln path is about 2 miles on flat, paved ground with bench seating at every major monument.

Red Flags

  • The pedicab driver does not display a posted rate card or National Park Service authorization
  • No price is quoted before you get in, or the driver says 'we'll work it out after'
  • The driver is parked on park service property rather than at a designated pickup zone
  • The pedicab lacks visible business branding, licensing numbers, or insurance information
  • The driver aggressively solicits you rather than waiting at a designated stand

How to Avoid

  • Only use pedicab services that display clear per-hour pricing and are licensed by the National Park Service, such as Nonpartisan Pedicab.
  • Always confirm the total fare, whether it is per trip or per hour, and get a verbal agreement before boarding.
  • Take a photo of the rate card and the pedicab's license number before the ride begins.
  • Walk the National Mall instead; the distance from the Capitol to the Lincoln Memorial is about 2 miles and manageable for most visitors.
  • Use the free National Mall shuttle or DC Circulator bus ($1) as cheaper legitimate alternatives.

Scam #4
Friendship Bracelet and CD Hustle
🟢 Low
📍 National Mall near the Washington Monument, outside Smithsonian museums, Georgetown M Street, Adams Morgan on weekend nights

Street hustlers near the Washington Monument and Smithsonian museums tie a colorful bracelet on your wrist before you can decline, or press a music CD into your hands, then demand $10–$20 and turn hostile when you try to return it — a pickpocket accomplice often works the crowd during the encounter.

The National Mall attracts the bracelet and CD hustle for the same reason Times Square does: dense, slow-moving foot traffic of tourists who are distracted, looking at their phones or pointing at monuments, and reluctant to cause a scene in public. The stretch between the Smithsonian museums and the Washington Monument is the primary zone. Georgetown's M Street sees the same play on weekend evenings when sidewalks slow with bar and restaurant foot traffic.

The bracelet version works on physical imposition. Someone steps alongside you and immediately begins tying a colorful bracelet on your wrist with a friendly "gift for you, welcome to DC." By the time you process what's happening, it's half-tied. Once it's on, the frame shifts from "gift" to "purchase": "$15 for the bracelet." Trying to remove it in public or refuse feels aggressive and embarrassing on a crowded path. The CD version is more direct — a person presses a disc into your hands while saying "it's free, I just want you to hear my music" — and the demand appears once you've touched it: "$20 to support local music." While either engagement runs, a second person nearby is watching your bag or back pocket.

Keep your hands at your sides or in your pockets and walk past without stopping — if you don't extend your arm, the bracelet can't be tied. If a bracelet does end up on your wrist, remove it and set it on the ground; you owe nothing for an unsolicited item placed on you without consent. The same logic applies to the CD. Keep your bag zipped and worn across your front during any street encounter on the Mall, and walk toward the nearest Park Police officer or into a Smithsonian museum entrance if someone becomes aggressive.

Red Flags

  • Someone approaches you on the street and immediately tries to put something in your hand or on your wrist
  • The person claims the item is 'free' but then asks for a donation or payment
  • A second person lingers unusually close during the interaction
  • The person becomes aggressive or guilt-trips you when you try to decline or walk away
  • You are targeted specifically because you are taking photos, consulting a map, or carrying shopping bags

How to Avoid

  • Keep your hands in your pockets or at your sides and firmly say 'No thank you' without breaking stride.
  • Never allow a stranger to place anything on your body; physically step back if someone reaches for your wrist.
  • If a bracelet is tied on, remove it and place it on the ground; you are not obligated to pay for unsolicited items.
  • Keep your bag zipped and in front of you during any street interaction, especially near the National Mall.
  • If someone becomes aggressive, walk toward the nearest Park Police officer or into a Smithsonian museum entrance.
Scam #5
Three-Card Monte Gambling Hustle
🔶 Medium
📍 Near Union Station, Gallery Place-Chinatown sidewalks, busy intersections along Pennsylvania Avenue, downtown street corners

A dealer shuffles three cards on a cardboard box near Union Station and Gallery Place while planted shills win visibly to encourage tourist bets of $50–$100 — the DC Metropolitan Police Department states plainly that the only time a player wins is when the dealer allows it, and you cannot beat the game.

Three-Card Monte runs on the same DC sidewalks it has occupied for decades: near Union Station, around Gallery Place-Chinatown, and along Pennsylvania Avenue on busy tourist days. The operation is a coordinated team of four to six people — the dealer, two or three shills planted in the spectator ring, and one or two lookouts positioned at nearby corners watching for police. From outside the circle it looks like a crowd watching street magic; from inside, the shills are winning enticingly and encouraging you to try.

The first bet — $10–$20 — wins. The dealer allows it, and the shill next to you confirms: "It's easy, I've done it three times." Your next bet is $50–$100. You watch the red card through the shuffle and point to it confidently. It's the wrong card. The dealer used a sleight-of-hand technique requiring only seconds of misdirection to shift the card's position invisibly. The Metropolitan Police Department states it directly: you cannot win at Three-Card Monte. The dealer controls the outcome on every bet; the only winning hands are the ones designed to build your confidence for a bigger loss.

Don't stop to watch — even standing near the circle as a spectator puts you in range of the pickpockets working the crowd around the game. Walk past without pausing; the game depends on foot traffic converting to spectators, which converts to marks. If you see the operation running, call the Metropolitan Police non-emergency line at (202) 727-9099 with the location — the MPDC maintains a dedicated enforcement response for this specific scam. Street gambling is illegal in DC, and participants can be cited as well as victims.

Red Flags

  • A small crowd gathers around a person shuffling cards on a box or makeshift table on the sidewalk
  • Several 'spectators' seem to be winning easily and encourage you to try your luck
  • The dealer lets you win your first small bet to build your confidence
  • The game is set up near busy tourist walking routes between the Mall and downtown attractions
  • Lookouts are positioned at nearby corners watching for police

How to Avoid

  • Never participate in street gambling games; the game is mathematically rigged through sleight of hand and you cannot win.
  • Walk past without stopping to watch, as even spectators can be targeted by pickpockets working with the group.
  • Understand that the people 'winning' are planted accomplices, not random lucky tourists.
  • Report Three-Card Monte operations to the Metropolitan Police at (202) 727-9099 or 911.
  • Remember that street gambling is illegal in DC and participants can be cited as well as victims.
Scam #6
Fake Tour Guide Solicitation
🟢 Low
📍 Outside the White House on Pennsylvania Avenue, near the Capitol steps, around the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool, Arlington Cemetery entrance

Self-appointed guides outside the White House, Capitol, and Lincoln Memorial offer personal tours at $20–$50 per person, collect cash upfront, then provide fifteen minutes of freely available facts before claiming a schedule conflict — all major DC landmarks are free to enter and the NPS provides free ranger talks at every memorial.

Washington DC's major landmarks — the White House north fence, the Capitol steps, the Lincoln Memorial, and Arlington Cemetery's entrance gate — draw steady clusters of tourists pausing to photograph or orient themselves. Self-appointed guides position themselves at those natural pause points. The pitch is tailored to the moment: for families with children it's a "kid-friendly, behind-the-scenes" tour; for older visitors it's "insider access" or "skip the crowds." Payment is requested upfront and in cash, with no receipt.

The guide collects $20–$50 per person and begins walking. The information provided is correct but generic — facts available on the NPS placard three steps away — mixed with embellished anecdotes that sound authoritative but can't be verified. After fifteen to twenty minutes the guide claims a schedule conflict and drifts away, or simply stops at a souvenir shop (where he earns a commission) and considers the tour concluded. Some guides charge admission for attractions that are free: "I have reserved spots at the Capitol today" or "the Smithsonian requires a ticket this weekend."

All major DC memorials and Smithsonian museums are free — no guide, reservation, or fee of any kind is ever required for entry. Free National Park Service ranger talks operate at every Mall memorial on a published schedule posted at each site. For paid tours with an itinerary you can verify, book through Viator or GetYourGuide with written reviews and a refund policy. Capitol, White House, and Bureau of Engraving tours are bookable free through official .gov websites via your congressional representative's office — plan several months ahead.

Red Flags

  • Someone approaches you unsolicited outside a major landmark offering a personalized tour
  • The guide demands cash payment upfront before the tour begins
  • The guide promises access to places that are actually free to enter, like the Capitol or Smithsonian museums
  • The person cannot show any form of licensing, business card, or affiliation with a known tour company
  • The tour route includes stops at specific souvenir shops presented as 'local favorites'

How to Avoid

  • Book tours only through established companies with verifiable websites, TripAdvisor profiles, and posted reviews.
  • Take advantage of free National Park Service ranger-led walks and talks at all Mall memorials and monuments.
  • Reserve free Capitol, White House, and Bureau of Engraving tours through official.gov websites well in advance.
  • All Smithsonian museums are free; never pay anyone for 'skip the line' access to these institutions.
  • If you want a paid tour, book through platforms like Viator or GetYourGuide where reviews and refund policies are transparent.
Scam #7
Distraction Pickpocketing on the Metro
⚠️ High
📍 Gallery Place-Chinatown station, Metro Center transfer platform, crowded Orange/Blue/Silver line trains, Smithsonian station escalators

A two-person team on DC Metro — one creates a distraction by bumping into you, dropping something, or asking a question, while a partner lifts your wallet, phone, or passport — with Gallery Place, Metro Center, and Smithsonian stations at highest risk during tourist season and event days.

The DC Metro system carries millions of tourists annually through its deep, vaulted stations, and the transition points — long escalators, crowded platform edges, and the tight boarding press at doors — create the close-contact conditions that pickpocket teams need. Gallery Place-Chinatown during Capitals and Wizards games, Smithsonian station during cherry blossom season, and Metro Center during rush hour are the documented high-density windows. Teams target tourists who are visibly distracted: earbuds in, phone out, map open, children in tow.

The standard two-person escalator play: one person steps in front of you and slows unexpectedly, causing you to focus on not walking into them. Their partner steps close from behind during the stall. Your wallet, in a back pocket or an unzipped bag, is accessible for three to five seconds — more than enough. The "door rush" variant runs at train entry and exit: one person bumps into you hard as the doors open, creating a moment of physical confusion while the partner, already inside the car, reaches for your bag during the jostle. Victims typically don't notice the loss until they reach for their wallet at the next stop.

Keep your wallet in a front pocket and your bag zipped and worn across your front before you enter any Metro station — not just when you feel crowded, but from the fare gate forward. A money belt under clothing is the right solution for your passport, backup card, and large cash on any day you're spending extended time on transit. Be extra alert on escalators at Smithsonian, Gallery Place, and Metro Center during the 10 AM to 3 PM tourist window. If you realize you've been pickpocketed, report immediately to Metro Transit Police at (202) 962-2121 and cancel cards while still in the station.

Red Flags

  • Someone bumps into you unnecessarily hard in a Metro station or on a train
  • A stranger drops something near you and makes a production of picking it up while you are in a tight space
  • You feel someone pressing unusually close to your bag or pockets in a crowd that is not dense enough to justify it
  • Someone asks you an overly complicated question while standing very close on a platform or escalator
  • A group of people suddenly surrounds you during boarding or exiting the train

How to Avoid

  • Carry your wallet in a front pocket and keep your bag zipped and positioned in front of your body at all times on the Metro.
  • Use a money belt or neck wallet for your passport, large cash, and backup credit card when touring the city.
  • Be especially vigilant on escalators at Smithsonian, Metro Center, and Gallery Place stations during peak tourist season.
  • Keep your phone secured and avoid using it while walking through crowded station corridors.
  • Report pickpocketing immediately to Metro Transit Police at (202) 962-2121 and cancel stolen cards right away.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Metropolitan Police Department (MPDC) station. Call (202) 727-9099. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at MPDC Online Reporting.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your embassy on Embassy Row along Massachusetts Avenue NW; most embassies are located between Dupont Circle and the Naval Observatory

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
