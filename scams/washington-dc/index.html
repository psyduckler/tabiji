The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Fake Parking Ticket QR Code
⚠️ High
📍 Georgetown streets, National Mall parking areas, Dupont Circle metered spots, any street with out-of-state plates

Scammers leave realistic-looking parking tickets on tourist cars in Georgetown and National Mall lots — the QR codes lead to fraudulent .com payment sites that harvest credit card data rather than to the legitimate DC government's .gov domain, with victims charged $75–$150 before they realize anything is wrong.

Vehicles with out-of-state plates parked in Georgetown, Dupont Circle, and around the National Mall are targeted with printed fake citations that closely mimic official DC Department of Transportation tickets — correct logos, city seals, violation codes, and the look of a real government document. The tickets appear on windshields exactly as legitimate citations do, and the only structural difference from a real ticket is the QR code printed on the face.

Scanning the code directs the driver to a .com website designed to look like an official DC payment portal. Victims who pay are charged $75–$150 and have their credit card details harvested for further unauthorized purchases. The DC DMV issued a formal warning noting that the agency never issues parking citations — all legitimate tickets come from the DC Department of Transportation or Metropolitan Police, payable only through .gov websites with no QR-code component.

Legitimate DC parking citations can be searched by plate number directly on the official government site — no QR code required. If you find a ticket, type the official DMV address into your browser manually and search for the violation by plate number; any ticket that produces no record in the official system is fake, and you should photograph it and report it to the DC DMV at (202) 737-4404 rather than scanning any code it contains.

Red Flags

  • The ticket has a QR code directing you to a website ending in.com instead of.gov
  • The citation lists the DC DMV as the issuing agency, which never issues parking tickets
  • The payment website asks for full credit card details rather than offering multiple payment options
  • The ticket appears on your windshield in an area where you are confident you parked legally
  • The paper quality or font looks slightly different from official government documents

How to Avoid

  • Only pay DC parking tickets through the official DC DMV website at dmv.dc.gov or by mail to the address listed on legitimate tickets.
  • Check the issuing agency: legitimate DC tickets come from the DC Department of Transportation (DDOT) or Metropolitan Police, never from 'DC DMV.'
  • Never scan a QR code on a parking ticket; instead, go directly to the official website and search for your violation by plate number.
  • Take a photo of the ticket and compare it with official DC citation examples available on the DC government website.
  • Report suspicious tickets to the DC DMV at (202) 737-4404 and file a complaint with the Better Business Bureau.
Scam #2
Metro Station Change Scam
🔶 Medium
📍 Smithsonian Metro station, Gallery Place-Chinatown Metro, Union Station entrance, L'Enfant Plaza Metro concourse

Scammers at Smithsonian, Gallery Place, and Union Station Metro stops approach tourists holding two $10 bills and asking to exchange them for a $20 — they palm the $20 in the handoff and present a $1 bill claiming the tourist gave them a single, stealing $19 in a transaction small enough that most victims skip the police report.

Inside busy DC Metro stations, men approach tourists who look helpful and slightly distracted — consulting a map, checking their phone, waiting on a platform. The opener is disarmingly simple: two $10 bills are held up with a request to exchange them for a $20. It sounds like a small courtesy between travelers, the kind of thing anyone would do.

When you hand over the $20, the scammer palms it in the brief handoff and presents a folded $1 bill, insisting the tourist made a mistake and only gave them a single. In a common variant, the target is asked to break a $50, and the bills returned are $5s folded to resemble $20s. Scammers work in pairs — one makes the request while a partner creates a nearby distraction. Losses of $19 to $45 are calculated precisely to stay below the threshold that most victims consider worth a police report.

The complete refusal is the only protection: never make change for a stranger in any Metro station regardless of how simple the request sounds. If someone approaches asking for bill change, say "sorry" without slowing down and walk toward the station manager booth or any uniformed Metro Transit Police officer — moving in that direction ends virtually every such encounter within seconds.

Red Flags

  • A stranger approaches you specifically inside a Metro station asking to make change for bills
  • The person is overly friendly and creates a sense of urgency about needing change quickly
  • A second person appears nearby and creates a distraction while the exchange happens
  • The scammer insists on handling your bills rather than letting you count the exchange yourself
  • The person targets tourists who are visibly consulting maps or looking at their phones

How to Avoid

  • Never make change for strangers in Metro stations or on the street; politely say 'no' and keep walking.
  • If someone needs Metro fare help, direct them to the station manager booth rather than giving cash.
  • Keep large bills in a secure wallet and avoid displaying cash in crowded transit areas.
  • Be especially alert at Smithsonian, Gallery Place, and Union Station Metro stops, which are prime hunting grounds.
  • If you feel pressured, walk toward the station manager booth or a Metro Transit Police officer.
Scam #3
Unlicensed Pedicab Price Gouging
🔶 Medium
📍 National Mall between the Capitol and Lincoln Memorial, around the Washington Monument, Jefferson Memorial area, Tidal Basin during cherry blossom season

Unlicensed pedicabs on National Mall grounds quote no fare before the ride, then demand $50–$120 after a 10-minute trip — operating illegally on National Park Service property where only the Tourmobile holds an authorized commercial contract, with no posted rate and no way to negotiate once the ride is over.

The National Mall's two-mile stretch between the Capitol and the Lincoln Memorial draws millions of tourists, and during cherry blossom season around the Tidal Basin the crowds become thick enough that walking seems hard. Unlicensed pedicab operators aggressively solicit overwhelmed visitors near the Washington Monument and Jefferson Memorial, positioning themselves where tired tourists are most likely to accept a ride without asking about price.

No price is quoted before you board — the driver answers questions with "we'll work it out" or "just a small donation." After a 10-minute ride, the demand arrives: $50, sometimes $80 or $120. Some drivers claim a per-minute rate of $5–$8 that was never disclosed at the start. The Tourmobile bus holds the only authorized commercial transportation contract on NPS grounds, making these unlicensed pedicabs illegal passengers-for-hire — but enforcement is inconsistent, and the driver has already collected the fare before any official can intervene.

Licensed operators like Nonpartisan Pedicab display a posted rate card before you board — that rate card is the baseline any legitimate DC pedicab must show. Always confirm the total fare in dollars before getting in, and never board any pedicab that cannot show a posted rate or National Park Service authorization for commercial operations on Mall grounds; the free DC Circulator bus covers the same routes for $1 as a no-surprise alternative.

Red Flags

  • The pedicab driver does not display a posted rate card or National Park Service authorization
  • No price is quoted before you get in, or the driver says 'we'll work it out after'
  • The driver is parked on park service property rather than at a designated pickup zone
  • The pedicab lacks visible business branding, licensing numbers, or insurance information
  • The driver aggressively solicits you rather than waiting at a designated stand

How to Avoid

  • Only use pedicab services that display clear per-hour pricing and are licensed by the National Park Service, such as Nonpartisan Pedicab.
  • Always confirm the total fare, whether it is per trip or per hour, and get a verbal agreement before boarding.
  • Take a photo of the rate card and the pedicab's license number before the ride begins.
  • Walk the National Mall instead; the distance from the Capitol to the Lincoln Memorial is about 2 miles and manageable for most visitors.
  • Use the free National Mall shuttle or DC Circulator bus ($1) as cheaper legitimate alternatives.

Scam #4
Friendship Bracelet and CD Hustle
🟢 Low
📍 National Mall near the Washington Monument, outside Smithsonian museums, Georgetown M Street, Adams Morgan on weekend nights

Vendors near the National Mall and Smithsonian entrances tie friendship bracelets on tourists' wrists without asking or press "free" CDs into their hands, then demand $10–$20 and turn hostile — while a partner may pick pockets during the distraction, making the bracelet the misdirection rather than the main event.

Around the National Mall, outside Smithsonian museum entrances, and on Georgetown's M Street on weekend afternoons, a person approaches with a broad smile and ties a colorful bracelet on your wrist before you have time to pull back — or presses a CD into your hands while saying "it's free, I just want you to hear my music." The approach is designed to complete the physical contact before refusal becomes practical, relying on the social awkwardness of pulling your wrist away from someone already looping a bracelet around it.

Once the item is in your possession, the tone shifts. Demands of $10–$20 arrive with guilt and escalating pressure, and returning the bracelet or CD is met with hostility or aggressive pursuit. The dollar amount is low enough that most visitors pay just to end the encounter — which is exactly the calculation. The more serious risk, flagged on Rick Steves' travel forum, is the partner working your pockets or bag while the bracelet-giver holds your attention forward; the bracelet is the misdirection, not the main event.

Keep both hands in your pockets or at your sides near the Mall and do not break stride if someone steps in front of you with an item. If a bracelet is tied on before you can react, remove it and place it on the ground — you have no legal obligation to pay for anything placed on your body without your agreement, and walking toward the nearest Park Police officer or Smithsonian entrance ends any aggressive pursuit immediately.

Red Flags

  • Someone approaches you on the street and immediately tries to put something in your hand or on your wrist
  • The person claims the item is 'free' but then asks for a donation or payment
  • A second person lingers unusually close during the interaction
  • The person becomes aggressive or guilt-trips you when you try to decline or walk away
  • You are targeted specifically because you are taking photos, consulting a map, or carrying shopping bags

How to Avoid

  • Keep your hands in your pockets or at your sides and firmly say 'No thank you' without breaking stride.
  • Never allow a stranger to place anything on your body; physically step back if someone reaches for your wrist.
  • If a bracelet is tied on, remove it and place it on the ground; you are not obligated to pay for unsolicited items.
  • Keep your bag zipped and in front of you during any street interaction, especially near the National Mall.
  • If someone becomes aggressive, walk toward the nearest Park Police officer or into a Smithsonian museum entrance.
Scam #5
Three-Card Monte Gambling Hustle
🔶 Medium
📍 Near Union Station, Gallery Place-Chinatown sidewalks, busy intersections along Pennsylvania Avenue, downtown street corners

A coordinated team of four to six people runs Three-Card Monte near Union Station and Gallery Place — a dealer, planted shills who appear to win easily, and police-watching lookouts — in a game the Metropolitan Police confirm you physically cannot win because the dealer controls the outcome through sleight of hand.

On busy sidewalks near Union Station, Gallery Place-Chinatown, and along Pennsylvania Avenue, a small crowd gathers around a cardboard-box table with a dealer shuffling three cards or shells. Several people among the spectators appear to be winning casually and wave you over — the atmosphere is confident, friendly, and looks like an obvious opportunity. The setup is a practiced performance; every person in the crowd is part of the team.

Early bets of $10 or $20 are allowed to win to build confidence in the mark, then the amounts climb to $50 or $100 — at which point the dealer's sleight of hand ensures the winning card is never where it appears to be, regardless of how carefully you tracked it. The Metropolitan Police maintain a formal advisory on Three-Card Monte stating flatly: "The only time you win in this game is when the suspect lets you, and the only reason a suspect will let you is to fool you into thinking you can beat them." Losses range from $50 to $300. The whole group packs up in seconds when police appear.

Three-Card Monte is physically unbeatable — the dealer controls the card's position through hand mechanics that no level of attention prevents. Walk past without slowing down, because stopping to watch marks you as a potential player and puts you among the pickpockets the group sometimes runs alongside the game; report active operations to the Metropolitan Police at (202) 727-9099 or 911.

Red Flags

  • A small crowd gathers around a person shuffling cards on a box or makeshift table on the sidewalk
  • Several 'spectators' seem to be winning easily and encourage you to try your luck
  • The dealer lets you win your first small bet to build your confidence
  • The game is set up near busy tourist walking routes between the Mall and downtown attractions
  • Lookouts are positioned at nearby corners watching for police

How to Avoid

  • Never participate in street gambling games; the game is mathematically rigged through sleight of hand and you cannot win.
  • Walk past without stopping to watch, as even spectators can be targeted by pickpockets working with the group.
  • Understand that the people 'winning' are planted accomplices, not random lucky tourists.
  • Report Three-Card Monte operations to the Metropolitan Police at (202) 727-9099 or 911.
  • Remember that street gambling is illegal in DC and participants can be cited as well as victims.
Scam #6
Fake Tour Guide Solicitation
🟢 Low
📍 Outside the White House on Pennsylvania Avenue, near the Capitol steps, around the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool, Arlington Cemetery entrance

Self-appointed guides approach tourists outside the White House, Capitol, and Lincoln Memorial offering personalized tours for $20–$50 per person — collecting cash upfront for 15-minute walks of basic placard information, while legitimate NPS ranger tours at the same sites are free and staffed by trained historians.

Outside major DC landmarks — the White House fence on Pennsylvania Avenue, the Capitol steps, the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool, the Arlington Cemetery entrance — self-appointed guides approach tourists with confident, polished pitches for personalized tours at $20–$50 per person. They target families with children by describing "kid-friendly" or "behind-the-scenes" experiences that sound more interesting than standing in the public areas, and they collect cash upfront before anything begins.

The tour that follows is 15 minutes of information available on any placard or smartphone, mixed with fabricated historical anecdotes. The guide then announces a "schedule conflict" and ends the session early, or steers the group to specific souvenir shops where he receives commissions — spending more time at shops than at historical sites. Some guides collect fees claiming to have "reserved spots" at the Capitol, the White House, or the Smithsonian museums — all of which are free to enter through official channels and require no third-party reservation.

The National Park Service provides free ranger-led walks at every major Mall memorial and monument, staffed by trained historians with accurate information and no upsell. Book any paid tour before arriving through Viator or GetYourGuide, which carry verified reviews and refund policies; never hand cash upfront to a guide who approached you at a landmark without a verifiable business name or website, regardless of how knowledgeable the pitch sounds.

Red Flags

  • Someone approaches you unsolicited outside a major landmark offering a personalized tour
  • The guide demands cash payment upfront before the tour begins
  • The guide promises access to places that are actually free to enter, like the Capitol or Smithsonian museums
  • The person cannot show any form of licensing, business card, or affiliation with a known tour company
  • The tour route includes stops at specific souvenir shops presented as 'local favorites'

How to Avoid

  • Book tours only through established companies with verifiable websites, TripAdvisor profiles, and posted reviews.
  • Take advantage of free National Park Service ranger-led walks and talks at all Mall memorials and monuments.
  • Reserve free Capitol, White House, and Bureau of Engraving tours through official.gov websites well in advance.
  • All Smithsonian museums are free; never pay anyone for 'skip the line' access to these institutions.
  • If you want a paid tour, book through platforms like Viator or GetYourGuide where reviews and refund policies are transparent.
Scam #7
Distraction Pickpocketing on the Metro
⚠️ High
📍 Gallery Place-Chinatown station, Metro Center transfer platform, crowded Orange/Blue/Silver line trains, Smithsonian station escalators

Two-person pickpocket teams work DC Metro stations and trains — one bumps or distracts with a complex question while the other lifts wallets, phones, or passports — with Gallery Place, Metro Center, and Smithsonian stations during event days being the highest-risk locations, and losses ranging from $300 to identity theft.

Gallery Place-Chinatown, Metro Center, and Smithsonian stations carry concentrated pickpocket risk during event days, rush hour, and peak cherry blossom season. Teams of two work the platform approaches and escalator runs: one person bumps you, drops something nearby, or asks an unusually complicated question while standing close — the kind of interaction that feels socially demanding to disengage from without appearing rude.

While your attention is on the front interaction, the partner reaches into your back pocket, side bag, or open jacket pocket. The "sandwich" technique — one person ahead of you on a slow escalator while another presses close behind — is the Metro-specific variant most frequently documented in traveler reports. Victims typically don't discover the loss until they try to pay somewhere else. Losses range from a replaced phone ($300–$1,200) to the full cascading cost of stolen credit cards and identity exposure.

Carrying your wallet in a front pocket and keeping your bag zipped and against your body removes the most common access points these teams rely on. On escalators and during boarding, stand with your back to a wall or railing rather than to the crowd — it eliminates the rear partner's operating position entirely; report pickpocketing to Metro Transit Police at (202) 962-2121 immediately and cancel stolen cards before leaving the station.

Red Flags

  • Someone bumps into you unnecessarily hard in a Metro station or on a train
  • A stranger drops something near you and makes a production of picking it up while you are in a tight space
  • You feel someone pressing unusually close to your bag or pockets in a crowd that is not dense enough to justify it
  • Someone asks you an overly complicated question while standing very close on a platform or escalator
  • A group of people suddenly surrounds you during boarding or exiting the train

How to Avoid

  • Carry your wallet in a front pocket and keep your bag zipped and positioned in front of your body at all times on the Metro.
  • Use a money belt or neck wallet for your passport, large cash, and backup credit card when touring the city.
  • Be especially vigilant on escalators at Smithsonian, Metro Center, and Gallery Place stations during peak tourist season.
  • Keep your phone secured and avoid using it while walking through crowded station corridors.
  • Report pickpocketing immediately to Metro Transit Police at (202) 962-2121 and cancel stolen cards right away.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Metropolitan Police Department (MPDC) station. Call (202) 727-9099. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at MPDC Online Reporting.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your embassy on Embassy Row along Massachusetts Avenue NW; most embassies are located between Dupont Circle and the Naval Observatory

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
