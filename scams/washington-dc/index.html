Key Takeaways

  • The #1 reported scam is the Fake Parking Ticket QR Code.
  • 2 of 7 scams are rated high risk.
  • Use app-based ride services (Uber, Lyft) instead of unmarked vehicles or unlicensed cabs.
  • Never accept unsolicited offers from strangers near tourist sites in Washington DC.

⚡ Quick Safety Tips

  • Stick to well-lit, populated areas around the National Mall, Georgetown, and Dupont Circle; avoid walking alone east of the Capitol or in Southeast DC at night.
  • Use the Metro or official DC taxi services (identified by dome lights and proper licensing) rather than accepting rides from strangers outside Metro stations.
  • All Smithsonian museums and National Mall memorials are completely free; never pay anyone claiming to offer skip-the-line access or reserved entry.
  • Download the official WMATA app for Metro schedules and use SmarTrip cards instead of carrying large amounts of cash for transit.

The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Fake Parking Ticket QR Code
⚠️ High
📍 Georgetown streets, National Mall parking areas, Dupont Circle metered spots, any street with out-of-state plates

Scammers place realistic-looking parking tickets with QR codes on tourist vehicles — especially those with out-of-state plates in Georgetown, around the National Mall, and near Dupont Circle — directing victims to fraudulent payment websites where $75–$150 is charged and credit card data is harvested for further unauthorized use.

You return to your rental car parked on a Georgetown side street or near the National Mall and find what looks like an official DC Department of Transportation parking citation on the windshield. The design is convincing — city logos, official-sounding language, and a violation amount of around $75–$100. Tourists with out-of-state plates are specifically targeted because they are unfamiliar with local parking rules and less likely to question a ticket.

The ticket includes a QR code for quick payment. You scan it and land on a site that looks official — until you notice the URL ends in .com rather than .gov. By then you may have already entered your full card details. The fraudulent portal charges $75–$150 and stores your credit card information for additional unauthorized purchases discovered days later.

Pay any DC parking ticket only by going directly to dmv.dc.gov and searching for your violation by plate number — never by scanning a QR code on the ticket itself. Real DC citations come from the DC Department of Transportation or Metropolitan Police, never from 'DC DMV.' If a ticket looks suspicious, call (202) 737-4404 to verify with the DC DMV before entering any payment information.

Red Flags

  • The ticket has a QR code directing you to a website ending in.com instead of.gov
  • The citation lists the DC DMV as the issuing agency, which never issues parking tickets
  • The payment website asks for full credit card details rather than offering multiple payment options
  • The ticket appears on your windshield in an area where you are confident you parked legally
  • The paper quality or font looks slightly different from official government documents

How to Avoid

  • Only pay DC parking tickets through the official DC DMV website at dmv.dc.gov or by mail to the address listed on legitimate tickets.
  • Check the issuing agency: legitimate DC tickets come from the DC Department of Transportation (DDOT) or Metropolitan Police, never from 'DC DMV.'
  • Never scan a QR code on a parking ticket; instead, go directly to the official website and search for your violation by plate number.
  • Take a photo of the ticket and compare it with official DC citation examples available on the DC government website.
  • Report suspicious tickets to the DC DMV at (202) 737-4404 and file a complaint with the Better Business Bureau.
Scam #2
Metro Station Change Scam
🔶 Medium
📍 Smithsonian Metro station, Gallery Place-Chinatown Metro, Union Station entrance, L'Enfant Plaza Metro concourse

Scammers approach tourists inside Washington DC Metro stations — particularly at Smithsonian and Gallery Place — holding two $10 bills and asking to exchange them for a $20, then pocket the $20 and insist the victim only handed over a $1, for a typical net loss of $19–$45 that is too small to prompt a police report.

You are navigating the Smithsonian Metro station on your way to the National Mall when someone approaches holding two bills visibly. They seem in a hurry and ask a quick favor: can you change two $10s for a $20? The request feels harmless — a simple transaction, no different from breaking a bill at a register.

You hand over your $20. The person takes it smoothly, turns slightly, then holds up a $1 bill and claims you only gave them a single. Your $20 has been palmed in the handoff. A second person nearby may create a brief distraction at exactly that moment. In the $50 variant, the scammer hands back $5 bills folded to look like $20s. Losses run $19–$45 per victim — small enough that most people never file a police report, which is precisely the design.

Never make change for strangers in Metro stations — say 'no thank you' and keep walking without explaining yourself. If someone claims they cannot afford a fare, direct them to the station manager booth rather than reaching for your wallet. Be especially alert at Smithsonian, Gallery Place, and Union Station stations, which are the highest-density locations for this scam.

Red Flags

  • A stranger approaches you specifically inside a Metro station asking to make change for bills
  • The person is overly friendly and creates a sense of urgency about needing change quickly
  • A second person appears nearby and creates a distraction while the exchange happens
  • The scammer insists on handling your bills rather than letting you count the exchange yourself
  • The person targets tourists who are visibly consulting maps or looking at their phones

How to Avoid

  • Never make change for strangers in Metro stations or on the street; politely say 'no' and keep walking.
  • If someone needs Metro fare help, direct them to the station manager booth rather than giving cash.
  • Keep large bills in a secure wallet and avoid displaying cash in crowded transit areas.
  • Be especially alert at Smithsonian, Gallery Place, and Union Station Metro stops, which are prime hunting grounds.
  • If you feel pressured, walk toward the station manager booth or a Metro Transit Police officer.
Scam #3
Unlicensed Pedicab Price Gouging
🔶 Medium
📍 National Mall between the Capitol and Lincoln Memorial, around the Washington Monument, Jefferson Memorial area, Tidal Basin during cherry blossom season

Unlicensed pedicab operators on the National Mall — especially during cherry blossom season — quote no price upfront and then demand $50–$120 or more after a 10-minute ride, operating illegally on National Park Service property with no licensing board to complain to if you refuse to pay.

You have been walking the National Mall for hours when a pedicab driver pulls alongside and offers a lift to the next memorial. The price is deliberately vague: 'very reasonable,' 'just a small tip,' 'whatever you feel is right.' The ride is smooth and you arrive at the Lincoln Memorial or Jefferson Memorial relaxed and grateful.

The driver then announces the fare: $80 to $120 for a ten-minute trip. Some claim a rate of $5–$8 per minute but reveal this only after you have arrived. When you object, the driver points out that you agreed to board with no price set — there is no written agreement, no receipt, and no refund. These operators are frequently unlicensed and operating illegally on NPS property, so there is no licensing authority to appeal to. During cherry blossom season, when walking is difficult and crowds are overwhelming, the pressure to climb in is highest.

Only board a pedicab that displays a posted rate card and NPS authorization before you step in — if the driver cannot show either, decline and walk away. Ask for a total fare for your specific trip before sitting down, not a per-minute rate. The free DC Circulator bus and National Mall shuttle are legitimate zero-cost alternatives, and the Capitol-to-Lincoln Memorial walk is about two miles along flat ground.

Red Flags

  • The pedicab driver does not display a posted rate card or National Park Service authorization
  • No price is quoted before you get in, or the driver says 'we'll work it out after'
  • The driver is parked on park service property rather than at a designated pickup zone
  • The pedicab lacks visible business branding, licensing numbers, or insurance information
  • The driver aggressively solicits you rather than waiting at a designated stand

How to Avoid

  • Only use pedicab services that display clear per-hour pricing and are licensed by the National Park Service, such as Nonpartisan Pedicab.
  • Always confirm the total fare, whether it is per trip or per hour, and get a verbal agreement before boarding.
  • Take a photo of the rate card and the pedicab's license number before the ride begins.
  • Walk the National Mall instead; the distance from the Capitol to the Lincoln Memorial is about 2 miles and manageable for most visitors.
  • Use the free National Mall shuttle or DC Circulator bus ($1) as cheaper legitimate alternatives.

Scam #4
Friendship Bracelet and CD Hustle
🟢 Low
📍 National Mall near the Washington Monument, outside Smithsonian museums, Georgetown M Street, Adams Morgan on weekend nights

Street hustlers around the National Mall and Smithsonian museums press colorful bracelets onto tourists' wrists or thrust CDs into their hands as 'free gifts,' then aggressively demand $10–$20 in payment — and sometimes use the distraction to position an accomplice near bags and pockets to steal from the crowd.

You are walking near the Washington Monument when a smiling person reaches out and starts tying a colorful bracelet onto your wrist before you can react, chatting warmly about friendship and good luck. Or they press a CD into your hands saying it is free and they just want you to hear their music. The approach is fast and friendly enough that refusing feels rude.

Once the bracelet is on or the CD is in your hands, the tone shifts. The person demands $10–$20 as a 'donation' and becomes hostile if you try to return the item or walk away without paying. Meanwhile, a second person who was standing unusually close may be working the crowd near your bag or pockets. The financial demand is deliberately small — the bracelet or CD is the distraction, not the primary revenue stream.

Keep your hands in your pockets and say 'No thank you' without breaking stride — never let a stranger place anything on your wrist or press something into your hands. If a bracelet is already tied on, remove it and place it on the ground without paying — you are not obligated for unsolicited items. Wear your bag across your front and keep it zipped during any street interaction near the Mall.

Red Flags

  • Someone approaches you on the street and immediately tries to put something in your hand or on your wrist
  • The person claims the item is 'free' but then asks for a donation or payment
  • A second person lingers unusually close during the interaction
  • The person becomes aggressive or guilt-trips you when you try to decline or walk away
  • You are targeted specifically because you are taking photos, consulting a map, or carrying shopping bags

How to Avoid

  • Keep your hands in your pockets or at your sides and firmly say 'No thank you' without breaking stride.
  • Never allow a stranger to place anything on your body; physically step back if someone reaches for your wrist.
  • If a bracelet is tied on, remove it and place it on the ground; you are not obligated to pay for unsolicited items.
  • Keep your bag zipped and in front of you during any street interaction, especially near the National Mall.
  • If someone becomes aggressive, walk toward the nearest Park Police officer or into a Smithsonian museum entrance.
Scam #5
Three-Card Monte Gambling Hustle
🔶 Medium
📍 Near Union Station, Gallery Place-Chinatown sidewalks, busy intersections along Pennsylvania Avenue, downtown street corners

Teams of four to six people set up Three-Card Monte tables on DC sidewalks near Union Station, Gallery Place, and Pennsylvania Avenue — using planted shills who win visibly to lure tourists — then use sleight of hand to ensure bettors always lose, with typical losses of $50–$300 per encounter that victims rarely report.

A small crowd has gathered around a person shuffling three cards on a cardboard box near Union Station. Several spectators seem to be winning easily. A couple of them wave you over — the dealer is not very good, they say, and the trick is easy to spot. You watch a few rounds and think you see the pattern.

Your first small bet wins — the dealer allows it deliberately. You bet larger. This time the card is never where it appeared to be. The dealer uses practiced sleight of hand to ensure that once serious money is on the table, you cannot win. The friendly spectators who encouraged you are paid shills. Lookouts watch nearby corners and the whole operation folds within seconds if police appear. Losses run $50–$300, and few people report it because they feel embarrassed or realize that participating in street gambling is itself illegal in DC.

Never stop to watch Three-Card Monte even as a spectator — pickpockets work the crowd while the dealer holds everyone's attention. The DC Metropolitan Police Department states plainly that a player wins only when the dealer allows it, and the only reason for that is to lure in a larger bet. Walk past without pausing; if you see an active game, report it to Metropolitan Police at (202) 727-9099.

Red Flags

  • A small crowd gathers around a person shuffling cards on a box or makeshift table on the sidewalk
  • Several 'spectators' seem to be winning easily and encourage you to try your luck
  • The dealer lets you win your first small bet to build your confidence
  • The game is set up near busy tourist walking routes between the Mall and downtown attractions
  • Lookouts are positioned at nearby corners watching for police

How to Avoid

  • Never participate in street gambling games; the game is mathematically rigged through sleight of hand and you cannot win.
  • Walk past without stopping to watch, as even spectators can be targeted by pickpockets working with the group.
  • Understand that the people 'winning' are planted accomplices, not random lucky tourists.
  • Report Three-Card Monte operations to the Metropolitan Police at (202) 727-9099 or 911.
  • Remember that street gambling is illegal in DC and participants can be cited as well as victims.
Scam #6
Fake Tour Guide Solicitation
🟢 Low
📍 Outside the White House on Pennsylvania Avenue, near the Capitol steps, around the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool, Arlington Cemetery entrance

Self-appointed 'guides' approach tourists outside the White House, Capitol steps, Lincoln Memorial, and Arlington Cemetery collecting $20–$50 per person upfront for tours that deliver publicly available facts mixed with fabricated anecdotes — and sometimes route groups through souvenir shops where the guide earns a commission.

You are standing outside the White House on Pennsylvania Avenue reading the historical plaques when a personable stranger approaches with a confident tour pitch. They mention insider details, quote a price of $30 per person for a walking tour of the Mall, and seem knowledgeable enough that the offer sounds legitimate. They collect the cash before the walk begins.

The tour consists largely of information from the plaques you were already reading, plus some colorful anecdotes of uncertain accuracy. After 15 minutes, the guide announces a schedule conflict and stops responding. In one common variant, the guide steers the group past specific souvenir shops where they earn a commission, spending more time inside than at actual monuments. Another pitch claims to have 'reserved spots' at the Capitol or Bureau of Engraving — both of which are entirely free and require no facilitator.

Book any DC tour through a verified company with published TripAdvisor or Viator reviews and a refund policy — never pay an unsolicited stranger who approaches you outside a landmark. The National Park Service offers free ranger-led talks at all major Mall memorials. Capitol tours, White House tours, and the Bureau of Engraving are free through official .gov websites. All Smithsonian museums are free with no queue requiring a paid facilitator.

Red Flags

  • Someone approaches you unsolicited outside a major landmark offering a personalized tour
  • The guide demands cash payment upfront before the tour begins
  • The guide promises access to places that are actually free to enter, like the Capitol or Smithsonian museums
  • The person cannot show any form of licensing, business card, or affiliation with a known tour company
  • The tour route includes stops at specific souvenir shops presented as 'local favorites'

How to Avoid

  • Book tours only through established companies with verifiable websites, TripAdvisor profiles, and posted reviews.
  • Take advantage of free National Park Service ranger-led walks and talks at all Mall memorials and monuments.
  • Reserve free Capitol, White House, and Bureau of Engraving tours through official.gov websites well in advance.
  • All Smithsonian museums are free; never pay anyone for 'skip the line' access to these institutions.
  • If you want a paid tour, book through platforms like Viator or GetYourGuide where reviews and refund policies are transparent.
Scam #7
Distraction Pickpocketing on the Metro
⚠️ High
📍 Gallery Place-Chinatown station, Metro Center transfer platform, crowded Orange/Blue/Silver line trains, Smithsonian station escalators

Teams of two to three pickpockets work Washington DC's Metro — particularly at Gallery Place, Metro Center, and Smithsonian stations — using staged bumps, dropped items, or over-complicated questions to distract tourists while a partner lifts wallets, phones, or passports from bags and pockets, with losses ranging from a stolen phone's replacement cost to the cascading damage of identity theft.

You board the Metro at Gallery Place during a Capitals game or at Smithsonian during cherry blossom season. The platform is packed and the trains are crowded. You are consulting your phone for the transfer directions when someone bumps into you hard enough to make you look up.

While you registered the bump and checked that person's face, a second person has lifted your wallet from a back pocket or your phone from a bag that shifted open in the press of the crowd. The 'sandwich' technique places one person in front of you on an escalator and another pressing close behind. The 'door rush' targets the moment of boarding or exiting when bodies compress and attention scatters. Victims typically discover the theft only when they reach for their wallet at the next stop.

Carry your wallet in a front zip pocket and keep your bag zipped and positioned across your front — never in a back pocket or an open pouch on crowded Metro platforms or trains. Use a money belt or neck wallet for your passport and backup card on busy event days. On escalators at Smithsonian, Metro Center, and Gallery Place, stay aware of anyone pressing closer than the crowd density warrants. Report pickpocketing to Metro Transit Police at (202) 962-2121 and cancel stolen cards immediately.

Red Flags

  • Someone bumps into you unnecessarily hard in a Metro station or on a train
  • A stranger drops something near you and makes a production of picking it up while you are in a tight space
  • You feel someone pressing unusually close to your bag or pockets in a crowd that is not dense enough to justify it
  • Someone asks you an overly complicated question while standing very close on a platform or escalator
  • A group of people suddenly surrounds you during boarding or exiting the train

How to Avoid

  • Carry your wallet in a front pocket and keep your bag zipped and positioned in front of your body at all times on the Metro.
  • Use a money belt or neck wallet for your passport, large cash, and backup credit card when touring the city.
  • Be especially vigilant on escalators at Smithsonian, Metro Center, and Gallery Place stations during peak tourist season.
  • Keep your phone secured and avoid using it while walking through crowded station corridors.
  • Report pickpocketing immediately to Metro Transit Police at (202) 962-2121 and cancel stolen cards right away.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Metropolitan Police Department (MPDC) station. Call (202) 727-9099. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at MPDC Online Reporting.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your embassy on Embassy Row along Massachusetts Avenue NW; most embassies are located between Dupont Circle and the Naval Observatory

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

The National Mall is patrolled by US Park Police 24/7 and is generally safe for evening walks near the well-lit memorials. However, the less-trafficked areas between monuments can be isolated after dark, so stick to lit pathways and visit popular memorials like the Lincoln and WWII memorials where other visitors are present.
DC Metro stations are generally safe, especially during daytime hours. Metro Transit Police patrol stations, but tourists should remain alert for pickpockets during rush hour and large events. Avoid empty rail cars late at night and stay near other passengers.
For legitimate paid tours, a tip of 15-20% is customary. For free National Park Service ranger talks, tips are not expected or accepted. Never tip or pay someone who approaches you unsolicited offering a tour.
Driving in DC is challenging due to traffic circles, restricted lanes, and aggressive parking enforcement. Street parking is expensive and confusing, and predatory towing is common. Consider using Metro instead, which reaches all major tourist sites.
