The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Shell Game Hustle
⚠️ High
📍 Magnificent Mile, CTA Red Line trains, State Street, Michigan Avenue sidewalks

On the Magnificent Mile and CTA Red Line trains, a dealer and a ring of planted accomplices run a rigged three-cup ball game that lures visitors into losing $50–$200 or more, while other crew members simultaneously work the watching crowd as pickpockets.

The setup looks like a moment of street luck. On Michigan Avenue or inside a Red Line car, a man shuffles three plastic cups over a small red ball on a portable board. People crowd around as two or three apparent spectators bet and win easily, calling out tips and encouraging bystanders to try. The location is chosen precisely — heavily trafficked tourist corridors where new faces arrive constantly and a small crowd forms without suspicion.

The moment real money goes down, it disappears. The dealer uses a sleight-of-hand palm move that ensures the ball is never under the cup the mark selects. Every winner in the audience was a paid accomplice — a shill whose only job is to make the game look beatable. A Streeterville resident reported the operation to CBS Chicago, which confirmed the ring targets the Mag Mile. The trap compounds on CTA trains: while riders watch the cups, other crew members work the audience as pickpockets, lifting wallets and phones from people whose attention is entirely on the game. The crew disperses in seconds when a police uniform appears — portable boards fold flat and everyone scatters in different directions.

The real danger is the crowd, not just the game. Every onlooker cheering you on is likely a crew member, and the pickpockets are already working while your eyes are on the cups. Walk past any street game without slowing down — even a brief pause is enough for a pickpocket to mark you as a target. If you witness the operation, report it to CPD non-emergency at 311 rather than intervening directly.

Red Flags

  • A crowd has gathered around someone shuffling cups or cards on a portable surface
  • Multiple people seem to be winning easily — they are planted accomplices
  • Someone in the crowd nudges you or encourages you to bet, saying 'it's easy'
  • The setup is on a foldable table or board that can disappear in seconds
  • You notice people standing unusually close to you in the crowd — potential pickpockets

How to Avoid

  • Never gamble with street dealers — shell games are mathematically rigged cons, not games of skill.
  • The 'winners' you see are always planted accomplices working with the dealer.
  • If a crowd has gathered around a street game, walk the other direction immediately.
  • Keep your phone and wallet secure if you even pause near a game — pickpockets work the audience.
  • Report shell game operators to CPD non-emergency at 311 or (312) 746-6000.
Scam #2
Fake Taxi and Rideshare at O'Hare
⚠️ High
📍 O'Hare International Airport arrivals, Midway Airport, Union Station, downtown hotel areas

Unauthorized 'pirate taxi' drivers working O'Hare and Midway airport terminals approach arriving travelers posing as rideshare or cab services, then charge three to five times the standard fare — and in at least one 2024 case documented by CWB Chicago, a driver kidnapped victims and forced them to withdraw cash at ATMs.

The pitch comes before you reach the official taxi or rideshare pickup zones. A man approaches in baggage claim or just outside the arrivals doors, addresses you by destination — 'you need downtown?' — and quotes a flat rate that sounds reasonable after a long flight. Vehicles often display both taxi markings and rideshare app stickers to look legitimate from any angle, and the driver carries a card or wears a lanyard mimicking official airport credentials.

Once inside the vehicle, the meter never runs because there is no meter — the 'flat rate' inflates on arrival. WGN-TV planted an investigator who found four unauthorized drivers soliciting rides within 45 minutes at O'Hare; one offered a '$29 flat fee' while displaying a taxi medallion and an Uber sticker simultaneously and was cited for deceptive practice. In a 2024 Cook County prosecution, a driver picked women up from O'Hare under the pretense of a cab service, then drove them to ATMs under duress. In ordinary overcharge cases, victims have found $200–$300 charges for rides that should cost $45–$55 by meter.

The key rule is spatial: a legitimate driver will never approach you inside a terminal. Only request rideshares through the official Uber or Lyft app from the designated pickup zone, and verify the driver's name, car model, and plate on your screen before opening the door. Official yellow cab stands are curbside at arrivals — any driver who walks up to you before you reach them is unauthorized.

Red Flags

  • Any driver who approaches you inside the terminal or baggage claim offering rides
  • A vehicle displaying both taxi markings and a rideshare app sticker simultaneously
  • The driver quotes a flat fee rather than using a meter or app-based pricing
  • No visible city-issued taxi medallion number or chauffeur license on display
  • The driver insists on cash payment or processes your card on a personal device

How to Avoid

  • Always request rideshares through the official Uber or Lyft app and verify the driver, car model, and license plate before entering.
  • Use only taxis from the official taxi stand — never accept rides from anyone who approaches you inside the terminal.
  • For licensed taxis, use the Curb or Arro apps which connect you with vetted drivers at no surge pricing.
  • Report suspicious drivers to 311 or airport police immediately.
  • The standard taxi fare from O'Hare to downtown Chicago is approximately $40-50 with meter — anything quoted as a 'flat rate' inside the terminal is suspect.
Scam #3
Magnificent Mile Pickpocketing
⚠️ High
📍 Magnificent Mile (N. Michigan Avenue), Streeterville, State Street shopping district, Millennium Park

Organized pickpocket rings work the Magnificent Mile and Millennium Park in coordinated teams, using shoulder bumps, staged spills, and crowd misdirection to lift wallets and phones — then exploit stolen devices to drain bank accounts through Venmo and Zelle before victims notice anything is missing.

On a busy weekend afternoon on North Michigan Avenue, a visitor is carrying shopping bags and distracted by storefronts. A person bumps into them apologetically, creating a brief moment of disorientation. Nearby, a second team member is already moving. NBC Chicago linked a coordinated ring to multiple incidents across the Streeterville and Mag Mile corridor, and CBS Chicago confirmed that scammers specifically target tourists during peak periods — holiday season, summer weekends, and major event days.

The distraction is engineered to look accidental. Some teams use the spill approach — coffee or sauce lands on the victim, two people rush to help clean it up, and a third lifts the wallet while everyone else is focused on the stain. Others stage a nearby argument that draws gawkers, then move in on distracted bystanders. The theft takes under three seconds from first contact. Police documents show suspects using stolen phones to send themselves funds through Venmo and Zelle within minutes — before victims realize the phone is gone and before banks flag the transactions as suspicious.

Speed is the thieves' advantage and awareness is yours. The moment any physical contact occurs in a crowded area — a bump, a spill, an unexpected touch — your first reflex should be checking your pockets, not the distraction. Carry your phone in a front pocket or zipped crossbody bag and enable real-time transaction alerts on all payment apps so you receive an immediate notification if your device is used for any transfer, and lock it with biometric authentication so a stolen unlocked phone cannot reach payment apps.

Red Flags

  • Someone bumps into you and is excessively apologetic in a crowded shopping area
  • A sudden commotion or argument breaks out right next to you — classic misdirection
  • A stranger accidentally spills something on you and rushes to help clean it up
  • A group of people seem to be coordinating movements around you or watching specific targets
  • Someone stands unusually close to you in a space where there is room to spread out

How to Avoid

  • Keep your phone in a front pocket or zippered crossbody bag — never in a back pocket or open purse.
  • If someone spills something on you, immediately clutch your belongings and step away before dealing with the stain.
  • Be especially vigilant during holiday shopping season when the Magnificent Mile is most crowded.
  • Enable instant transaction alerts on all banking and payment apps so unauthorized charges appear immediately.
  • If bumped or jostled, check all your belongings immediately — do not wait until you reach your hotel.

Scam #4
Fake Charity and Petition Hustle
🔶 Medium
📍 Michigan Avenue, State Street, outside Millennium Park, Navy Pier entrance area

Near Navy Pier, Millennium Park, and along Michigan Avenue, solicitors approach tourists with clipboards or flyers for nonexistent youth sports teams and deaf-mute charities, collect cash donations on the spot, and grow aggressive toward anyone who tries to leave without paying.

Outside Navy Pier or near Millennium Park, a young man in a branded youth sports t-shirt approaches with a clipboard and a practiced story about keeping kids off Chicago's streets. He has a flyer, a team name, and just enough specifics — neighborhood, upcoming tournament, coach's first name — to sound credible. The setup is designed to engage you emotionally before you have time to be skeptical, and the friendliness of the appeal makes saying no feel unnecessarily cold.

After a donation, the story evaporates. The team does not exist — Chicago tourism forums and TripAdvisor have documented the 'Raptors' basketball hustle and its variants for years. The clipboard version goes further: someone asks you to sign a petition for a deaf-mute charity, then points to a minimum donation line after you have already signed. The Chicago Loop Alliance launched a public awareness campaign about the problem downtown precisely because the volume is high enough to affect the visitor experience. Restaurants in the Loop have reported solicitors entering dining rooms and moving table to table demanding money from seated guests.

Legitimate organizations do not send people to solicit cash from pedestrians on the street. Keep walking without engaging — a firm 'no thank you' delivered while maintaining pace ends most approaches instantly, whereas stopping to explain signals that sustained pressure will eventually work. If a solicitor follows you or enters a restaurant to harass other guests, alert staff who can call 311.

Red Flags

  • Someone approaches with a flyer or clipboard asking for donations to an organization you have never heard of
  • The charity has no verifiable website, EIN number, or physical address
  • A person claims to represent a youth sports team and asks for cash donations on the street
  • You are asked to sign a petition first, then pressured for a donation after signing
  • The person becomes aggressive or follows you when you decline

How to Avoid

  • Never donate cash on the street — legitimate charities do not solicit donations from pedestrians this way.
  • Say 'no thank you' firmly and keep walking without breaking stride or making eye contact.
  • If you want to support youth programs, donate through verified organizations like Chicago Park District or After School Matters.
  • Do not sign any petitions or clipboards on the street — your signature and information can be misused.
  • If someone enters a restaurant and harasses diners, alert staff immediately — businesses can call 311.
Scam #5
CTA Distraction Robbery
⚠️ High
📍 CTA Red Line and Blue Line trains, Roosevelt station, downtown Loop stations, Lake/State station

Coordinated groups of two to seven people board CTA Red and Blue Line trains, surround lone riders, then use a staged distraction to snatch phones and wallets — a pattern serious enough that Chicago Police issued a formal 2025 community alert covering at least seven incidents.

Late at night, or on a quiet mid-day run, a small group boards a Red Line car and positions itself around a solo passenger without sitting down. An Illinois Policy Institute analysis found that the most common crimes at CTA stations are battery followed by deceptive practices, and the Red Line Roosevelt station recorded the highest incident rate in the network. The train environment suits the crew perfectly: enclosed, moving, with few witnesses and a station stop every two minutes where they can exit and scatter.

The mechanics are fast and practiced. One person starts a loud argument or commotion directly in front of the target. Another approaches from behind and bumps them. In the two-second window while the victim processes the distraction, a phone in hand or a wallet in a jacket pocket is gone. Chicago police's 2025 community alert described groups using force as well as misdirection — some incidents involved phones physically snatched mid-scroll. The CTA shell game scam runs in parallel during daytime on the same lines, with pickpockets embedded in the crowd watching the cups game.

The crew counts on you staying seated and passive. If you notice a group positioning itself around you on a train, stand and move to a different car at the next stop — this single action breaks the setup before it can complete. Keep your phone stored and out of sight when riding, and use the operator's car (the first car) after dark, which is the most monitored section of the train.

Red Flags

  • A group of people suddenly surrounds you or positions themselves on both sides of you on the train
  • Someone starts a loud argument or creates a distraction nearby while others move closer
  • Your phone is visible in your hand and someone keeps glancing at it
  • You are riding alone late at night on a sparsely populated car
  • People enter your train car and do not sit down, instead standing close to doors and near passengers

How to Avoid

  • Keep your phone secured and out of sight on CTA trains — do not hold it loosely or rest it on your lap.
  • Ride in the car closest to the train operator, which is typically the most monitored.
  • Avoid riding alone on the CTA late at night — use rideshare services after 10 PM if traveling solo.
  • If a group moves to surround you, stand up immediately and move to another car at the next station.
  • Report incidents to CTA security at (888) 968-7282 or text 'CTA' to 78328 in an emergency.
Scam #6
Navy Pier and Event Ticket Scam
🔶 Medium
📍 Navy Pier entrance, Wrigley Field area, United Center vicinity, Soldier Field surroundings, Millennium Park event areas

Street scalpers outside Chicago venues — Wrigley Field, United Center, and Navy Pier — sell counterfeit or duplicate-barcode tickets to sold-out events for $80–$300 cash, a racket serious enough that Chicago Police arrested an eight-person ring that ran it for more than a decade.

Outside a sold-out Cubs game at Wrigley or a concert near Navy Pier, a man approaches with printed tickets at a price just slightly above face value — reassuringly modest for a sellout show. The pitch is calibrated: he knows the venue is sold out, he knows you want in, and the price is low enough to seem like a deal rather than a trap. He may flash a phone screen showing what looks like a Ticketmaster listing, or hold up a paper ticket with an authentic-looking barcode.

The tickets are worthless at the gate. They may be photocopies with duplicated barcodes that were scanned when the legitimate holder entered, or the same barcode printed and sold to multiple people — only the first scanner gets in. Chicago Police arrested eight members of a ring that ran this operation for more than a decade, and the Illinois Attorney General issued a formal consumer alert on the practice. Even scalpers with real tickets inflate prices dramatically for last-minute buyers who are too pressed to compare options. Cash-only demands mean no dispute recourse once the exchange is done.

A ticket bought on the street from a stranger has no consumer protection of any kind. Buy tickets exclusively through the official box office or verified resale platforms like StubHub or SeatGeek, which guarantee authenticity and refund invalid entries. If you arrive without a ticket, watching the game at a nearby bar is a better outcome than paying $300 for a counterfeit.

Red Flags

  • Anyone selling tickets on the street outside a venue rather than through official channels
  • Ticket prices that seem too good to be true for a sold-out event
  • The seller insists on cash only and refuses to show identification
  • The tickets are printed on plain paper rather than official ticket stock
  • The seller becomes agitated when you want to verify the ticket before purchasing

How to Avoid

  • Buy tickets only from official box offices, Ticketmaster, or other authorized resellers.
  • If buying resale, use platforms like StubHub or SeatGeek that guarantee ticket authenticity.
  • Never buy tickets from someone on the street — even if they look real, duplicated barcodes are undetectable visually.
  • Pay with a credit card for dispute protection — never cash for street ticket purchases.
  • For Red Sox, Bears, or Blackhawks games, check the official team resale marketplace first.
Scam #7
Friendship Bracelet and CD Force
🔶 Medium
📍 Millennium Park, Buckingham Fountain, Michigan Avenue, Riverwalk, outside major museums

Near Millennium Park and along Michigan Avenue, street hustlers tie braided bracelets onto tourists' wrists or thrust unsolicited CDs into their hands, declare the items free gifts, then aggressively demand $10–$20 cash per item and physically block departure until paid.

At the Cloud Gate sculpture in Millennium Park, a man approaches a visitor with a coiled bracelet and ties it onto her wrist before she can step back. He introduces himself warmly, explains the bracelet is a blessing from the city, and seems genuinely friendly — no money is mentioned yet. A moment later a second person places beaded jewelry around her neck. The gift framing is deliberate: it creates a felt obligation before any transaction has been proposed, and the physical placement on your body makes declining far harder than refusing something you were only offered.

The demand follows immediately. Each person claims the items are handmade and blessed, worth far more than the $10–$20 they are asking, and that refusing to pay would be insulting their generosity. When the visitor tries to remove the bracelet, one man grabs her wrist. A parallel version runs on Michigan Avenue with music CDs — someone thrusts an album into your hands, calls it a promotional gift, then turns aggressive when you try to hand it back. Chicago tourism forums document both versions consistently, with the bracelet scam concentrated around Cloud Gate and Buckingham Fountain and the CD hustle focused on the Mag Mile shopping stretch.

Nothing placed on your body without permission creates any obligation. Step back immediately and put your hands up if anyone reaches toward you with a bracelet or item — physical distance before contact is far easier than untying a knot under social pressure. If a bracelet is already on your wrist, remove it calmly and set it on the ground; you owe nothing regardless of what was declared about blessings or gifts.

Red Flags

  • Someone approaches and places a bracelet, beads, or any item on your body without asking permission
  • A stranger hands you a CD and calls it 'free' before immediately demanding payment
  • The item is described as a 'blessing' or 'gift from the city' but a payment demand follows
  • Multiple people close in around you, creating a sense of being trapped
  • The person becomes verbally aggressive when you try to decline or return the item

How to Avoid

  • Keep your hands in your pockets or crossed when walking through heavy tourist areas.
  • Do not allow anyone to place anything on your body — step back immediately with palms up.
  • If a bracelet is tied on you, you owe nothing — remove it and place it on the ground.
  • Never accept a CD or item handed to you on the street — say 'no' before it reaches your hands.
  • Walk with purpose and confidence — scammers target people who appear hesitant or easily stopped.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Chicago Police Department (CPD) station. Call 911 (Emergency) or 311 / (312) 746-6000 (Non-Emergency). Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at chicago.gov/cpd.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

For international visitors, contact your country's consulate in Chicago. Many nations maintain consulates downtown. US State Department emergency line: +1-888-407-4747 (from US) or +1-202-501-4444 (international).

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
