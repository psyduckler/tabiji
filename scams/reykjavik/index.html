Key Takeaways

  • The #1 reported scam is the Keflavik Arrivals Taxi-Tout Per-Person Quote.

⚡ Quick Safety Tips

  • Book Northern Lights tours and glacier excursions only through operators with verifiable reviews — some budget operators cancel without refunds.

  • Don't leave valuables visible in rental cars at tourist stops — car break-ins at remote sites like Seljalandsfoss happen despite Iceland's low crime rate.

The 6 Scams

Scam #1
The Keflavik Arrivals Taxi-Tout Per-Person Quote
⚠️ High
📍 Keflavík International Airport (KEF) arrivals curb, the area between the Flybus stop and the official taxi rank, the unmarked-cab line past the curb

You land at Keflavík at 7 a.m. after an overnight flight, walk past the duty-free maze, and step onto the arrivals curb where the wind is sharp and the buses are pulling out.

A driver in a black jacket steps forward, smiles, and asks where you are going. You name a hotel near Hallgrímskirkja in central Reykjavík, fifty kilometers away. He quotes you what sounds like a reasonable rate — 'ten thousand króna, very fair,' he says — and waves you toward a sedan parked along the curb that has a small 'TAXI' sticker on the windshield but no company branding on the doors. You are tired. You climb in.

Forty-five minutes later he pulls up at your hotel and presents the bill. The number is now 24,000 ISK (about €165). The 'ten thousand' he quoted, he explains calmly, was per person — there are two of you, plus a 4,000 ISK 'starting fare' or 'airport surcharge' or 'morning rate' that he pulls out of nowhere. Some drivers run a meter that ticks at three or four times the legitimate rate; others simply quote a number that bears no relation to the metered fare.

The legitimate metered fare from Keflavík to central Reykjavík runs about 22,500–28,000 ISK (€155–195) on the official Hreyfill or BSR Taxi tariff, which is itself expensive because Iceland is expensive — but only twice the price of a Flybus ticket, not four times. Iceland has no Uber; the rideshare gap means tired arrivals at KEF are the most consistent target market for unethical taxi operators, and recent deregulation has allowed more unbranded cabs to enter the airport market.

The cleanest fix is to skip the curb entirely. The Flybus and Flybus+ shuttles run every flight arrival from KEF to the BSÍ bus terminal in central Reykjavík for about 4,000 ISK (€28), with a hotel-doorstep upgrade option for 6,000 ISK. The buses are scheduled to meet every flight, accept credit cards, and run the same route in roughly the same time as a taxi. Bookable on flybus.is, also available at the kiosk inside arrivals.

Book Flybus or Flybus+ on the airport Wi-Fi or at the kiosk inside arrivals — 4,000–6,000 ISK to the BSÍ terminal or your hotel doorstep, fixed price, no negotiation. If you prefer a taxi, walk past the curb to the official Hreyfill or BSR Taxi rank, use only branded cars, and confirm the fixed flat rate (22,500 ISK for a 4-seater as of 2025) before sitting down. Refuse any 'per-person' or 'starting fare' framing — taxi pricing in Iceland is per vehicle, not per passenger. If a driver overcharges, refuse to pay the disputed amount, dial 112 (Iceland's single emergency number for police, fire, ambulance), and report the plate number to the airport authority at +354 425 6000.

Red Flags

  • Taxi driver approaches you inside the terminal rather than waiting at the rank
  • No meter visible in the car
  • Driver quotes a price per person rather than per journey

How to Avoid

  • Book the official Flybus or Flybus+ shuttle in advance — far cheaper than a taxi.
  • If taking a taxi, use only Hreyfill or BSR Taxi and confirm the fixed rate (22,500 ISK for a 4-seater as of 2025).
  • Ask for the price before getting in and check the starting meter.
Scam #2
The Keflavik Currency Exchange Hidden-Commission
🔶 Medium
📍 Keflavík Airport currency-exchange booths in arrivals, the kiosks before the duty-free, and standalone Travelex-style booths in central Reykjavík

You walk past the Keflavík airport currency-exchange booth on the way to baggage claim, see the bright 'NO COMMISSION' sign, and decide to convert $200 USD to Icelandic króna while you have a moment.

The teller takes your two $100 bills, taps a calculator, and slides you 24,000 ISK across the counter. The board on the wall behind her shows USD/ISK at a rate that looks plausible. You count the bills, thank her, and walk on. It feels like a clean, painless transaction, and the 'no commission' framing made it feel like a fair deal.

Back at your hotel that evening, you check the actual USD/ISK interbank rate on Wise or Google. $200 should have bought you about 27,500 ISK at the mid-rate. You have just lost 3,500 ISK — about $24 — on a single transaction. The 'no commission' sign was technically true: there was no separate commission charge. The fraud lives in the spread between the booth's posted rate and the interbank rate, and that spread runs 12–15% at airport exchange booths in Keflavík.

The deeper irony is that Iceland is almost entirely cashless. Card payment is accepted at every shop, café, gas station, hotel, parking meter, public bathroom, and Flybus kiosk in the country, and most Icelanders themselves rarely carry physical króna. You have just paid a 12–15% spread on currency you will mostly not need, when your debit card would have worked for everything you wanted to buy at a much better rate.

A second variation runs at card terminals across Reykjavík, where merchants offer Dynamic Currency Conversion at the moment of payment — 'do you want to be charged in USD or ISK?' Choosing your home currency at the terminal applies a poor exchange rate (5–10% worse than your bank's rate) plus a small markup. The pattern is documented in Wise, Revolut, and Visa consumer-protection materials, and is consistent across European tourist destinations.

Do not exchange currency at Keflavík airport or at standalone exchange booths in Reykjavík — the spread is 12–15% even with 'no commission' signs. Iceland is 95%+ cashless: your debit and credit cards work everywhere, including parking meters, public toilets, and tiny rural cafés. If you genuinely need cash, withdraw in ISK from an ATM at a major Icelandic bank (Landsbankinn, Íslandsbanki, Arion banki) inside Reykjavík, never at the airport. Decline DCC at every card terminal — always choose to be charged in ISK. Use Wise or Revolut for the closest-to-interbank conversion automatically.

Red Flags

  • Exchange booth is positioned right at arrivals for maximum convenience
  • Rate displayed without commission or fees shown clearly
  • Staff encourages large exchanges

How to Avoid

  • Don't exchange currency at the airport — Iceland is 95%+ cashless and your card works everywhere.
  • If you need cash, use an ATM inside the city at a bank branch (not airport kiosks).
  • Always decline dynamic currency conversion on card payments.
Scam #3
The Reykjavik Rental Gravel-Damage Surprise Charge
⚠️ High
📍 Reykjavík and Keflavík car-rental counters (Avis, Hertz, Europcar, Sixt, plus the discount chains Procar, Lava, Blue), the off-airport rental cluster on Vatnsmýrarvegur

You arrive at the rental counter at Keflavík to pick up the small 4WD you booked online for the Ring Road, and the agent immediately slides four laminated cards across the counter listing optional insurance products.

There is gravel protection (€25/day), sand-and-ash protection (€20/day), tire protection (€15/day), undercarriage protection (€18/day), and a Super CDW package that bundles all of them at €60/day. Your rental was advertised at €80/day; the optional add-ons would push it to €140. The agent describes Iceland's gravel roads, the windblown sand on the south coast, the volcanic ash, and the F-roads with worn tone, and you start to feel that declining is irresponsible. You decline gravel and tire to save money, accept the basic CDW that came with the booking, and drive off.

Ten days later you return the car, having driven the full Ring Road without incident. The agent does a slow walk-around with a small flashlight in poor lighting and stops at a barely-visible chip in the windshield, a fine scratch on the front bumper, and what he calls 'gravel pitting' on the passenger door. He produces a printed quote: €380 for the windshield (which already had a star when you collected the car), €220 for the bumper, €450 for the door pitting (which you cannot see at all in normal light). The total is €1,050.

The pattern is the most consistently reported high-loss tourist scam in Iceland, documented across r/VisitingIceland, the long-running Lonely Planet Iceland forum, and formal warnings from the Icelandic Consumer Authority (Neytendastofa). Some discount chains (named in r/VisitingIceland threads as Lava, Blue, Geysir Car Rental's discount tier, and a few smaller operators) appear to operate the gravel-damage charge as a routine business model rather than a rare incident. Major chains like Avis, Hertz, Europcar, and Sixt have a fraction of the complaints by volume.

The defense is mechanical and starts before you drive away. Iceland's 'standard' SCDW (Super Collision Damage Waiver) explicitly excludes gravel damage, sand damage, and tire damage in most contracts — the fine print is intentional, and the upsell is calibrated to the moment you realize the base coverage is inadequate. The right move is either to take Sand and Ash Protection if you are driving the south coast or F-roads (where damage really does happen), or to use a credit card with primary auto-rental coverage and document the vehicle exhaustively before driving.

Film every centimeter of the rental car before driving away — windshield, all four bumpers, all four wheels, both side mirrors, undercarriage if possible, and the roof — with the agent visibly in frame so the timestamp is verifiable. Email the video to yourself immediately so it has a server-stamped time. Take Sand and Ash Protection if you are driving the south coast or F-roads, and use only major chains (Avis, Hertz, Europcar, Sixt) with established complaint procedures, never the deep-discount chains. Read the SCDW exclusions before signing — gravel is almost always excluded. If a rental agency claims fake damage, refuse to pay the disputed amount, file a complaint with Neytendastofa at neytendastofa.is, and dispute via your card issuer.

Red Flags

  • Agent rushes through the vehicle inspection before you drive away
  • Damage documentation is incomplete or done in poor lighting
  • Agent pushes multiple overlapping insurance packages

How to Avoid

  • Film every centimeter of the car before driving away — including tires, undercarriage, and roof.
  • Email or WhatsApp the video to yourself as a timestamped record.
  • Understand that standard SCDW in Iceland does NOT cover gravel damage — read the fine print.

Scam #4
The Reykjavik 'Handmade Lopapeysa' Machine-Knit Counterfeit
🟢 Low
📍 Tourist shops on Laugavegur, Skólavörðustígur, the harbour-side shops near Harpa, the cluster around Hallgrímskirkja

You fall for the classic Icelandic lopapeysa — the wool sweater with the circular yoke pattern — displayed in a charming shop on Laugavegur, and the price tag says 24,000 ISK (about $170) with a label reading 'Made in Iceland.'

The shop has wood floors, soft lighting, a row of identical sweaters in every size lined up on a long shelf, and a friendly clerk who tells you the pattern is 'traditional.' You pay by card, fold the sweater into your bag, and walk out feeling like you have just bought the perfect souvenir of Iceland. The label inside reads 'Iceland' in a circular logo, which feels official enough.

Back home, a friend who knits shows you the truth. Real lopapeysa wool is Icelandic léttlopi or álafoss lopi — long-fiber, water-resistant, lanolin-rich, with a slightly hairy hand-feel that comes from the unique Icelandic sheep. Your sweater is machine-knit acrylic-and-merino blend produced in factories in China or Bulgaria using a generic Nordic yoke pattern, then labeled 'Iceland' (which describes design inspiration, not manufacture). The actual cost to produce was perhaps $20–30; you paid $170.

The handknitting Association of Iceland (Handprjónasamband Íslands) and the Icelandic Tourist Board have been raising this issue for years. Roughly half of the lopapeysa sold in Reykjavík tourist shops are mass-produced overseas with deceptive labeling. The legitimate version — hand-knit in Iceland from Icelandic wool — is sold through the Handknitting Association's shop at Skólavörðustígur 19, the Álafoss factory store in Mosfellsbær, and a handful of independent designers, and runs 30,000–50,000 ISK ($210–350) for a typical adult sweater.

The tells are consistent. Genuine lopapeysa have small imperfections — uneven stitches, slight asymmetries in the yoke, knot ends visible inside — because they are hand-knit by individual makers. Machine-perfect patterns, identical sweaters in every size, no variation between pieces, and labels that say 'Iceland' rather than 'Hand-knit in Iceland from Icelandic wool' all point to the imported version. The 'Made in Iceland' on a label, without 'Hand-knit,' is technically permitted as long as the design was created in Iceland — even if the physical sweater was knit in Asia.

Buy lopapeysa only from the Handknitting Association of Iceland shop at Skólavörðustígur 19, the Álafoss factory store in Mosfellsbær, or from a named individual designer with a transparent atelier — these are the only sources that guarantee hand-knit, Icelandic-wool sweaters. Look for slight imperfections in the yoke pattern, knot ends visible inside, and a 'Hand-knit in Iceland from Icelandic wool' tag with the maker's name. Expect to pay 30,000–50,000 ISK; anything significantly cheaper than this in Reykjavík is almost certainly machine-made overseas. If a shop misrepresents origin, file a complaint at Neytendastofa.

Red Flags

  • Label says 'Iceland' without specifying 'Handmade' or '100% Icelandic wool'
  • Price is suspiciously low for a hand-knitted item
  • Shop sells dozens of identical sweaters in all sizes

How to Avoid

  • Buy from the Handknitting Association of Iceland shop on Skólavörðustígur for guaranteed authenticity.
  • Genuine Lopapeysa have slight imperfections — machine-perfect patterns are a red flag.
  • Look for the 'Handmade in Iceland' certification tag.
Scam #5
The Reykjavik Hotel Bottled-Water Tap-Water Lie
🟢 Low
📍 Hotels in central Reykjavík (especially smaller independent hotels), restaurant tables across Laugavegur and the harbourside, hot-pool café upsells

You check into a small independent hotel in central Reykjavík after a long flight, the receptionist hands you the keys, and almost in passing she mentions that the tap water 'might not be suitable for drinking — sometimes it has a sulfur smell.'

She offers to put a case of mineral water in your room for 2,000 ISK (about $14). You accept without thinking — you do not want to risk getting sick on the first night, the price seems modest, and the hotel staff seems to be helpfully warning you. The mineral water arrives in glass bottles, neatly arranged on the desk. You drink it for the first two days, then a third when you cannot find tap water that does not smell faintly of eggs.

The truth is that Reykjavík's tap water is among the purest in the world. It comes directly from glacial springs and ancient lava aquifers, requires no filtration or chlorination, and is consistently ranked at the top of global drinking-water quality indices. The faint sulfur smell that some travelers detect comes only from the hot tap water, not the cold — Reykjavík uses geothermal water for the hot supply, which carries a slight egg odor that disappears within seconds and is harmless. Cold tap water, the kind you drink, has zero sulfur smell.

The pattern is documented across r/VisitingIceland, the Reykjavík Tourist Board's own consumer-protection page, and a famous 2018 case in which a hotel owner was publicly named for systematically discouraging guests from drinking tap water in order to sell bottled water at €15 a case. Most major hotels do not run this scam, and Icelandair Hotels, Hilton, Marriott, Hotel Borg, and Reykjavík Konsulat are all reputable. The pattern survives at smaller independent hotels and Airbnb hosts who treat the bottled-water sale as a margin opportunity.

A second variation runs at restaurants. The waiter brings a bottle of 'Iceland Spring' or 'Glacia' to the table without asking and pours it into your glasses. The bottle is then on the bill at 600–900 ISK. Some restaurants do this routinely, with the assumption that tourists will not push back. A polite 'tap water, please' (vatn, takk in Icelandic) is the simple counter, and Iceland's restaurant culture genuinely expects this — there is no social cost to ordering tap water in even the most expensive Reykjavík restaurants.

Drink the cold tap water in Reykjavík — it is genuinely exceptional, free, and consistently ranked among the world's purest. Bring a reusable water bottle and refill it everywhere, including from public fountains and bathroom sinks. The faint sulfur smell, if you encounter it, is only on the hot tap water (geothermal supply) and is harmless; the cold supply has zero smell. At restaurants, ask explicitly for tap water (vatn, takk) before any bottle reaches the table, and decline any bottled water offered without asking. If a hotel actively discourages tap-water drinking, that is a documented scam — choose a different hotel.

Red Flags

  • Hotel proactively warns you against drinking tap water
  • Restaurant brings bottled water to your table without asking
  • Any establishment discouraging tap water in Iceland

How to Avoid

  • Drink the tap water freely — it is genuinely exceptional.
  • Bring a reusable water bottle and refill it everywhere.
  • Politely decline bottled water in restaurants to avoid automatic charges.
Scam #6
The Reykjavik Northern Lights Tour No-Refund Cloud Out
🔶 Medium
📍 Reykjavík tour-operator booking desks at hotels, harbour-side bus terminals, online aggregators, and BSÍ Bus Terminal pickup points

You book an expensive Northern Lights bus tour from Reykjavík weeks in advance, the booking confirmation arrives with stunning aurora photos, and you are excited for the lifetime experience on your second night in Iceland.

The tour is 13,000 ISK (about $90) per person — you book for two, total $180. The pickup is at 8 p.m. from your hotel; the bus drives ninety minutes east to a 'dark sky site' on the Reykjanes peninsula and waits for two to three hours, then drives back. The photos on the booking page show vivid green and purple aurora bands across a clear sky, and the operator's marketing copy promises 'one of nature's greatest spectacles.'

On your tour night, the bus arrives, the guide is friendly, and you reach the dark site in good time. But the entire sky is socked in — eight-eighths cloud cover, zero visibility, no break the entire night. The guide tries gamely for forty-five minutes, drives ten kilometers down the road to try a different angle, and finally apologizes around 11 p.m. and drives the bus back to Reykjavík. You have seen exactly nothing.

When you ask about a refund, the operator points calmly to the fine print on the booking confirmation. The guarantee, it turns out, is for the attempt, not the aurora itself. Some operators offer a free rebook if you stay long enough to use it; many offer only a partial credit toward another tour, with terms that exclude peak-season dates or require a 30-day window. If you are leaving Iceland in two days, the rebook is worthless. Your $180 is gone.

The pattern is documented across r/VisitingIceland, the long-running Lonely Planet Iceland forum, and most updated guidebooks. The genuinely reputable Northern Lights operators in Reykjavík (Reykjavík Excursions, Grayline, Arctic Adventures, BusTravel Iceland) all offer free rebooking on cloud-out nights with no time limit and no peak-season exclusions, often with three or more rebook attempts allowed. The aggregator sites and small independent operators are the ones with restrictive fine print, and a $90 tour with a no-refund policy is the warning sign.

Book Northern Lights tours only with operators who explicitly offer free rebooking on cloud-out nights — Reykjavík Excursions, Grayline, Arctic Adventures, and BusTravel Iceland are the four most reputable, with no-quibble rebook policies. Check Veður (vedur.is) cloud-cover and aurora forecasts yourself the night of, and only commit if cloud cover is forecast under 50%. If a tour offers no refund or rebook, do not book it. Stay 4+ nights in Iceland to give yourself multiple aurora chances. If an operator refuses a documented rebook, dispute via your card issuer and file a complaint with Neytendastofa.

Red Flags

  • Tour advertised with photos of incredible auroras but no guarantee clause mentioned
  • Non-refundable deposit policy buried in terms
  • Operator discourages you from checking the weather forecast yourself

How to Avoid

  • Only book with operators who offer a free rebooking or full refund for cloudy nights.
  • Check Veður (Icelandic Met Office) aurora forecasts yourself before booking.
  • Read cancellation policy carefully — reputable operators always offer a rebook option.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Icelandic Police (Lögreglan) station. Call 112. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at logreglan.is.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The US Embassy in Reykjavik is at Laufásvegur 21, 101 Reykjavik. For emergencies: +354 595-2200.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
