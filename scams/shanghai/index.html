The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Nanjing Road Tea House / Bar Scam
⚠️ High
📍 Nanjing Road East and West pedestrian shopping area, side streets off People's Square, Tianjin Road, Bund approach

Strangers near Nanjing Road and People's Square approach tourists in fluent English with a "traditional tea ceremony" invitation, then present bills of ¥3,000–¥10,000 in locked side-street tea houses — Shanghai's most widely documented and consistently active tourist scam.

The Nanjing Road pedestrian strip draws tens of millions of tourists each year, and a small ecosystem of scammers has operated along its side streets for decades. Two friendly people approach in fluent English — "We're visiting from Xi'an, first time in Shanghai?" — and within a few minutes suggest a "traditional Chinese tea ceremony" at a place they know nearby. The venue is always a side street, always their choice, and always off the main pedestrian flow.

Inside, the tea is served and the atmosphere is pleasant. Then the bill arrives: ¥3,000–¥10,000 for a session that legitimately costs ¥150–¥300 at a real teahouse. Staff block the door. The people who brought you in disappear. The venue at 510 Tianjin Road has been flagged by name in traveler reports for running this operation continuously. If you pay with cash, the money is gone; credit card is the only option that leaves any recourse.

The only protection is not entering in the first place. Any stranger who approaches you near Nanjing Road, People's Square, or the Bund and steers the conversation toward "tea," "cultural experience," or "traditional ceremony" is running this scam — walk past with "bu yao xie xie" and do not engage further. For genuine Shanghai tea culture, the Huxinting Teahouse inside Yu Garden (¥150–¥300, posted menu, no solicitation) and hotel-concierge-recommended venues are the alternatives.

Red Flags

  • Strangers approach you in fluent English near Nanjing Road, People's Square, or the Bund
  • Invitation to 'traditional tea ceremony' or 'cultural experience' within minutes of meeting
  • Tea house is in a side street or upper floor you did not choose yourself
  • 510 Tianjin Road (SMOOTH dining bar) and similar named venues flagged on traveler reports
  • Door locks or staff block exit when the bill arrives

How to Avoid

  • Walk past any stranger approaching near Nanjing Road with 'bu yao xie xie.'
  • Never follow a stranger to a 'nearby' tea house — the venue is always the scam.
  • Avoid 510 Tianjin Road (SMOOTH dining bar) and any similar side-street 'tea' venue.
  • For genuine tea, go to Shanghai Propaganda Poster Art Centre cafe or hotel-concierge-recommended venues.
  • If trapped, photograph the bill, pay with credit card (for chargeback leverage), then report to 12315 and your card company.
Scam #2
Pudong (PVG) Airport Black Taxi & Meter Rigging
⚠️ High
📍 Pudong International Airport (PVG) arrivals hall, official taxi queue, Longyang Road Maglev terminus, Hongqiao Airport (SHA) secondary

Unlicensed drivers intercept tourists before they reach the official taxi queue at Pudong Airport, quoting ¥400–¥600 for a central Shanghai ride the metered fare covers for ¥180–¥220 — and meter tampering has been documented even inside licensed cabs at the Longyang Road Maglev terminus.

Pudong International Airport is 45 km from central Shanghai — a long ride worth ¥180–¥220 on a metered licensed taxi. Arriving tourists clearing customs are visible targets: luggage in hand, unfamiliar with the local fare, looking for the fastest exit. Unofficial drivers position themselves between the arrivals gate and the official queue, approaching in casual dress with immediate offers.

"Taxi? Shanghai city? 400 yuan? Cheap price." The quote sounds specific and confident, but it is 2–3x the metered rate. The Longyang Road Maglev terminus compounds the problem: travelers who take the 8-minute Maglev from the airport and arrive at Longyang Road looking for a connecting taxi find a documented second concentration of the same scam. Meter tampering has been caught on video at this location even inside licensed cabs.

The metered rate from PVG to central Shanghai should be ¥180–¥220 — any flat quote above that from someone in casual dress at arrivals is a scam. Take Shanghai Metro Line 2 directly from PVG station to People's Square for ¥7 in 90 minutes, or use DiDi (English interface, regulated fares, digital receipt) from the official rideshare pickup zone. If you take the Maglev, connect onward by Metro Line 2 or 7 at Longyang Road rather than getting into a taxi at the Maglev terminus.

Red Flags

  • Driver in casual dress approaches before you reach the official taxi queue
  • 'Fixed price' of ¥400+ quoted to central Shanghai (legitimate meter is ¥180–¥220)
  • Meter tampered to run faster than 3.2 yuan per km base rate
  • Luggage 'fee' added at destination before driver opens the boot
  • Longyang Road taxi rank after Maglev arrival — higher scam density than PVG airport itself

How to Avoid

  • Take Shanghai Metro Line 2 from PVG station: ¥7, 90 min, scam-proof.
  • Install DiDi before arrival; English interface, regulated fares, digital receipt.
  • If taking licensed taxi, say 'da biao' (打表) before boarding; screenshot DiDi fare as reference.
  • Photograph the taxi license number from the rear windscreen on entering.
  • If you take the Maglev, transfer to Metro Line 2/7 at Longyang Road, not a taxi.
Scam #3
Bund & People's Square Wine / Champagne Bar Overcharge
⚠️ High
📍 Bars near the Bund, Nanjing Road East/West side streets, People's Square north side, Xintiandi tourist-facing venues

An English-speaking woman near the Bund leads tourists to an upper-floor bar with no posted prices, orders champagne without discussing the bill, and disappears when a ¥6,000–¥20,000 check arrives while staff block the exit — one of Shanghai's highest-cost single-encounter scams.

The Bund promenade runs adjacent to Nanjing Road's bar and restaurant district, and the evening foot traffic provides easy cover for a practiced opener. A friendly, English-fluent woman starts conversation naturally — complimenting a camera, asking about the trip — then mentions she knows a "perfect sunset view" bar that is "very local." She seems genuine, leads the way confidently, and the offer feels personal.

Inside, the bar has a real view and genuine ambiance. She orders casually — "just one drink" — and a bottle of champagne arrives at the table without a price discussion. When the bill comes — ¥6,000–¥20,000 — she has disappeared. Staff block the door. The bar is the operation; she earns a commission. Cash and Alipay payments leave no recourse; credit card is the only transaction that can be disputed via chargeback.

The setup is always the same: someone you just met on the Bund recommends a specific bar and leads you there. Any bar recommendation from a stranger met on the Bund or Nanjing Road is a scam — choose your own venue, check for a posted English menu and card payment before ordering, and verify the price of every item before it arrives at the table. Legitimate Bund options — M on the Bund, Bar Rouge, the Waldorf Astoria's Long Bar, the Peace Hotel's Jazz Bar — all publish pricing and require reservations.

Red Flags

  • Stranger on the Bund or Nanjing Road invites you to a 'perfect sunset view' or 'local' bar
  • Bar is on an upper floor, side street, or unmarked entrance
  • No posted prices at entrance or on the menu; 'ordering' happens casually
  • Drink prices ¥500–¥2,000 per glass with no menu shown before serving
  • Staff become hostile or block exit when bill arrives

How to Avoid

  • Choose Bund-view bars yourself: M on the Bund, Bar Rouge, Hakkasan, Waldorf Long Bar, Peace Hotel Jazz Bar (all posted pricing, booking required).
  • Walk past any stranger offering a 'local bar' invitation on the Bund.
  • Always verify a bar has posted English menu and card payment BEFORE ordering.
  • Pay with credit card for chargeback leverage; cash and Alipay are much harder to dispute.
  • If trapped, photograph the bill and inflated prices; dispute via card and report to 12315.

Scam #4
Shanghai Tea Ceremony Ambush at Yuyuan & The Bund
🔶 Medium
📍 Yu Garden (Yuyuan) bazaar area, the Bund promenade, People's Square south side, pedestrian shopping zones

Strangers near Yu Garden's bazaar approach tourists with "English practice" or photo requests, then steer them to side-street tea houses where bills of ¥800–¥2,500 arrive — a milder variant of the Nanjing Road tea scam running on the district's 10 million annual visitors.

Yu Garden's bazaar area draws enormous tourist traffic, which sustains a lower-price version of the Nanjing Road tea scam. The approach is more photogenic: "Hello, we are students — can we practice English with you?" or "Excuse me, could you help us take a photo?" The conversation is warm, and a "nearby traditional tea ceremony" invitation arrives within ten minutes. It feels spontaneous; it is not.

The tea house is always in a side street off the bazaar, never inside Yu Garden itself. The bill arrives at ¥800–¥2,500 — lower than the Nanjing Road version but still 10–20 times legitimate pricing. Police, when they have appeared, have historically told victims they "willingly attended." The practical remedy is a credit-card chargeback via 12315, China's consumer-protection English line, not the police.

The invitation always originates with a stranger — the defense starts there. At Yu Garden, the Bund, and People's Square, walk past anyone approaching with "English practice" or "photo help" offers, and never follow a new acquaintance to a "nearby" tea venue of their choosing. For genuine tea at Yu Garden, the Huxinting Teahouse — the pavilion in the middle of the Yu Garden pond, serving tea since 1855 at ¥150–¥300 per person — has a clearly posted menu and no solicitation. Go there directly.

Red Flags

  • Strangers approach near Yu Garden, the Bund, or People's Square asking you to 'practice English' or 'take a photo'
  • Within 10 minutes, invitation to 'nearby tea ceremony' or 'traditional culture' appears
  • Tea house is in a side street or upper floor, not within Yu Garden's main walkway
  • Menu shown too briefly to read before ordering
  • Bill at ¥800+ per person for what should cost ¥150–¥300 at legitimate venues

How to Avoid

  • Walk past any stranger at Yu Garden, Bund, or People's Square offering 'English practice' or 'photo help.'
  • For genuine tea at Yu Garden, visit Huxinting Teahouse (the pavilion in the middle of the pond, 1855).
  • Published tea prices at Huxinting: ¥150–¥300 per person with clear menu.
  • Never follow a stranger to a 'nearby' tea house, even if they seem friendly.
  • Pay with credit card for chargeback leverage; report via 12315 and your card issuer.
Scam #5
Shanghai Disneyland 'Fast Pass' & Fake Ticket Scams
🔶 Medium
📍 Shanghai Disneyland main entrance (Pudong), Disney Resort Metro station, third-party resale sites, hotel-concierge packages

Touts near the Disney Resort Metro station and third-party resale platforms sell fake or heavily marked-up QR-code tickets for Shanghai Disneyland — outright counterfeits are rejected at the gate with no refund, and legitimate Disney Premier Access is resold at 1.5–2x the official ¥300–¥600 rate.

Shanghai Disneyland's complex ticketing tiers — day passes, two-day passes, Disney Premier Access fast-track, VIP tours — create confusion that third-party sellers exploit. Touts operate near the Disney Resort Metro station (Line 11) and through hotel-lobby packages and online resale platforms, positioning themselves as convenient alternatives to the official app for tourists unfamiliar with the system.

A "Disney Premier Access" reseller quotes ¥800 per person for fast-track entry when the official rate is ¥300–¥600 at the gate or through the Disney app. The worse version is outright fake tickets: QR codes that scan as invalid at the turnstile. The reseller is untraceable and the tourist is turned away with no recourse. Hotel-lobby "Shanghai Highlights" tour packages sometimes bundle Disneyland tickets at ¥1,500+ per person through the same markup chain.

There is one legitimate purchase channel for Disney tickets and Premier Access. Book all tickets directly through shanghaidisneyresort.com or the official Disney app — do not buy from hotel-lobby booths, street vendors near the Metro station, or any third-party resale platform regardless of how official it looks. For transit, Shanghai Metro Line 11 runs to Disney Resort station for ¥5–¥7 in 40 minutes from central Shanghai, removing any need for tout-connected transport packages.

Red Flags

  • Third-party reseller offers Disney Premier Access at ¥700+ when official rate is ¥300–¥600
  • QR-code ticket purchased from a street tout or hotel lobby booth
  • 'Bundled' Shanghai-highlights tour including Disney at ¥1,500+ per person
  • Seller insists on cash or Alipay payment; refuses credit card for chargeback leverage
  • Tickets delivered via WeChat or email in a format you cannot verify before arrival

How to Avoid

  • Book directly via Shanghai Disneyland official app or shanghaidisneyresort.com.
  • Disney Premier Access is ¥300–¥600; anything higher is a markup or scam.
  • Shanghai Metro Line 11 to Disney Resort station: ¥5–¥7, 40 min, scam-proof.
  • Pay with credit card for fraud protection; avoid cash/Alipay for chargeback reasons.
  • For hotel stays, book only Disney Resort Hotel or Toy Story Hotel via Disney's own site.
Scam #6
AP Xinyang Fake Market Bait-and-Switch
🔶 Medium
📍 AP Xinyang Fashion Market (Science & Technology Museum metro), Han City Fashion & Accessories Plaza, various 'underground fake' markets

Vendors at AP Xinyang Fashion Market and Han City agree on a price for replica watches or bags during negotiation, then hand over an inferior item after "wrapping it in back" — a bait-and-switch where the scam is in the specific item delivered, not the product category.

AP Xinyang at the Science & Technology Museum Metro station and Han City in Jing'an sell replica brand goods in plain sight — a legal-grey experience that draws curious tourists as much as bargain hunters. The negotiation theater is part of the market culture: vendors quote ¥500, buyers counter at ¥80, both end somewhere around ¥150–¥200. The problem is not the category; it is the moment after the price is agreed.

After agreeing on a price, the vendor offers to "wrap it nicely in the back" or "get you the better one from the stock room." The item that comes back is lower-grade than the one you inspected. A separate variant uses a "diamond tester" or "authenticity tool" to "prove" that stones in a fake Rolex are real — the device is rigged. For foreign travelers, a secondary risk applies: customs in the US, UK, and EU prohibit commercial counterfeits and can confiscate goods on arrival.

The bait-and-switch happens in the transition from the item you agreed on to the item you leave with. Hold the specific item you inspected in your own hands from the moment the price is agreed through payment — refuse any offer to "wrap it in back" or "check the inventory." Start negotiations at 10–15% of the first-quoted price and walk away if the vendor won't let you keep hold of the merchandise. If customs consequences concern you, consider Shanghai's Global Harbor, Xintiandi, or IFC Mall instead — genuine goods, receipts, and return policies.

Red Flags

  • Vendor offers to 'wrap in the back' or 'check for you' — this is the moment of the switch
  • Multiple 'grades' of the same product with opaque pricing
  • 'Diamond tester' or authenticity demo produced on request to prove a fake is 'real'
  • First-quoted price is 5–10x what you'd realistically pay
  • Vendor pressures you to pay in cash or Alipay without inspecting the item

How to Avoid

  • Start negotiations at 10–15% of first-quoted price; walk away if vendor refuses.
  • Hold the specific item yourself from inspection through payment; refuse 'wrapping in back.'
  • Understand home-country customs prohibit commercial counterfeits; personal-use amounts vary but risks are real.
  • Consider alternatives: Global Harbor, Xintiandi, IFC Mall for genuine shopping with returns.
  • If shopping anyway, budget only what you can afford to lose entirely.
Scam #7
Pudong Airport SIM Card Tout Markup
🟢 Low
📍 Pudong Airport arrivals hall, along airport transit paths, hotel check-in desks offering 'tourist SIM' packages

Unofficial SIM card touts at Pudong Airport arrivals charge ¥250–¥300 for tourist SIM cards that China Mobile and China Unicom counters sell for ¥100–¥150 — a 2–3x markup on a commodity, with some touts selling cards that stop working within a week of the promised 30-day window.

Pudong arrivals is a high-pressure environment: new country, no connectivity, luggage in hand, unfamiliar with the carrier landscape. SIM card touts position themselves near the arrivals exit, sometimes with clipboards or branded lanyards that suggest official affiliation. They quote ¥250–¥300 for a "tourist SIM card, 30GB, 30 days" — the same plan that China Mobile and China Unicom sell at their official counters for ¥100–¥150.

The tout's SIM may work initially but sometimes stops functioning after 7–10 days, well before the promised 30-day window. Once you've left the airport and the seller is gone, there is no recourse. Traveler reports group SIM-card touts with taxi touts as the same arrivals-hall ecosystem — people positioned at high-pressure transit moments to capture tourists before they find the official option a few meters away.

The official China Mobile and China Unicom counters are in the Pudong arrivals hall, clearly branded, and sell the same plans for ¥100–¥150. Walk past any informal SIM booth not visibly branded China Mobile or China Unicom, and buy connectivity only from an official carrier counter or purchase an eSIM from Airalo or Holafly before you fly. An eSIM (around US$15–18 for 5–10 GB on 30 days) activates before you land and eliminates the arrivals-hall pressure entirely.

Red Flags

  • Booth or person in arrivals hall not clearly branded China Mobile or China Unicom
  • Price over ¥150 for a 30-day + 30GB tourist SIM
  • Seller insists on cash payment and cannot provide a fapiao receipt
  • SIM 'for tourists' that requires you to provide passport scan to a third-party booth
  • Package promises 'unlimited' data — China carrier plans are always capped at 30GB or similar

How to Avoid

  • Buy an eSIM before flying: Airalo, Holafly, or Nomad sells China 5GB 30-day at US$15–US$30.
  • At PVG, use ONLY the official China Unicom or China Mobile counter — ¥100–¥150 for 30 days/30GB.
  • Ignore any SIM booth not clearly carrier-branded.
  • For short trips (<10 days), home-carrier roaming is often competitive.
  • Keep a receipt (fapiao) from whichever carrier you choose for any warranty or support needs.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Chinese Police (公安局) station. Call 110 (Police) or 120 (Ambulance). Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at mps.gov.cn.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The US Embassy in Beijing is at No. 55 An Jia Lou Road, Chaoyang District, Beijing 100600. For emergencies: +86 10-8531-3000.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
