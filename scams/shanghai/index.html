The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Nanjing Road Tea House / Bar Scam
⚠️ High
📍 Nanjing Road East and West pedestrian shopping area, side streets off People's Square, Tianjin Road, Bund approach

Pairs of fluent-English speakers posing as fellow tourists on Nanjing Road steer visitors into side-street tea houses, then present bills of ¥3,000–¥10,000 for a ceremony that costs a few dozen yuan, with staff blocking the door until payment is made.

On Nanjing Road's pedestrian shopping strip near People's Square, pairs of young men and women — fluent in English and dressed like tourists — approach visitors and introduce themselves as travelers from another province. They ask friendly questions, share recommendations, and within ten minutes suggest experiencing a "traditional Chinese tea ceremony" together at a nearby shop they happen to know.

The side-street tea house looks authentic, the host is gracious, and the tea is fine. Then the bill arrives: ¥3,000–¥10,000 for a ceremony that any honest tea shop charges a few dozen yuan for. Staff or associates block the door and do not return your belongings until you pay. The same ecosystem operates with "art student galleries" and "language exchange" invitations along the same stretch of road.

The entire operation depends on you following a stranger off the main road. Any person who approaches you near Nanjing Road, People's Square, or the Bund with an invitation to tea, a cultural experience, or a student art show is running this scam — walk past without engaging. For genuine Shanghai tea, visit Huxinting Teahouse inside Yu Garden's bazaar (posted prices, open since 1855, no solicitation) or ask your hotel concierge for a verified venue.

Red Flags

  • Strangers approach you in fluent English near Nanjing Road, People's Square, or the Bund
  • Invitation to 'traditional tea ceremony' or 'cultural experience' within minutes of meeting
  • Tea house is in a side street or upper floor you did not choose yourself
  • 510 Tianjin Road (SMOOTH dining bar) and similar named venues flagged on traveler reports
  • Door locks or staff block exit when the bill arrives

How to Avoid

  • Walk past any stranger approaching near Nanjing Road with 'bu yao xie xie.'
  • Never follow a stranger to a 'nearby' tea house — the venue is always the scam.
  • Avoid 510 Tianjin Road (SMOOTH dining bar) and any similar side-street 'tea' venue.
  • For genuine tea, go to Shanghai Propaganda Poster Art Centre cafe or hotel-concierge-recommended venues.
  • If trapped, photograph the bill, pay with credit card (for chargeback leverage), then report to 12315 and your card company.
Scam #2
Pudong (PVG) Airport Black Taxi & Meter Rigging
⚠️ High
📍 Pudong International Airport (PVG) arrivals hall, official taxi queue, Longyang Road Maglev terminus, Hongqiao Airport (SHA) secondary

Unlicensed taxi touts intercept arriving passengers at Pudong Airport before the official queue, quoting ¥400–¥600 for rides the metered fare covers for ¥180–¥220, with a secondary scam cluster at Longyang Road for Maglev passengers attempting to continue by taxi.

Before you reach the official taxi queue at Pudong arrivals, men in casual dress step forward — "Taxi? Shanghai city? Good price?" The legitimate metered fare from PVG to central Shanghai runs ¥180–¥220 for the 45-kilometer, 50-minute trip. Touts quote ¥400–¥600 and project authority by matching your destination confidently.

If you follow them, you ride an unlicensed car with no meter and no recourse if the driver demands more at the destination. A secondary cluster operates at Longyang Road station, where passengers arriving via the Maglev pick up taxis — that location is reportedly denser with scammers than the airport itself. A 2025 report documented meter-tampering even in a licensed Shanghai cab booked at the street.

The safest option requires no negotiation at all. Take Metro Line 2 directly from Pudong Airport to People's Square for ¥7 in 90 minutes, or install DiDi before flying and order at the official rideshare pickup zone with a fixed digital fare. If you use a licensed taxi from the airport queue, confirm "da biao" (打表 — run the meter) before you move and photograph the taxi license from the rear windscreen.

Red Flags

  • Driver in casual dress approaches before you reach the official taxi queue
  • 'Fixed price' of ¥400+ quoted to central Shanghai (legitimate meter is ¥180–¥220)
  • Meter tampered to run faster than 3.2 yuan per km base rate
  • Luggage 'fee' added at destination before driver opens the boot
  • Longyang Road taxi rank after Maglev arrival — higher scam density than PVG airport itself

How to Avoid

  • Take Shanghai Metro Line 2 from PVG station: ¥7, 90 min, scam-proof.
  • Install DiDi before arrival; English interface, regulated fares, digital receipt.
  • If taking licensed taxi, say 'da biao' (打表) before boarding; screenshot DiDi fare as reference.
  • Photograph the taxi license number from the rear windscreen on entering.
  • If you take the Maglev, transfer to Metro Line 2/7 at Longyang Road, not a taxi.
Scam #3
Bund & People's Square Wine / Champagne Bar Overcharge
⚠️ High
📍 Bars near the Bund, Nanjing Road East/West side streets, People's Square north side, Xintiandi tourist-facing venues

A woman posing as a local leads tourists from the Bund to a private bar where champagne arrives uninvited, producing bills of ¥6,000–¥20,000, with bouncers blocking the door until payment is made.

On the Bund waterfront, a friendly, English-speaking woman strikes up conversation and mentions she knows "a perfect sunset bar with a Bund view — very local, not for tourists." The bar's ambiance is genuine, the view is real, and the first few minutes feel like a lucky insider tip rather than a setup.

She orders "just one drink" and a bottle of champagne or wine arrives without your input. When the bill lands — ¥6,000–¥20,000 — she disappears, and bouncers block the door. The bar has its own posted menu, but the prices she has arranged are several times higher. Paying by cash or Alipay leaves no recourse; credit-card chargebacks succeed only when the charged amount exceeds the venue's own posted menu.

The setup depends entirely on following a stranger's venue recommendation. Do not enter any bar or restaurant on the Bund with a person you just met — walk away with a firm "bù yào, xièxiè" and choose your own venue from a pre-researched list. M on the Bund, Bar Rouge, and the Waldorf Astoria's Long Bar all have published English menus and card payment with digital receipts.

Red Flags

  • Stranger on the Bund or Nanjing Road invites you to a 'perfect sunset view' or 'local' bar
  • Bar is on an upper floor, side street, or unmarked entrance
  • No posted prices at entrance or on the menu; 'ordering' happens casually
  • Drink prices ¥500–¥2,000 per glass with no menu shown before serving
  • Staff become hostile or block exit when bill arrives

How to Avoid

  • Choose Bund-view bars yourself: M on the Bund, Bar Rouge, Hakkasan, Waldorf Long Bar, Peace Hotel Jazz Bar (all posted pricing, booking required).
  • Walk past any stranger offering a 'local bar' invitation on the Bund.
  • Always verify a bar has posted English menu and card payment BEFORE ordering.
  • Pay with credit card for chargeback leverage; cash and Alipay are much harder to dispute.
  • If trapped, photograph the bill and inflated prices; dispute via card and report to 12315.

Scam #4
Shanghai Tea Ceremony Ambush at Yuyuan & The Bund
🔶 Medium
📍 Yu Garden (Yuyuan) bazaar area, the Bund promenade, People's Square south side, pedestrian shopping zones

"English student" pairs near Yu Garden approach tourists for language practice, steer them to a side-street tea ceremony, and present bills of ¥800–¥2,500 — the same mechanism as Nanjing Road, calibrated to the softer atmosphere of a sightseeing district.

In and around Yu Garden's bazaar — which draws ten million visitors a year — pairs of well-spoken young people approach tourists in fluent English. The opener is softer than Nanjing Road: "We are students, can we practice English with you?" or "Excuse me, could you help us with a photo?" Conversation flows naturally before the tea invitation appears within about ten minutes.

The "nearby traditional tea ceremony" leads to a side-street venue with a bill of ¥800–¥2,500 — lower than the full Nanjing Road version but ten to twenty times legitimate pricing. Local police have historically declined to help, framing the victim as having attended willingly. The practical remedy in 2025 is a credit-card chargeback or a call to the 12315 consumer-protection English line, not a police report.

The approach is gentler than Nanjing Road but the outcome is identical. Walk past anyone near Yu Garden who approaches in fluent English offering tea, a cultural experience, or photo help — and if you want authentic tea, go directly to Huxinting Teahouse in the middle of the Yu Garden pond (¥150–¥300 posted menu, no solicitation, open since 1855). Yu Garden's entry ticket is ¥40 at the gate, with licensed vendors inside the bazaar.

Red Flags

  • Strangers approach near Yu Garden, the Bund, or People's Square asking you to 'practice English' or 'take a photo'
  • Within 10 minutes, invitation to 'nearby tea ceremony' or 'traditional culture' appears
  • Tea house is in a side street or upper floor, not within Yu Garden's main walkway
  • Menu shown too briefly to read before ordering
  • Bill at ¥800+ per person for what should cost ¥150–¥300 at legitimate venues

How to Avoid

  • Walk past any stranger at Yu Garden, Bund, or People's Square offering 'English practice' or 'photo help.'
  • For genuine tea at Yu Garden, visit Huxinting Teahouse (the pavilion in the middle of the pond, 1855).
  • Published tea prices at Huxinting: ¥150–¥300 per person with clear menu.
  • Never follow a stranger to a 'nearby' tea house, even if they seem friendly.
  • Pay with credit card for chargeback leverage; report via 12315 and your card issuer.
Scam #5
Shanghai Disneyland 'Fast Pass' & Fake Ticket Scams
🔶 Medium
📍 Shanghai Disneyland main entrance (Pudong), Disney Resort Metro station, third-party resale sites, hotel-concierge packages

Touts near Disney Resort Metro station sell "Premier Access" passes or full park entry tickets at 1.5–2× official rates, with the worst cases being fake QR-code tickets refused at the gate with no recourse.

Near Disney Resort Metro station and on resale platforms, sellers offer "Disney Premier Access" or "Priority Pass" tickets framed as official fast-track passes at ¥800 per person. The genuine Disney Premier Access pass costs ¥300–¥600 depending on demand and is sold only through the Shanghai Disneyland app or entrance kiosks. Multiple ticket tiers — one-day, two-day, Premier Access, VIP tours — create room for resellers to exploit the confusion over what each tier includes and what it should cost.

In better cases, you pay 1.5–2× the legitimate rate for a pass that actually works. In the worst cases, the QR code you receive looks authentic but is refused at the gate, and the seller is untraceable. The loss is both the ticket cost and the day's plans, with no avenue for a refund.

There is exactly one safe channel for Shanghai Disneyland tickets. Book exclusively through the Shanghai Disneyland official app or shanghaidisneyresort.com — never from hotel-lobby touts, street vendors near the Metro station, or third-party resale platforms. The app handles all ticket types, Premier Access, and rider reservations. Metro Line 11 runs directly to Disney Resort station for ¥5–¥7 from central Shanghai in 40 minutes.

Red Flags

  • Third-party reseller offers Disney Premier Access at ¥700+ when official rate is ¥300–¥600
  • QR-code ticket purchased from a street tout or hotel lobby booth
  • 'Bundled' Shanghai-highlights tour including Disney at ¥1,500+ per person
  • Seller insists on cash or Alipay payment; refuses credit card for chargeback leverage
  • Tickets delivered via WeChat or email in a format you cannot verify before arrival

How to Avoid

  • Book directly via Shanghai Disneyland official app or shanghaidisneyresort.com.
  • Disney Premier Access is ¥300–¥600; anything higher is a markup or scam.
  • Shanghai Metro Line 11 to Disney Resort station: ¥5–¥7, 40 min, scam-proof.
  • Pay with credit card for fraud protection; avoid cash/Alipay for chargeback reasons.
  • For hotel stays, book only Disney Resort Hotel or Toy Story Hotel via Disney's own site.
Scam #6
AP Xinyang Fake Market Bait-and-Switch
🔶 Medium
📍 AP Xinyang Fashion Market (Science & Technology Museum metro), Han City Fashion & Accessories Plaza, various 'underground fake' markets

Vendors at AP Xinyang and Han City replica markets display high-grade sample goods during negotiation, then swap a lower-grade item into your bag at payment — with rigged diamond testers used to justify premiums on fake watches and jewelry.

AP Xinyang at Science & Technology Museum Metro station and Han City in Jing'an openly sell replica watches, bags, and clothing in a grey-legal tourist market. Vendors display clean, high-grade samples for your inspection, quote ¥500, and negotiate down to ¥200 in a way that feels like a genuine win.

When you agree on a price, the vendor takes the item to "wrap it in back." The package you walk away with contains a lower-grade piece than the sample you negotiated over. A variation involves a handheld "diamond tester" that purports to prove the stones in a bargain Rolex are real — the device is rigged to produce a positive reading regardless. Customs in the US, UK, and EU all prohibit commercial counterfeits; confiscation at the border on your way home is a genuine secondary risk.

Every bait-and-switch pivots on the moment the item leaves your hands before payment. Examine the specific item you will take home before paying, hold onto it yourself through the transaction, and refuse any vendor who offers to "wrap it for you in back" — that is the exact moment the switch happens. Start negotiations at 10–15% of the first quote, and consider skipping entirely in favor of genuine retail at Xintiandi or IFC Mall.

Red Flags

  • Vendor offers to 'wrap in the back' or 'check for you' — this is the moment of the switch
  • Multiple 'grades' of the same product with opaque pricing
  • 'Diamond tester' or authenticity demo produced on request to prove a fake is 'real'
  • First-quoted price is 5–10x what you'd realistically pay
  • Vendor pressures you to pay in cash or Alipay without inspecting the item

How to Avoid

  • Start negotiations at 10–15% of first-quoted price; walk away if vendor refuses.
  • Hold the specific item yourself from inspection through payment; refuse 'wrapping in back.'
  • Understand home-country customs prohibit commercial counterfeits; personal-use amounts vary but risks are real.
  • Consider alternatives: Global Harbor, Xintiandi, IFC Mall for genuine shopping with returns.
  • If shopping anyway, budget only what you can afford to lose entirely.
Scam #7
Pudong Airport SIM Card Tout Markup
🟢 Low
📍 Pudong Airport arrivals hall, along airport transit paths, hotel check-in desks offering 'tourist SIM' packages

Unofficial SIM-card touts in Pudong arrivals charge ¥300 for plans that cost ¥100–¥150 at official China Mobile and China Unicom counters, sometimes selling cards that stop working after seven days with no recourse.

In Pudong's arrivals hall, booths and men in casual dress offer "tourist SIM card, 30GB, 30 days" at ¥300. The pitch is confident and the location — directly in the arrivals flow — makes it feel official. The actual China Mobile or China Unicom plan costs ¥100–¥150 for the same data allowance, from carrier counters a few meters away in the same hall.

Third-party touts mark up 2–3× and sometimes sell SIMs that stop working after seven days, leaving you without data mid-trip and no recourse since the seller has no fixed address. Airport SIM touts operate in the same ecosystem as airport taxi touts — intercepting arrivals before they know the local alternatives exist.

Official carrier counters are in the same arrivals hall and take only a few minutes longer to find. Use only clearly labeled China Mobile or China Unicom counters — or, better, buy a China eSIM from Airalo or Holafly before you fly for US$15–$30 with no counter visit required. For longer stays an official in-country carrier plan remains best value; the tout-booth markup is never worth the risk.

Red Flags

  • Booth or person in arrivals hall not clearly branded China Mobile or China Unicom
  • Price over ¥150 for a 30-day + 30GB tourist SIM
  • Seller insists on cash payment and cannot provide a fapiao receipt
  • SIM 'for tourists' that requires you to provide passport scan to a third-party booth
  • Package promises 'unlimited' data — China carrier plans are always capped at 30GB or similar

How to Avoid

  • Buy an eSIM before flying: Airalo, Holafly, or Nomad sells China 5GB 30-day at US$15–US$30.
  • At PVG, use ONLY the official China Unicom or China Mobile counter — ¥100–¥150 for 30 days/30GB.
  • Ignore any SIM booth not clearly carrier-branded.
  • For short trips (<10 days), home-carrier roaming is often competitive.
  • Keep a receipt (fapiao) from whichever carrier you choose for any warranty or support needs.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Chinese Police (公安局) station. Call 110 (Police) or 120 (Ambulance). Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at mps.gov.cn.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The US Embassy in Beijing is at No. 55 An Jia Lou Road, Chaoyang District, Beijing 100600. For emergencies: +86 10-8531-3000.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
