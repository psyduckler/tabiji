The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Nanjing Road Tea House / Bar Scam
⚠️ High
📍 Nanjing Road East and West pedestrian shopping area, side streets off People's Square, Tianjin Road, Bund approach

Friendly strangers near Nanjing Road, People's Square, and the Bund invite you to a "traditional tea ceremony" and lead you to a side-street venue where the bill runs ¥3,000–¥10,000 per person, the door locks when it arrives, and the new friend who brought you disappears.

Nanjing Road's pedestrian shopping strip is one of the most heavily touristed streets in Asia, and the tea-house scam running there has been documented continuously since the late 1990s. The actors — typically young men or women in their 20s — approach foreigners in confident, fluent English with a warm opener: "We're visiting from Xi'an, first time in Shanghai? Have you tried a traditional tea ceremony?" They seem genuine, the conversation feels natural, and within ten minutes they're suggesting a tea house "just nearby."

The venue is always on a side street, typically an upper floor with no visible signage from the road. The interior is atmospheric — genuine wooden furniture, porcelain teaware, a practiced tea host. The ceremony proceeds with no mention of cost. When it's over, a bill arrives: ¥3,000–¥10,000. The "friends" who brought you have stepped out. Staff position themselves near the exit. Credit cards are accepted but cash is preferred, and the pressure to pay quickly is sustained.

Walk past anyone approaching you in English near Nanjing Road, People's Square, or the Bund with an invitation to tea, a cultural experience, or anything "just nearby" — the rule is absolute, with no exceptions for how friendly or credible the person seems. A venue flagged in 2025 reports: 510 Tianjin Road (operating as SMOOTH dining bar) has multiple documented cases. If you are already inside and the bill has arrived, pay with a credit card — chargebacks against inflated posted-menu prices are often successful — then report to 12315 (China's consumer-protection line, available in English). For genuine Shanghai tea culture, the Huxinting Teahouse at Yu Garden has operated since 1855 with a posted menu and no solicitation.

Red Flags

  • Strangers approach you in fluent English near Nanjing Road, People's Square, or the Bund
  • Invitation to 'traditional tea ceremony' or 'cultural experience' within minutes of meeting
  • Tea house is in a side street or upper floor you did not choose yourself
  • 510 Tianjin Road (SMOOTH dining bar) and similar named venues flagged on traveler reports
  • Door locks or staff block exit when the bill arrives

How to Avoid

  • Walk past any stranger approaching near Nanjing Road with 'bu yao xie xie.'
  • Never follow a stranger to a 'nearby' tea house — the venue is always the scam.
  • Avoid 510 Tianjin Road (SMOOTH dining bar) and any similar side-street 'tea' venue.
  • For genuine tea, go to Shanghai Propaganda Poster Art Centre cafe or hotel-concierge-recommended venues.
  • If trapped, photograph the bill, pay with credit card (for chargeback leverage), then report to 12315 and your card company.
Scam #2
Pudong (PVG) Airport Black Taxi & Meter Rigging
⚠️ High
📍 Pudong International Airport (PVG) arrivals hall, official taxi queue, Longyang Road Maglev terminus, Hongqiao Airport (SHA) secondary

Unlicensed "black taxi" drivers in Pudong Airport arrivals halls quote ¥400–¥600 for the central-Shanghai run that costs ¥180–¥220 by metered licensed cab — and even some licensed cabs have documented 2025 meter-tampering cases on the same route.

Pudong International Airport (PVG) sits 45 km from central Shanghai — a 50-to-60-minute ride that costs ¥180–¥220 by metered licensed taxi. The gap between that official rate and what an unlicensed driver can charge makes the arrivals hall one of the most concentrated scam zones in China. Men in casual dress position themselves before the official taxi queue, making contact with travelers as they exit customs: "Taxi? Shanghai city? Fixed price, 400 yuan."

The figure sounds authoritative because it's presented as a "fixed" rate — implying the meter would run higher. In reality it's two to three times the legitimate metered fare. Licensed drivers at the official taxi queue can also meter-rig: a 2025 case captured on video showed a cab where the meter advanced at roughly double the legal rate. A secondary hotspot is the Longyang Road taxi rank at the Maglev terminus — travelers who take the fast train and then try to grab a cab at Longyang Road face higher scam density than at the airport itself.

The scam-proof option is Shanghai Metro Line 2, which runs directly from PVG to People's Square and Nanjing Road for ¥7 in about 90 minutes. If you prefer a car, install DiDi before your flight, order at the official rideshare pickup zone, and you'll pay ¥120–¥150 with a GPS-verified route and digital receipt. If taking a licensed taxi, say "da biao" (打表) before getting in, screenshot a DiDi fare estimate as a benchmark, and photograph the taxi license number on the rear windscreen. If you take the Maglev to Longyang Road, connect to Metro Line 2 or 7 rather than a taxi — the rail transfer adds only ten minutes.

Red Flags

  • Driver in casual dress approaches before you reach the official taxi queue
  • 'Fixed price' of ¥400+ quoted to central Shanghai (legitimate meter is ¥180–¥220)
  • Meter tampered to run faster than 3.2 yuan per km base rate
  • Luggage 'fee' added at destination before driver opens the boot
  • Longyang Road taxi rank after Maglev arrival — higher scam density than PVG airport itself

How to Avoid

  • Take Shanghai Metro Line 2 from PVG station: ¥7, 90 min, scam-proof.
  • Install DiDi before arrival; English interface, regulated fares, digital receipt.
  • If taking licensed taxi, say 'da biao' (打表) before boarding; screenshot DiDi fare as reference.
  • Photograph the taxi license number from the rear windscreen on entering.
  • If you take the Maglev, transfer to Metro Line 2/7 at Longyang Road, not a taxi.
Scam #3
Bund & People's Square Wine / Champagne Bar Overcharge
⚠️ High
📍 Bars near the Bund, Nanjing Road East/West side streets, People's Square north side, Xintiandi tourist-facing venues

An English-speaking Chinese woman on the Bund steers you to an unmarked upper-floor bar with a genuine view, a bottle of champagne arrives uninvited, and the bill runs ¥6,000–¥20,000 — she vanishes when it does, and bouncers block the door.

The Bund promenade draws thousands of tourists each evening, and this con works by exploiting the genuine desire for a riverside sunset drink with a local. The actor — typically an attractive, well-dressed woman in her 20s or 30s — approaches with confident English: "I know a beautiful bar with Huangpu River view, very local, not tourist. You should come." The Bund is crowded and publicly lit; the invitation seems low-risk.

The bar is on an upper floor, accessible by a narrow stairwell or elevator, with no posted prices at the entrance. The ambiance is genuine — the view is real, the decor is upscale, service is attentive. The woman orders "just one drink to celebrate." A bottle of champagne arrives, uncorked and poured before you've seen a menu. When the experience ends, the bill arrives: ¥6,000–¥20,000. The woman says she needs to make a call and doesn't return. Staff positioned near the exit make leaving without paying visibly confrontational.

Never follow a stranger's bar recommendation on the Bund — walk away from any approach with a polite "bu yao, xie xie" and choose your own venue. The legitimate Bund sunset experience is available at published prices: M on the Bund, Bar Rouge, the Waldorf Astoria Long Bar, and the Peace Hotel Jazz Bar all post English menus and accept reservations — book directly before arriving. If you end up trapped and the bill has arrived, pay with a credit card rather than cash or Alipay; chargebacks against inflated posted-menu prices are often successful, and a card transaction creates a documented record for a 12315 consumer complaint.

Red Flags

  • Stranger on the Bund or Nanjing Road invites you to a 'perfect sunset view' or 'local' bar
  • Bar is on an upper floor, side street, or unmarked entrance
  • No posted prices at entrance or on the menu; 'ordering' happens casually
  • Drink prices ¥500–¥2,000 per glass with no menu shown before serving
  • Staff become hostile or block exit when bill arrives

How to Avoid

  • Choose Bund-view bars yourself: M on the Bund, Bar Rouge, Hakkasan, Waldorf Long Bar, Peace Hotel Jazz Bar (all posted pricing, booking required).
  • Walk past any stranger offering a 'local bar' invitation on the Bund.
  • Always verify a bar has posted English menu and card payment BEFORE ordering.
  • Pay with credit card for chargeback leverage; cash and Alipay are much harder to dispute.
  • If trapped, photograph the bill and inflated prices; dispute via card and report to 12315.

Scam #4
Shanghai Tea Ceremony Ambush at Yuyuan & The Bund
🔶 Medium
📍 Yu Garden (Yuyuan) bazaar area, the Bund promenade, People's Square south side, pedestrian shopping zones

Around Yu Garden's bazaar, strangers ask to "practice English" or "take a photo," then invite you to a "traditional tea ceremony" nearby — bills run ¥800–¥2,500 per person at side-street venues, and police historically side with the operator when confronted.

Yu Garden draws 10 million visitors a year, making its bazaar area a primary hunting ground for the milder cousin of the Nanjing Road tea-house scam. The approach is softer — actors are younger, often claiming to be university students: "Hello, can we practice English with you?" or "Could you take a photo for us?" The initial contact is brief and friendly, with no mention of tea. Conversation flows for five to ten minutes before the invitation appears: "We know a nearby traditional tea ceremony, very authentic."

The venue is always a side street or upper floor outside Yu Garden's main walkway, with no posted prices visible. The tea ceremony is conducted with practiced formality — multiple varieties poured in small cups, each variety introduced. When it ends, the bill arrives: ¥800–¥2,500 per person for what a legitimate teahouse charges ¥150–¥300. If you protest, staff note that you ordered and consumed the tea voluntarily. Police called to the scene historically take the venue's side, citing voluntary attendance.

Walk past anyone at Yu Garden, the Bund, or People's Square who approaches in fluent English with "English practice," "photo help," or any variation — the tea-ceremony invitation always follows within ten minutes, regardless of how the initial contact is framed. Yu Garden's legitimate teahouse is the Huxinting Teahouse — a pavilion in the middle of the Garden's pond, operating since 1855 with a clearly visible menu at ¥150–¥300 per person and no solicitation. If you are already inside a suspicious venue with a bill, pay with a credit card and file a 12315 complaint and card chargeback simultaneously.

Red Flags

  • Strangers approach near Yu Garden, the Bund, or People's Square asking you to 'practice English' or 'take a photo'
  • Within 10 minutes, invitation to 'nearby tea ceremony' or 'traditional culture' appears
  • Tea house is in a side street or upper floor, not within Yu Garden's main walkway
  • Menu shown too briefly to read before ordering
  • Bill at ¥800+ per person for what should cost ¥150–¥300 at legitimate venues

How to Avoid

  • Walk past any stranger at Yu Garden, Bund, or People's Square offering 'English practice' or 'photo help.'
  • For genuine tea at Yu Garden, visit Huxinting Teahouse (the pavilion in the middle of the pond, 1855).
  • Published tea prices at Huxinting: ¥150–¥300 per person with clear menu.
  • Never follow a stranger to a 'nearby' tea house, even if they seem friendly.
  • Pay with credit card for chargeback leverage; report via 12315 and your card issuer.
Scam #5
Shanghai Disneyland 'Fast Pass' & Fake Ticket Scams
🔶 Medium
📍 Shanghai Disneyland main entrance (Pudong), Disney Resort Metro station, third-party resale sites, hotel-concierge packages

Street touts and hotel-lobby resellers sell "Disney Premier Access" passes at ¥700–¥900 when the official rate is ¥300–¥600, and some sell outright fake QR-code tickets that are refused at the gate with no recourse since the seller is untraceable.

Shanghai Disneyland's ticket structure has enough tiers — one-day, two-day, Premier Access, VIP tour, seasonal pricing — to create genuine confusion, and resellers exploit that confusion systematically. You'll encounter them at hotel lobbies, near the Disney Resort Metro station entrance, and on third-party booking platforms. The pitch sounds credible: "I have Disney Premier Access, normally ¥800, today ¥700" — a price that can fall within the legitimate demand-period range, making the deal feel real.

There are two failure modes. In the first, the ticket is genuine but overpriced — Premier Access is sold only through the official app or entrance kiosks at ¥300–¥600 depending on demand, and the reseller marks it up 1.5–2x. In the second, the QR code looks legitimate but is invalid at the gate — a complete fake. Victims of the second case have no recourse: the seller was a street tout with no fixed address, payment was in cash, and gate staff cannot help with a third-party purchase.

Book directly through the Shanghai Disneyland official app or shanghaidisneyresort.com — those are the only authorized sources for all ticket types including Premier Access. Anything purchased from a hotel tout, street vendor, WeChat contact, or third-party platform carries real risk of being overpriced or invalid. For transport, Shanghai Metro Line 11 runs directly to Disney Resort station from central Shanghai for ¥5–¥7 in 40 minutes, bypassing all street-level hawking entirely.

Red Flags

  • Third-party reseller offers Disney Premier Access at ¥700+ when official rate is ¥300–¥600
  • QR-code ticket purchased from a street tout or hotel lobby booth
  • 'Bundled' Shanghai-highlights tour including Disney at ¥1,500+ per person
  • Seller insists on cash or Alipay payment; refuses credit card for chargeback leverage
  • Tickets delivered via WeChat or email in a format you cannot verify before arrival

How to Avoid

  • Book directly via Shanghai Disneyland official app or shanghaidisneyresort.com.
  • Disney Premier Access is ¥300–¥600; anything higher is a markup or scam.
  • Shanghai Metro Line 11 to Disney Resort station: ¥5–¥7, 40 min, scam-proof.
  • Pay with credit card for fraud protection; avoid cash/Alipay for chargeback reasons.
  • For hotel stays, book only Disney Resort Hotel or Toy Story Hotel via Disney's own site.
Scam #6
AP Xinyang Fake Market Bait-and-Switch
🔶 Medium
📍 AP Xinyang Fashion Market (Science & Technology Museum metro), Han City Fashion & Accessories Plaza, various 'underground fake' markets

Vendors at AP Xinyang (Science & Technology Museum Metro) and Han City show you a high-grade replica watch or bag, agree a negotiated price, then swap in a lower-grade item at the wrap — and customs in most home countries can confiscate any counterfeit goods you bring back.

AP Xinyang Fashion Market at Science & Technology Museum station and Han City in Jing'an are Shanghai's best-known replica-goods markets, selling branded watches, bags, clothing, and electronics openly in a legal-grey zone. The experience is familiar to experienced bargain shoppers, but the scam-within-the-scam catches tourists who think they've already protected themselves by negotiating hard. A vendor shows you a convincing "Grade A" replica watch, quotes ¥500, you negotiate to ¥200, shake hands, and consider yourself a savvy buyer.

The switch happens at the handoff. The vendor says "let me get it boxed for you" and takes the item behind the stall or into a back room. What comes back in the box is a lower-grade version — same color, similar weight, but visibly inferior materials on close inspection. A variant uses props: the vendor produces a "diamond tester" or "grade certification" to prove the item is "real" before the switch. Once you've paid and left the stall, return claims are refused and receipts are typically not issued.

Inspect the specific item you plan to take home, seal it in your own bag, and pay without letting it leave your hands — the moment a vendor offers to "wrap it in back," the switch is about to happen; decline and insist on wrapping yourself or walk away. Beyond the market scam, understand that bringing counterfeit branded goods home is illegal in most countries: US, UK, and EU customs routinely confiscate commercial quantities on arrival. If you want a Shanghai shopping experience without replica-market risks, Global Harbor, Xintiandi, and IFC Mall offer genuine goods with posted prices and return policies.

Red Flags

  • Vendor offers to 'wrap in the back' or 'check for you' — this is the moment of the switch
  • Multiple 'grades' of the same product with opaque pricing
  • 'Diamond tester' or authenticity demo produced on request to prove a fake is 'real'
  • First-quoted price is 5–10x what you'd realistically pay
  • Vendor pressures you to pay in cash or Alipay without inspecting the item

How to Avoid

  • Start negotiations at 10–15% of first-quoted price; walk away if vendor refuses.
  • Hold the specific item yourself from inspection through payment; refuse 'wrapping in back.'
  • Understand home-country customs prohibit commercial counterfeits; personal-use amounts vary but risks are real.
  • Consider alternatives: Global Harbor, Xintiandi, IFC Mall for genuine shopping with returns.
  • If shopping anyway, budget only what you can afford to lose entirely.
Scam #7
Pudong Airport SIM Card Tout Markup
🟢 Low
📍 Pudong Airport arrivals hall, along airport transit paths, hotel check-in desks offering 'tourist SIM' packages

Unofficial booths and touts in Pudong Airport arrivals charge ¥250–¥300 for a "tourist SIM card" that China Mobile and China Unicom's own airport counters sell for ¥100–¥150 — and some third-party SIMs stop working within seven days.

Arriving at Pudong Airport with no working Chinese SIM is a genuine problem — WeChat Pay, DiDi, and navigation all require data. The touts and unofficial booths in the arrivals hall exploit that dependency immediately: a man in casual dress approaches before you reach the baggage carousel, or a professional-looking booth offers a "tourist SIM, 30GB, 30 days, ¥300." The price sounds reasonable to someone who hasn't researched local rates, and the urgency is real — you need connectivity now. SIM-card touts operate alongside taxi touts as part of the same airport-arrivals scam ecosystem targeting travelers who don't yet know the legitimate alternatives.

The markup is two to three times the legitimate rate. China Unicom has a dedicated counter in PVG Terminal 2 arrivals selling its "Trip SIM" at ¥100–¥150 for 30 days and 30GB; China Mobile's counter offers similar plans at comparable prices. A second failure mode: some third-party SIM cards stop authenticating after seven days when the seller's prepaid arrangement with the carrier expires, leaving you without data mid-trip with no recourse.

Buy an eSIM before you board your flight to China — Airalo, Holafly, and Nomad all sell China-compatible eSIMs for US$15–US$30, they activate remotely before you land, and there is no counter interaction required at the airport. If you prefer a physical SIM, use only the clearly branded China Mobile or China Unicom counter inside PVG arrivals — not any booth without that branding. For trips under ten days, your home carrier's international roaming may be competitive and avoids the airport interaction entirely.

Red Flags

  • Booth or person in arrivals hall not clearly branded China Mobile or China Unicom
  • Price over ¥150 for a 30-day + 30GB tourist SIM
  • Seller insists on cash payment and cannot provide a fapiao receipt
  • SIM 'for tourists' that requires you to provide passport scan to a third-party booth
  • Package promises 'unlimited' data — China carrier plans are always capped at 30GB or similar

How to Avoid

  • Buy an eSIM before flying: Airalo, Holafly, or Nomad sells China 5GB 30-day at US$15–US$30.
  • At PVG, use ONLY the official China Unicom or China Mobile counter — ¥100–¥150 for 30 days/30GB.
  • Ignore any SIM booth not clearly carrier-branded.
  • For short trips (<10 days), home-carrier roaming is often competitive.
  • Keep a receipt (fapiao) from whichever carrier you choose for any warranty or support needs.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Chinese Police (公安局) station. Call 110 (Police) or 120 (Ambulance). Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at mps.gov.cn.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The US Embassy in Beijing is at No. 55 An Jia Lou Road, Chaoyang District, Beijing 100600. For emergencies: +86 10-8531-3000.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
