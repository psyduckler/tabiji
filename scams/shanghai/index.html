The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Nanjing Road Tea House / Bar Scam
⚠️ High
📍 Nanjing Road East and West pedestrian shopping area, side streets off People's Square, Tianjin Road, Bund approach

Friendly "tourists" visiting from other Chinese cities approach foreign travelers near Nanjing Road and invite them to a traditional tea ceremony, then present bills of ¥3,000–¥10,000 and lock the door until payment is made.

You are walking along Nanjing Road's pedestrian shopping strip when two well-dressed young Chinese travelers approach in fluent English: "We're visiting from Xi'an — is this your first time in Shanghai? There's a traditional tea ceremony nearby, would you like to join us?" The invitation sounds cultural and spontaneous.

You follow them to a small tea house on a side street, one block off the main strip. The tea is served and conversation continues pleasantly. When you finish, a bill arrives for ¥3,000–¥10,000. The door is now locked. Staff members position themselves between you and the exit until the amount is paid in full. The "tourists" vanish — they are paid touts working on commission.

Any stranger approaching you near Nanjing Road, People's Square, or the Bund with an invitation to "tea," a "cultural experience," or a "traditional ceremony" is running this scam — walk past without conversation. For genuine Shanghai tea culture, visit the Huxinting Teahouse at Yu Garden (menus posted, ¥150–¥300 per person) or book a hotel-concierge-recommended venue with published pricing.

Red Flags

  • Strangers approach you in fluent English near Nanjing Road, People's Square, or the Bund
  • Invitation to 'traditional tea ceremony' or 'cultural experience' within minutes of meeting
  • Tea house is in a side street or upper floor you did not choose yourself
  • 510 Tianjin Road (SMOOTH dining bar) and similar named venues flagged on traveler reports
  • Door locks or staff block exit when the bill arrives

How to Avoid

  • Walk past any stranger approaching near Nanjing Road with 'bu yao xie xie.'
  • Never follow a stranger to a 'nearby' tea house — the venue is always the scam.
  • Avoid 510 Tianjin Road (SMOOTH dining bar) and any similar side-street 'tea' venue.
  • For genuine tea, go to Shanghai Propaganda Poster Art Centre cafe or hotel-concierge-recommended venues.
  • If trapped, photograph the bill, pay with credit card (for chargeback leverage), then report to 12315 and your card company.
Scam #2
Pudong (PVG) Airport Black Taxi & Meter Rigging
⚠️ High
📍 Pudong International Airport (PVG) arrivals hall, official taxi queue, Longyang Road Maglev terminus, Hongqiao Airport (SHA) secondary

Unlicensed taxi touts accost arrivals outside Pudong Airport customs halls, quoting ¥400–¥600 for a trip that legitimately costs ¥180–¥220 — while some licensed cabs use tampered meters that inflate fares mid-route.

You clear customs at Pudong Airport and head toward the exit with your luggage. Before you reach the official taxi queue, men in casual clothing approach: "Taxi? Shanghai city? ¥400, very cheap." The legitimate metered fare from PVG to central Shanghai runs ¥180–¥220 for a 45-kilometre, 50-minute ride.

If you accept an unlicensed tout, you pay double or triple the legal rate with no receipt and no recourse. Even licensed cabs carry risk: a second fraud pattern operates at Longyang Road station — the Maglev terminus — where touts are more aggressive, and some licensed drivers use tampered meters that inflate the fare mid-journey.

Use DiDi from the official rideshare pickup zone or take Metro Line 2 from Pudong Airport directly to People's Square for ¥7 — both options bypass touts entirely. If you take a licensed taxi, say "da biao" (打表) to confirm the meter runs, screenshot a DiDi fare estimate as a price reference, and photograph the taxi license from the rear windscreen before moving.

Red Flags

  • Driver in casual dress approaches before you reach the official taxi queue
  • 'Fixed price' of ¥400+ quoted to central Shanghai (legitimate meter is ¥180–¥220)
  • Meter tampered to run faster than 3.2 yuan per km base rate
  • Luggage 'fee' added at destination before driver opens the boot
  • Longyang Road taxi rank after Maglev arrival — higher scam density than PVG airport itself

How to Avoid

  • Take Shanghai Metro Line 2 from PVG station: ¥7, 90 min, scam-proof.
  • Install DiDi before arrival; English interface, regulated fares, digital receipt.
  • If taking licensed taxi, say 'da biao' (打表) before boarding; screenshot DiDi fare as reference.
  • Photograph the taxi license number from the rear windscreen on entering.
  • If you take the Maglev, transfer to Metro Line 2/7 at Longyang Road, not a taxi.
Scam #3
Bund & People's Square Wine / Champagne Bar Overcharge
⚠️ High
📍 Bars near the Bund, Nanjing Road East/West side streets, People's Square north side, Xintiandi tourist-facing venues

A friendly English-speaking local on the Bund leads tourists to an upscale bar with genuine skyline views, then orders champagne without discussion — delivering a bill of ¥6,000–¥20,000 while staff block the exit until payment is made.

You are walking along the Bund at sunset when a friendly English-speaking Chinese woman strikes up a conversation. She is articulate and well-dressed, and mentions she knows a "perfect bar with a genuine Bund view — very local, not touristy." The ambiance, she says, is unlike the tourist traps.

She leads you to an upper-floor bar with a real skyline view. Before you have ordered, a bottle of champagne appears. The bill arrives at ¥6,000–¥20,000. Your companion disappears when payment is requested. Bouncers block the door until you settle the amount.

Never follow a stranger's bar recommendation on the Bund — choose your venue in advance from places with published English menus and online bookings. Bar Rouge, the Waldorf Astoria Long Bar, and the Peace Hotel Jazz Bar all post prices publicly. If presented with an undiscussed bill, pay by credit card rather than cash or Alipay to preserve chargeback rights, and photograph the menu and bill before paying.

Red Flags

  • Stranger on the Bund or Nanjing Road invites you to a 'perfect sunset view' or 'local' bar
  • Bar is on an upper floor, side street, or unmarked entrance
  • No posted prices at entrance or on the menu; 'ordering' happens casually
  • Drink prices ¥500–¥2,000 per glass with no menu shown before serving
  • Staff become hostile or block exit when bill arrives

How to Avoid

  • Choose Bund-view bars yourself: M on the Bund, Bar Rouge, Hakkasan, Waldorf Long Bar, Peace Hotel Jazz Bar (all posted pricing, booking required).
  • Walk past any stranger offering a 'local bar' invitation on the Bund.
  • Always verify a bar has posted English menu and card payment BEFORE ordering.
  • Pay with credit card for chargeback leverage; cash and Alipay are much harder to dispute.
  • If trapped, photograph the bill and inflated prices; dispute via card and report to 12315.

Scam #4
Shanghai Tea Ceremony Ambush at Yuyuan & The Bund
🔶 Medium
📍 Yu Garden (Yuyuan) bazaar area, the Bund promenade, People's Square south side, pedestrian shopping zones

"Art students" or "English-practice" partners approach tourists at Yu Garden and the Bund, pivot quickly to a nearby tea ceremony invitation, and deliver bills of ¥800–¥2,500 — roughly ten to twenty times legitimate tea-house pricing.

You are exploring the bazaar around Yu Garden when a young person approaches in fluent English: "We're students — can we practice our English with you?" or "Could you help us with a photo?" The conversation is relaxed and friendly; Yu Garden attracts ten million tourists a year, and the interaction feels like a natural cultural exchange.

Within ten minutes, a tea ceremony invitation appears: "There is a traditional tea house nearby — would you like to try it?" Inside, tea is served. The bill arrives at ¥800–¥2,500 — lower than the full Nanjing Road version but still ten to twenty times the price at a legitimate venue. Staff are unhelpful if you object; local police have historically told victims they attended willingly.

Walk past anyone approaching near Yu Garden in fluent English offering tea, a cultural experience, or photo assistance — the approach itself is the tell. The Huxinting Teahouse in the middle of Yu Garden pond has served tea since 1855 with a clearly posted menu at ¥150–¥300 per person and no solicitation — go there directly rather than following a stranger.

Red Flags

  • Strangers approach near Yu Garden, the Bund, or People's Square asking you to 'practice English' or 'take a photo'
  • Within 10 minutes, invitation to 'nearby tea ceremony' or 'traditional culture' appears
  • Tea house is in a side street or upper floor, not within Yu Garden's main walkway
  • Menu shown too briefly to read before ordering
  • Bill at ¥800+ per person for what should cost ¥150–¥300 at legitimate venues

How to Avoid

  • Walk past any stranger at Yu Garden, Bund, or People's Square offering 'English practice' or 'photo help.'
  • For genuine tea at Yu Garden, visit Huxinting Teahouse (the pavilion in the middle of the pond, 1855).
  • Published tea prices at Huxinting: ¥150–¥300 per person with clear menu.
  • Never follow a stranger to a 'nearby' tea house, even if they seem friendly.
  • Pay with credit card for chargeback leverage; report via 12315 and your card issuer.
Scam #5
Shanghai Disneyland 'Fast Pass' & Fake Ticket Scams
🔶 Medium
📍 Shanghai Disneyland main entrance (Pudong), Disney Resort Metro station, third-party resale sites, hotel-concierge packages

Touts near the Disney Resort Metro station sell overpriced "priority pass" resales at 1.5–2× the official rate or outright counterfeit QR-code tickets refused at the gate — leaving buyers with no recourse and no entry.

You are planning a Shanghai Disneyland visit and a "Disney Premier Access" reseller quotes ¥800 per person for fast-track entry. The genuine Disney Premier Access pass sells only through the official app or entrance kiosks at ¥300–¥600 depending on demand. The multiple ticket tiers — one-day, two-day, Premier Access, VIP tour — create confusion that resellers exploit.

Third-party sellers charge 1.5–2× the legitimate rate for passes that may or may not scan at the gate. Outright fake tickets — QR codes that look valid but are not registered in Disney's system — are sold by touts near the Disney Resort Metro station. Victims are refused entry at the gate with no means of recovery because the reseller is untraceable.

Buy all Shanghai Disneyland tickets exclusively through the official app or shanghaidisneyresort.com — no legitimate third party sells Disney tickets at the gate or near the Metro station. For transport, Shanghai Metro Line 11 runs directly to Disney Resort station for ¥5–¥7 from central Shanghai in about 40 minutes, with no exposure to touts.

Red Flags

  • Third-party reseller offers Disney Premier Access at ¥700+ when official rate is ¥300–¥600
  • QR-code ticket purchased from a street tout or hotel lobby booth
  • 'Bundled' Shanghai-highlights tour including Disney at ¥1,500+ per person
  • Seller insists on cash or Alipay payment; refuses credit card for chargeback leverage
  • Tickets delivered via WeChat or email in a format you cannot verify before arrival

How to Avoid

  • Book directly via Shanghai Disneyland official app or shanghaidisneyresort.com.
  • Disney Premier Access is ¥300–¥600; anything higher is a markup or scam.
  • Shanghai Metro Line 11 to Disney Resort station: ¥5–¥7, 40 min, scam-proof.
  • Pay with credit card for fraud protection; avoid cash/Alipay for chargeback reasons.
  • For hotel stays, book only Disney Resort Hotel or Toy Story Hotel via Disney's own site.
Scam #6
AP Xinyang Fake Market Bait-and-Switch
🔶 Medium
📍 AP Xinyang Fashion Market (Science & Technology Museum metro), Han City Fashion & Accessories Plaza, various 'underground fake' markets

Vendors at AP Xinyang and Han City replica markets show a quality item during negotiation, then swap it for a lower-grade substitute during payment or wrapping — and some use rigged gemstone testers to upsell worthless stones as genuine.

You visit AP Xinyang near Science & Technology Museum station or Han City in Jing'an to browse replica goods — a common tourist experience in Shanghai. A vendor presents a well-made "grade A" replica watch and quotes ¥500. You negotiate to ¥200, which feels like a win.

When the vendor takes the item to "wrap it for you in back," the switch happens: the watch you examined is replaced with a lower-grade version before it reaches your hands. In a second variant, the vendor produces a "diamond tester" that appears to prove the gemstone in your watch is genuine — the tester is rigged or the reading is misrepresented. Bringing counterfeit goods home is also a separate risk: US, UK, and EU customs can confiscate replicas on arrival.

Examine the specific item you will carry home before paying, hold it yourself throughout the transaction, and refuse any offer to "wrap it in back." Start negotiations at 10–15% of the asking price and treat any vendor who insists on boxing or wrapping out of your sight as running a switch. Shanghai also has legitimate shopping at Global Harbor, Xintiandi, and IFC Mall with honest pricing and returns.

Red Flags

  • Vendor offers to 'wrap in the back' or 'check for you' — this is the moment of the switch
  • Multiple 'grades' of the same product with opaque pricing
  • 'Diamond tester' or authenticity demo produced on request to prove a fake is 'real'
  • First-quoted price is 5–10x what you'd realistically pay
  • Vendor pressures you to pay in cash or Alipay without inspecting the item

How to Avoid

  • Start negotiations at 10–15% of first-quoted price; walk away if vendor refuses.
  • Hold the specific item yourself from inspection through payment; refuse 'wrapping in back.'
  • Understand home-country customs prohibit commercial counterfeits; personal-use amounts vary but risks are real.
  • Consider alternatives: Global Harbor, Xintiandi, IFC Mall for genuine shopping with returns.
  • If shopping anyway, budget only what you can afford to lose entirely.
Scam #7
Pudong Airport SIM Card Tout Markup
🟢 Low
📍 Pudong Airport arrivals hall, along airport transit paths, hotel check-in desks offering 'tourist SIM' packages

Touts and unofficial booths in Pudong arrivals charge ¥300 for tourist SIM cards that official China Mobile and China Unicom counters sell for ¥100–¥150 — and some third-party cards stop working within a week.

You land at Pudong and need a local SIM card before heading into Shanghai. A booth in the arrivals hall — or a casually dressed man near the exit — offers a "tourist SIM card, 30 GB, 30 days, ¥300." The price sounds reasonable when you are disoriented from a long flight and do not know the local rate.

The official China Unicom and China Mobile counters in PVG Terminal 2 sell equivalent plans for ¥100–¥150. Third-party tout SIMs are marked up two to three times, and some are configured to stop working after seven days rather than thirty. Airport SIM touts operate in the same ecosystem as black-taxi touts — both target arrivals who have not yet checked local pricing.

Buy your SIM at the clearly labeled China Unicom or China Mobile counter in arrivals — or purchase a China eSIM through Airalo or Holafly before you board your flight. Airalo's China 5 GB 30-day plan costs around US$17.50 and activates before you land. Ignore any booth or individual not carrying official carrier branding.

Red Flags

  • Booth or person in arrivals hall not clearly branded China Mobile or China Unicom
  • Price over ¥150 for a 30-day + 30GB tourist SIM
  • Seller insists on cash payment and cannot provide a fapiao receipt
  • SIM 'for tourists' that requires you to provide passport scan to a third-party booth
  • Package promises 'unlimited' data — China carrier plans are always capped at 30GB or similar

How to Avoid

  • Buy an eSIM before flying: Airalo, Holafly, or Nomad sells China 5GB 30-day at US$15–US$30.
  • At PVG, use ONLY the official China Unicom or China Mobile counter — ¥100–¥150 for 30 days/30GB.
  • Ignore any SIM booth not clearly carrier-branded.
  • For short trips (<10 days), home-carrier roaming is often competitive.
  • Keep a receipt (fapiao) from whichever carrier you choose for any warranty or support needs.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Chinese Police (公安局) station. Call 110 (Police) or 120 (Ambulance). Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at mps.gov.cn.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The US Embassy in Beijing is at No. 55 An Jia Lou Road, Chaoyang District, Beijing 100600. For emergencies: +86 10-8531-3000.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
