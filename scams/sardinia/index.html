Scam #1
Rental Car Pre-Existing Damage Scam
⚠️ High
📍 Car rental counters at Cagliari-Elmas Airport, Olbia Costa Smeralda Airport, and local rental offices in Alghero and Porto Cervo

Sardinia rental-car return agents at Cagliari Elmas and Olbia Costa Smeralda 'find' undocumented damage to the underside of the bumper at drop-off, demand €300–€500 cash repair fees, and threaten to charge the card on file before you can leave — one-way Cagliari-to-Olbia rentals are particularly risky since a different agent handles return, and the Rick Steves Travel Forum, TripAdvisor Cagliari Forum, and Mel365's Sardinia car-rental guide all flag this as the most common Sardinia scam.

Sardinia's two main airports — Cagliari Elmas (CAG) in the south and Olbia Costa Smeralda (OLB) in the north — funnel almost every visitor through one of the major rental-car desks (Avis, Hertz, Sicily by Car, Europcar, Locauto). The rental-car damage-claim ecosystem flagged across the Rick Steves Travel Forum, TripAdvisor's Cagliari Forum (with a named 'Warning, don't use! – Avis car rental Cagliari' thread), and Mel365.com's comprehensive Sardinia car-rental guide is the most-documented Sardinia scam, with one-way rentals between the two airports particularly exposed because a different agent handles the return inspection.

The mechanic is consistent across operators. Stage one is the pickup: the agent rushes through inspection, the rental form uses vague terms without a detailed damage diagram, and the underside of the bumper, the wheel rims, the roof, and the lower edge panels are typically not checked. Stage two is the drive: normal Sardinia roads (especially the Costa Smeralda coastal routes and the inland mountain SS131 sections) put scratches on bumper undersides and wheel rims that are present on essentially every rental in the fleet. Stage three is the return: the agent inspects the previously-unchecked areas, claims damage of €300–€500, and threatens to charge the card on file unless cash is paid immediately. As one Rick Steves Travel Forum poster put it, 'it would be unrealistic for a car in Sardinia not to have scratches underneath in normal driving' — the damage is essentially guaranteed at pickup, and the scam is the timing of when it gets discovered. A parallel Rick Steves thread ('BEWARE! Car rental scam in Italy') describes being charged for damage that was documented on the rental agreement but the agency claimed was new — the same mechanic with paperwork-tampering on top.

For older travellers renting cars in Sardinia, the defense is timestamped video evidence at pickup plus a standalone excess insurance policy. Take detailed photos and video of every panel, bumper, wheel, roof, undercarriage edge, and interior with timestamps before driving off — insist that every scratch, dent, and mark is documented on the rental agreement with the agent's signature before taking the keys, book through reputable international companies (Avis, Hertz, Europcar, Sixt, Budget) or comparison sites with documented dispute processes, purchase a standalone excess insurance policy through RentalCover.com or similar to protect against false damage claims, and at return inspect the car with the agent and get written or emailed confirmation that the vehicle is returned in acceptable condition. Avoid one-way rentals (Cagliari to Olbia or vice versa) where possible — same-airport pickup and return is significantly safer because the same operations team handles both ends. If the return agent claims damage that wasn't documented at pickup, refuse to pay cash, demand an itemised written estimate, decline to sign anything, and dispute the card charge with your home bank citing the photo evidence — Section 75 (UK) or chargeback (US/Canada) work cleanly when the photo timestamps predate the rental agreement.

Red Flags

  • The pickup agent rushes through the inspection and discourages you from documenting the vehicle thoroughly
  • The rental form uses vague terms and does not have a detailed diagram for marking existing damage
  • At return, the agent examines areas that were not checked at pickup, such as the underside of bumpers
  • One-way rentals (Cagliari to Olbia or vice versa) are particularly risky because a different agent handles the return
  • The agent refuses to let you leave or threatens to charge your card before you can dispute the claim

How to Avoid

  • Take detailed photos and video of every panel, bumper, wheel, roof, undercarriage edge, and interior before driving off — include timestamps.
  • Insist that every scratch, dent, and mark is documented on the rental agreement with the agent's signature before taking the keys.
  • Book through reputable international companies or comparison sites with customer dispute processes.
  • Purchase a standalone excess insurance policy through a provider like RentalCover.com that protects you against false damage claims.
  • At return, inspect the car with the agent and get written or emailed confirmation that the vehicle is returned in acceptable condition.
Scam #2
Beach Chair and Umbrella Extortion
🔶 Medium
📍 Popular beaches including La Pelosa (Stintino), Cala Luna, Spiaggia del Principe (Costa Smeralda), and beaches along the Villasimius coast

Sardinia's beach concessions claim entire stretches of sand at La Pelosa, Cala Luna, Spiaggia del Principe, and the Villasimius coast, charging €30–€50 per day for sunbed-and-umbrella sets (up to €80 at Costa Smeralda) and intimidating travellers who try to lay a towel in a 'gap' — La Pelosa additionally bans towels entirely and requires a €3.50 booking fee plus mandatory bamboo mat or face a €100 fine, while The Local Italy ranks Sardinia beach pricing among 'Italy's worst tourist scams.'

Sardinia has some of the Mediterranean's most beautiful beaches — La Pelosa at Stintino, Cala Luna on the Gulf of Orosei, Spiaggia del Principe on Costa Smeralda, the Villasimius coast — and they're also the most heavily-monetised beaches in Italy. Italian beach law requires concessions to leave a portion of every beach open for free public access ('spiaggia libera'), but enforcement is patchy and many concessions claim the entire visible stretch. Euronews has reported that Sardinia's popular beaches are 'protected with towel bans, pre-booked tickets, and entry fees,' La Pelosa specifically requires a €3.50 booking fee, a 1,500-visitor cap, and a mandatory bamboo mat (no direct towel contact with the sand), and France24 has documented over €350,000 in fines for illegal beach occupation — even as Dolcevia.com notes that many beach clubs operate on expired concessions raising questions about the legality of their charges.

The trap menu has three mechanics. The headline concession overcharge: a beach is almost entirely occupied by rows of sunbeds and umbrellas managed by a concession operator, staff tell you that you must rent a set for €30–€50 per day or €80 at Costa Smeralda premium beaches, and prices are not posted (you only learn the cost after sitting down). The aggressive-clearance variant: when travellers try to set up their own towel in a 'gap' between sunbeds, staff become aggressive and intimidating, insisting the entire beach is under their management even on stretches that legally require free public access. The La Pelosa-specific trap: towels on the sand are banned entirely under Stintino municipal rules, you must use a rigid bamboo mat or face a €100 fine, and arriving without pre-booking the €3.50 entry plus the mat means turning around. Some Italian Riviera locations charge up to €380 per day for a sunbed at the high end of the markup pattern.

For older travellers planning Sardinia beach days, the defense is to research the specific beach rules before visiting and look for 'spiaggia libera' signs. Know Italian law (concessions cannot occupy the entire beach and free public access must be maintained), research specific beach rules before visiting (La Pelosa requires pre-booking at €3.50 plus a rigid bamboo mat — no towels), look for 'spiaggia libera' signs marking sections where you can set up your own towel for free, and visit beaches early in the morning before concessions set up or choose less popular beaches off the main tourist trail to avoid the markup entirely; if a concession illegally blocks public access, report it to the Guardia Costiera coast guard at 1530. Refuse undocumented charges that aren't posted before you sit down — Italian consumer law requires price disclosure. Avoid premium-named Costa Smeralda beaches at peak summer if you don't want the €80 per day sunbed trap; the smaller coves a few kilometres away frequently have free public access at the same beach quality. La Pelosa is genuinely worth the €3.50 booking fee plus bamboo mat for the water and the views, but plan ahead — same-day arrivals frequently get turned away when the 1,500-visitor cap fills.

Red Flags

  • The entire beach appears covered in rented sunbeds with no visible free public area
  • Staff tell you the whole beach is managed and you cannot lay a towel anywhere without paying
  • Prices are not posted and you are only told the cost after you have sat down
  • At La Pelosa or similar protected beaches, officials demand fines for using a regular beach towel on the sand
  • The beach concession claims exclusive rights to the entire stretch of sand

How to Avoid

  • Know Italian law: concessions cannot occupy more than a portion of the beach, and public access must be maintained.
  • Research specific beach rules before visiting — La Pelosa requires pre-booking, a €3.50 fee, and a rigid mat (no direct towel contact).
  • Look for 'spiaggia libera' (free beach) signs indicating sections where you can set up your own towel for free.
  • Visit beaches early in the morning before the concessions set up, or choose less popular beaches off the tourist trail.
  • If you feel a concession is illegally blocking public access, report it to the local coast guard (Guardia Costiera) at 1530.
Scam #3
Restaurant Hidden Charges and Coperto Surprise
🟢 Low
📍 Tourist-oriented restaurants in Cagliari's Marina district, Porto Cervo, Alghero's old town, and beachfront restaurants throughout the coast

Sardinia tourist-strip restaurants in Alghero, Costa Smeralda, and the Cagliari old town pad bills with €2.50–€5 per person 'coperto' cover charges (often hidden in fine print), €3–€4 'unrequested bread and olives' that arrive at the table, and 15% 'servizio' service charges added without advance notice — Italian coperto is legal but the scam is the inflated tourist-zone version, with Costa Smeralda restaurants sometimes doubling the expected bill via add-ons.

Coperto (the cover charge) is a legitimate Italian restaurant tradition that pays for table linens, glassware, and bread service, with the average rate in Sardinia at €2.50 per person according to local food blogs and the Away to Italy restaurant guide. The scam element isn't the coperto itself — it's the inflation of the charge at tourist-zone restaurants in Alghero, on the Costa Smeralda strip, and around Cagliari's old town, where the coperto creeps to €5 per person, the bread basket and olives that arrive 'compliments of the house' get billed separately, and a 15% servizio (service charge) lands on top without prior notice. The Rick Steves Travel Forum has documented this through multiple 'Service charges added onto bills in Italy' threads, and at premium Costa Smeralda restaurants the surprise charges can double a €30 expected meal to €60.

The trap menu has four mechanics. The unrequested-item charge: bread, olives, antipasti, or bottled water appear at the table without being ordered, with no indication they aren't complimentary, and arrive on the bill at €3–€4 per item. The hidden coperto: the cover charge is shown in tiny print at the bottom of the menu or not listed at all, then appears on the bill at €2.50–€5 per person. The undisclosed servizio: a 10–15% service charge is added to the bill without advance notice on the menu. The Costa Smeralda multiplier: at premium Costa Smeralda restaurants — Porto Cervo, Porto Rotondo, Baja Sardinia — the same mechanics run at €5+ coperto, €6 bread baskets, €8 olive dishes, and 18% servizio, doubling the expected meal cost. The base trick is consistent: items that look like courtesy turn out to be billed, and the menu fine print is where the disclosure lives.

For older travellers eating in Sardinia, the defense is to refuse unordered items at the table and to confirm coperto-plus-servizio in writing before sitting. Refuse bread, olives, antipasti, and bottled water that arrive at the table without being ordered (they're charged for unless you actively decline), confirm the coperto amount per person and the servizio policy in writing on the menu before sitting (Italian law requires disclosure but many tourist-zone menus bury it), photograph the menu so you can dispute charges that don't match, and refuse 'servizio 15%' on bills where the menu didn't disclose it — Italian consumer law backs your refusal. Eat at locals' restaurants two streets off the tourist strip rather than the sea-view Alghero waterfront or the Costa Smeralda named-resort strip — the same regional Sardinian cuisine (fregola, culurgiones, porceddu, seadas) costs half the price with honest coperto. If a charge appears that you can't reconcile against the menu, ask for a written breakdown — the manager is required to provide one under Italian consumer law, and refusal to provide it is grounds for a complaint to the local Carabinieri or to the Comune's commercial-fraud office. Coperto at €2.50 per person is fair; coperto at €5 plus charges for unrequested bread is the trap.

Red Flags

  • Bread, olives, antipasti, or water appear at your table without being ordered — they will all be charged for
  • The menu shows a coperto charge in tiny print or it is not listed at all — but appears on the bill
  • A service charge (servizio) of 10-15% is added to the bill without advance notice
  • The restaurant is in a prime tourist location with sea views and prices to match — but the add-ons are not transparent
  • When you question the charges, the staff claim 'this is how it is done in Italy' without providing a satisfactory explanation

How to Avoid

  • Understand that coperto (€1-3 per person) and bread charges are standard in Italian restaurants — budget for them.
  • Ask the waiter specifically which items on the table are included and which are extra before touching them.
  • Check the menu for coperto and servizio charges — they must be listed to be legally charged.
  • If bread or antipasti arrive uninvited and you do not want to be charged, politely send them back immediately.
  • Eat at restaurants frequented by locals rather than tourist waterfront establishments for more transparent pricing.

Scam #4
Fuel Policy Rip-Off at Rental Agencies
🔶 Medium
📍 Car rental counters at Cagliari-Elmas Airport, Olbia Costa Smeralda Airport, and city-center rental offices

Sardinia rental-car agencies push 'full-to-empty' fuel policies where you prepay for a full tank at inflated €2.20/litre against local-station €1.80, then return with leftover fuel that the agency keeps — Mel365's Sardinia car-rental guide explicitly flags the 'full to empty' model as a Sardinia scam, recommending 'full to full' or 'same level' policies instead.

Sardinia rental-car agencies offer two fuel policies and the difference between them is the line between fair and exploitative. 'Full-to-full' (or 'same-level') is the legitimate option — you pick up the car with a full tank, refuel to the same level before return at a local Sardinia station, and pay only for fuel you actually consumed at €1.80 per litre. 'Full-to-empty' is the trap option — you prepay for a full tank at the agency's inflated rate of €2.20 per litre, return the car with whatever fuel remains, and the agency keeps both the money and the leftover fuel. Mel365.com's comprehensive Sardinia car-rental guide explicitly warns about the full-to-empty model as a Sardinia scam, and TripAdvisor's Italy forum and the Rick Steves community both have multiple long-running complaint threads about agencies pushing this policy as 'more convenient' without disclosing the cost gap.

The mechanic relies on two facts. First, the prepaid fuel rate is structurally inflated — €2.20 per litre at the agency vs €1.80 at a local Esso, ENI, or IP station, a 22% premium per litre that travellers don't notice when comparing the headline rental price. Second, it's nearly impossible to time a return with the tank exactly at empty: most travellers return with a quarter tank remaining (15–20% of capacity, or roughly €15–€25 of fuel that the agency keeps as pure profit). The agency frames the policy as 'convenient' and 'stress-free' precisely because it captures the convenience premium plus the fuel margin plus the leftover-fuel keep, all in one transaction. Older travellers are particularly exposed because the 'no need to refuel before return' framing reads as accessibility-friendly, especially after a one-way drive that ends at an airport with limited fuel options nearby.

For older travellers renting cars in Sardinia, the defense is to insist on a 'full-to-full' policy at booking and refuel at a local station before return. Insist on a 'full-to-full' or 'same-level' fuel policy at booking — refuse 'full-to-empty' prepay regardless of how convenient the agent makes it sound — refuel at a local Sardinia station (Esso, ENI, IP) at €1.80 per litre before returning the car, and keep the fuel receipt as evidence of return-level compliance; if the agent at booking only offers full-to-empty, walk to a different rental desk at the same airport since most international operators (Avis, Hertz, Europcar, Sixt) honour the full-to-full policy on request even when the default is full-to-empty. The fuel-station price gap (€1.80 vs €2.20) compounds across the trip: a 50-litre tank costs €90 at a local station vs €110 at the agency rate, and that's before the 'leftover fuel kept' overcharge of €15–€25. Plan your return route to pass a fuel station 5–10 minutes before the airport — Cagliari Elmas has Esso and ENI on the SS554 approach, Olbia has multiple stations on the SS125 ring road. Return with a tank-fill receipt photographed and the timestamp visible.

Red Flags

  • The agent offers a 'full to empty' fuel policy described as 'convenient' and 'stress-free'
  • The prepaid fuel rate is significantly higher than current gas station prices in Sardinia
  • The agent discourages you from choosing the 'full to full' option by claiming gas stations near the airport are hard to find
  • There is no refund for unused fuel if you return the car with fuel remaining in the tank
  • The fuel policy is buried in the contract terms and not clearly explained at the counter

How to Avoid

  • Always choose the 'full to full' (pieno a pieno) fuel policy — pick up with a full tank and return it full.
  • Fill up at a gas station near the airport before returning the car — there are always stations within a few kilometers of Sardinian airports.
  • Decline the prepaid fuel option regardless of how convenient the agent makes it sound.
  • Compare the agency's prepaid fuel rate to current local prices — the markup is typically 20-30%.
  • Take a photo of the fuel gauge at pickup and return to have evidence of the fuel level in case of disputes.
Scam #5
ZTL Traffic Fine Trap
🔶 Medium
📍 Historic centers of Cagliari, Alghero, Sassari, and Olbia — any Italian city with a Zona a Traffico Limitato

Sardinia historic towns (Alghero, Cagliari old town, Bosa, Castelsardo) have ZTL camera-enforced restricted-traffic zones with small Italian-only signs that tourists routinely miss — driving through for even 30 seconds to reach a hotel triggers a €100 fine that arrives 3–12 months later, plus a €30–€75 'administrative fee' from the rental company for forwarding your details, totalling €130–€175 per violation.

ZTL (Zona a Traffico Limitato) zones are restricted-traffic areas in Italian historic centres enforced by automated camera systems that photograph every entering vehicle's licence plate, cross-reference against a permitted-vehicle database, and issue fines for unauthorised entries. Italian traffic authorities have up to one year to formally notify the registered driver, and they routinely use the full window — fines arrive 3 to 12 months after the violation, often after the rental return and home-country travel are long over. Sardinia's main historic towns — Alghero, Cagliari old town, Bosa, Castelsardo — all have ZTLs at the most common hotel-access entrances, and the signs are small, frequently Italian-only, and easy to miss when a GPS routes a car directly into the zone. The Rick Steves Travel Forum has an extensive 'TRAFFIC VIOLATION SCAMS IN ITALY' thread and multiple 'Nightmare Italy traffic infractions with rental car' threads documenting the pattern.

The mechanic isn't a scam in the traditional sense — ZTL fines are legitimate and the Italian consulate in Toronto confirms enforcement is real — but the trap layer is the rental-company administrative fee that lands on top. The fine itself is €100, payable to the Italian municipality. The rental company adds a €30–€75 'administrative fee' for forwarding your details to the authorities, automatically charged to the card on file 3–12 months after the rental ended. A single 30-second drive into an Alghero ZTL to find a hotel turns into a €130–€175 line item that arrives months after you've forgotten the trip. Many travellers receive multiple ZTL fines from the same rental — every camera entry counts as a separate violation, so a hotel in the middle of an Alghero ZTL with multiple approach roads can generate two or three fines in one stay. GPS apps (Google Maps, Apple Maps) frequently route through ZTLs without warning, treating them as normal streets. Older travellers are particularly exposed because the long delay between violation and fine notification makes the cause hard to identify and the dispute harder to mount.

For older travellers driving in Sardinia, the defense is to never enter a historic centre with a rental car and to confirm hotel parking before booking. Never drive into a Sardinia historic centre (Alghero, Cagliari old town, Bosa, Castelsardo) with a rental car — park outside the ZTL and walk to your hotel — and watch for small electronic 'ZTL' or 'Zona Traffico Limitato' signs at the entrances to all old towns since these are camera-enforced; before booking a hotel inside an old town, confirm whether a ZTL permit is included or whether the hotel can register your plate with the local police for the duration of your stay (most central Sardinia hotels offer this for free but it must be requested in advance), and refuse rental-company 'administrative fees' charged months after the rental by disputing them as unauthorised charges if the fine itself is being separately billed by the Italian municipality. If your hotel is inside a ZTL, request the parking-permit registration in writing 24 hours before arrival — the hotel registers your plate with the local police database so the cameras don't issue a fine. GPS apps don't reliably warn about ZTLs, so cross-reference your route against a tourism-office map before driving into any historic Sardinian town. If a ZTL fine arrives months after the trip, you can pay the municipal fine directly online (which avoids future enforcement), but always dispute the rental-company administrative fee as an unauthorised post-return charge.

Red Flags

  • Small electronic signs with 'ZTL' or 'Zona Traffico Limitato' at entrances to old town centers — these are camera-enforced
  • Your GPS or Google Maps directs you through a historic center without warning about restricted zones
  • You drove into an old town to reach your hotel and only realized afterward that it was a restricted area
  • Your rental company charges an 'administrative fee' to your card months after returning the vehicle
  • A letter arrives from Italy with a traffic fine for entering a restricted zone on a date you were there

How to Avoid

  • Research ZTL zones in every Sardinian town you plan to visit before driving there — all historic centers have them.
  • Park outside the ZTL zone and walk into the old town — most hotels provide directions for parking outside the restricted area.
  • If your hotel is inside the ZTL, contact them before arrival to request a temporary access permit.
  • Use Google Maps or Waze which now include ZTL warnings for many Italian cities.
  • If a fine arrives months later, verify its authenticity with the issuing municipality before paying — but note that ignoring legitimate fines can result in collection action.
Scam #6
Street Sob Story Cash Request
🟢 Low
📍 Train stations in Cagliari and Sassari, tourist areas in Alghero's old town, and parking areas near popular beaches

A well-dressed Sardinia 'sob story' scammer at Cagliari and Sassari train stations and Alghero's old town approaches tourists with elaborate stories (broken car, lost wallet, bureaucratic problem) requesting an oddly-specific small amount like €2.60 for a train ticket — the small ask is calibrated to feel too trivial to refuse, but the same person collects €50–€100 daily from dozens of tourists running the same script.

Sardinia's main train stations (Cagliari and Sassari), Alghero's old town, and parking areas near popular beaches host a low-grade but persistent sob-story scam where a well-dressed person approaches tourists with an elaborate story and requests an oddly-specific small amount of money. The Rick Steves Travel Forum has a named 'Sardinia — attempted scam' thread describing the exact mechanic at Cagliari station, with 'a man who told an elaborate story about bureaucratic problems and was short 2 euros and 60 cents for a train ticket to Cagliari.' The pattern is consistent across Italy, but the specific delivery in Sardinia is calibrated to the tourist demographic and the language gap.

The mechanic relies on three elements that work together. First, the storyteller is well-dressed — clean shirt, jacket, sometimes a small briefcase or laptop bag — which removes the visible-poverty cue that would let travellers default to 'no.' Second, the story is elaborate and specific: a car that broke down on a specific stretch of the SS131, a lost wallet stolen at a specific Cagliari café, a bureaucratic problem at a specific government office, with timeline details that make the situation feel resolvable rather than fictional. Third, the cash ask is oddly specific and small — €2.60 for a train ticket, €4.80 for petrol, €3.50 for a coffee — calibrated to feel too trivial to refuse and too odd to be invented (real emergencies, the implicit logic goes, would have round numbers). The same person tells the same story to dozens of tourists each day at the same train station or piazza, collecting €50–€100 daily in small amounts. Variants include 'I need to call my embassy,' 'My daughter is at the hospital,' and 'I forgot my house keys at my mother's,' all with the same odd-specific cash ask. Tourists who decline are often followed by an emotional escalation ('please, just two euros') before the storyteller moves to the next target.

For older travellers walking Sardinia's train stations, town centres, and beach parking areas, the defense is a polite firm 'no' without engaging in the story. Refuse every odd-specific small-cash request from a well-dressed stranger with an elaborate sob story (broken car, lost wallet, bureaucratic problem, short €2.60 for a train ticket) — say 'mi dispiace, no' or 'sorry, no' once firmly without engaging with the details, keep walking, and don't open your wallet to 'see if you have change' since that's the secondary opening; never give cash to a stranger at Cagliari or Sassari train station, in Alghero's old town, or at beach parking areas regardless of how compelling the story sounds. If you genuinely want to help someone in distress, point them toward the Carabinieri (112), the train station information desk, or the local Comune's social services rather than handing over cash. Real bureaucratic emergencies have institutional pathways; tourist-facing 'help me' approaches with oddly-specific small cash asks don't. The same technique runs across Italy and across Mediterranean cities, so the same defense applies — polite firm no, no wallet opening, keep walking. The €2.60 isn't the problem; the problem is the dozens of repetitions per day across the same station, which is what makes it organised fraud rather than individual misfortune.

Red Flags

  • A stranger approaches with an elaborate story about a broken car, lost wallet, stolen purse, or bureaucratic problem
  • They need a very specific, small amount of money — usually €2-5 — that seems too minor to refuse
  • The person is well-dressed and does not appear to be in genuine distress
  • They become emotional or persistent if you initially decline, adjusting their story to seem more sympathetic
  • You see the same person approaching other tourists with the same story later in the day

How to Avoid

  • Politely decline and keep walking — a firm 'No, mi dispiace' (No, I am sorry) is sufficient.
  • If someone genuinely needs help, offer to call them a taxi or take them to the train station rather than giving cash.
  • Do not feel guilty for declining — this is a professional operation, not a person in genuine need.
  • Avoid engaging in conversation, as the stories become more elaborate and harder to walk away from once you stop.
  • Direct genuinely distressed people to the local police station or carabinieri for assistance.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Carabinieri / Polizia di Stato station. Call 112 (Carabinieri) or 113 (Polizia). Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at poliziadistato.it.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The US Embassy in Rome is at Via Vittorio Veneto 121, 00187 Rome. For emergencies: +39 06-4674-1.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
