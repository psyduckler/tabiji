The 7 Scams

Scam #1
16th Street Mall Distraction Theft
⚠️ High
📍 16th Street Mall, LoDo, Union Station area, and surrounding downtown blocks

Pickpocket crews and opportunistic thieves target tourists along Denver's 16th Street Mall and Union Station area using distraction tactics and coordinated group surges to snatch phones and wallets — a corridor that logged more than 900 crimes in a recent two-year period.

The 16th Street Mall is Denver's central pedestrian spine, stretching a mile through downtown past patio restaurants, shops, and outdoor seating. It draws millions of visitors each year, and that foot traffic makes it the city's highest-density target zone for opportunistic theft. Union Station anchors the northern end and logs more crimes per square mile than any other Denver neighborhood, with phone grabs, purse snatchings, and pickpocketing topping the incident list.

The approach is engineered to look accidental. Someone asks for directions or spills a drink on you while an accomplice clears a phone from the table or lifts a wallet from an open bag. Multiple Westword reports document groups of juveniles swarming tourists on the mall walkway — grabbing phones before dispersing into the crowd in seconds. A TripAdvisor reviewer described the scenario precisely: turning to glance at a TV for ten seconds, then finding a stranger drinking their beer and their phone gone from the table. Even after the mall's 2024 renovation improved conditions overall, the rebuilt sections continue to concentrate tourist foot traffic in ways that opportunistic thieves exploit.

The critical habit is treating table surfaces as unsafe in all outdoor settings here. Keep your phone on your lap or inside a zipped bag at all outdoor dining spots on the 16th Street Mall — nothing placed on a table or left in an open purse stays safe for long. The moment any physical contact occurs — a bump, a spill, a stranger sitting uninvited nearby — check your belongings immediately before addressing the distraction.

Red Flags

  • A stranger sits down uninvited at your outdoor table or stands very close while you eat
  • Someone approaches asking overly personal questions — where you're staying, how long you're in town
  • A sudden commotion or argument breaks out right next to you at a patio or bench
  • Groups of teenagers surround you on the mall walkway asking for money or selling items
  • Someone spills a drink on you or bumps into you repeatedly in a low-traffic area

How to Avoid

  • Never leave your phone, wallet, or bag on the table at outdoor dining spots on the mall — keep everything on your lap or in a zipped bag on your chair.
  • Stay in well-lit, busy sections of the 16th Street Mall and avoid walking alone after 10 PM.
  • Be especially vigilant around Union Station, where crime density is highest in all of Denver.
  • If someone bumps into you or creates a scene, immediately check your pockets and secure your belongings.
  • Use the free MallRide shuttle to move between blocks rather than walking with expensive items visible.
Scam #2
Rental Car Damage Shakedown at Denver Airport
⚠️ High
📍 Denver International Airport (DEN) rental car center, Routes Car Rental, NUCar Rentals, and off-airport lots in Commerce City

Off-airport budget rental car operators near Denver International fabricate post-return damage claims — most commonly hail damage — sending bills of $1,500–$3,000 weeks after a damage-free rental, with no evidence and no warning issued at the return desk.

Rental prices from off-airport budget operators near DEN look compelling at booking — sometimes 40–60% below major-brand rates. The lot operates via shuttle from the terminal and is often understaffed at the return desk. Agents rush through the pre-rental inspection and discourage customers from thoroughly photographing existing damage. The business model is not the daily rate; the margin is the post-return charge.

Weeks after returning the car without incident, a bill arrives for alleged hail damage from the rental period. Denver7 Investigates uncovered hundreds of complaints against Routes Car Rental in Commerce City, documenting surprise charges, refusal to issue refunds, and multi-hour pickup waits. Customers who contested the charges found the company had already applied the amount to the card on file. Some off-airport operators outsource collections to third-party claim agencies with no incentive to confirm the damage was real. A TripAdvisor Denver forum thread documents a visitor billed $1,500 for dents that were present before the rental began.

Documentation before you drive away is your entire leverage. Photograph and video every panel, bumper, roof, and wheel in time-stamped images before leaving the lot, and get a signed return receipt with a staff member present at drop-off. Renting from major brands at the main DEN Rental Car Center rather than off-airport budget lots eliminates this risk entirely — the daily rate difference is typically far less than the potential post-return claim.

Red Flags

  • The rental company operates from a remote off-airport lot with a shuttle, not from the main DEN rental car center
  • The agent rushes you through the pre-rental inspection and discourages you from documenting existing damage
  • Prices on the booking site are dramatically lower than major brands — the profit comes from post-rental charges
  • The company has fewer than 4 stars on Google Reviews with complaints about surprise bills
  • The agent insists you purchase their insurance even after you show proof of your own coverage

How to Avoid

  • Rent from major brands (Hertz, Enterprise, National) at the main DEN Rental Car Center — avoid off-airport budget lots.
  • Photograph and video every panel, bumper, roof, and wheel of the car before leaving the lot — include timestamps.
  • Photograph the car again upon return with a staff member present and get a signed return receipt.
  • Always decline the damage waiver if your personal auto insurance or credit card already covers rental cars — but confirm coverage first.
  • If you receive a post-rental damage claim, dispute it immediately through your credit card company with your photo evidence.
Scam #3
Fake Ski Lift Tickets on Craigslist
⚠️ High
📍 Denver Craigslist, Facebook Marketplace, and Colorado ski resorts including Vail, Steamboat, and Breckenridge

Colorado ski resort lift tickets sold at 30–50% discounts on Craigslist and Facebook Marketplace are typically purchased with stolen credit cards, voided at the resort ticket window on pickup day, and unrecoverable — Vail Police alone documented 39 cases in a single season, nine times the prior year's count.

Planning a ski weekend from Denver, a visitor finds a Craigslist listing for Vail lift tickets at half price. The seller responds quickly, has a convincing profile, and sends a confirmation email after receiving Venmo payment. The deal looks clean. Top Colorado resort day tickets cost $200 or more, so a half-price listing appears to be a genuine score — particularly for a multi-day trip where the apparent savings are substantial.

The tickets are void at the window. Sellers use stolen credit cards to purchase lift tickets online at full price, then resell them at a discount before the fraudulent charge is flagged. Resorts process the credit card only when the buyer arrives to pick up the tickets — at which point the stolen card has been canceled and the ticket is invalidated on the spot. Vail Police documented 39 such cases in a single season. Steamboat Resort issued a formal Facebook warning after multiple incidents. One group of four friends lost $2,000 this way, and because Colorado law makes lift ticket resale illegal in any form, they had no legal avenue for recovery.

There is no legitimate secondary market for Colorado ski lift tickets. Buy lift tickets only from the resort's official website or ticket window — no third-party platform, no Craigslist, no individual seller — and use the Ikon Pass or Epic Pass for multi-resort discounts instead of searching for deals from strangers. Report suspicious listings to the resort's fraud department; Vail and Steamboat both maintain dedicated contacts for this.

Red Flags

  • Lift tickets offered at 30-50% below face value on Craigslist, Facebook Marketplace, or similar platforms
  • The seller insists on payment via Venmo, Zelle, or Cash App — methods with no buyer protection
  • The seller cannot meet in person at the resort and sends tickets electronically or via email confirmation
  • The listing was posted recently but the seller has no history or profile on the platform
  • The seller claims to have multiple tickets for different dates and resorts — a sign they're using stolen cards at scale

How to Avoid

  • Only buy lift tickets directly from the resort's official website or ticket window — never from third parties.
  • Use the Ikon Pass or Epic Pass for multi-resort discounts instead of seeking deals from strangers.
  • Remember that reselling lift tickets is illegal in Colorado — any secondary market sale is inherently risky.
  • If a deal seems too good to be true, it is — Vail day tickets cost $200+ for a reason.
  • Report suspicious Craigslist listings to the resort's fraud department and local police.

Scam #4
Aggressive Panhandling and Sob Story Hustles
🔶 Medium
📍 16th Street Mall, Colfax Avenue, Capitol Hill, Five Points, and RTD light rail stations

Along Denver's 16th Street Mall, Colfax Avenue, and RTD light rail stations, persistent hustlers approach tourists with rehearsed emergency stories — stranded veteran, broken-down car, sick child — then follow or physically block anyone who declines to pay.

Near Civic Center Station, a man in reasonable clothes approaches with a specific story: his car broke down on I-25, he is a veteran, and he needs just $20 for gas to reach his daughter in Colorado Springs. He produces a gas can as a prop and speaks with practiced credibility. The specificity is deliberate — a named destination and a named child are harder to dismiss than a vague request, and the detail creates the impression that the need is real and uniquely urgent.

Twenty minutes later, the same man is telling the same story two blocks away to another tourist. The Denver Gazette and multiple Reddit threads document encounters along Colfax Avenue — historically the city's highest-crime corridor — where individuals follow tourists for blocks, stand in front of restaurant doors blocking entry, or approach cars stopped at red lights and refuse to step back. Aurora police created a specific safety plan for the Colfax and Havana intersection after documenting 92 crimes in a single block in early 2024. The Colorado Sheriff's Office advises against giving cash on the street, noting that many aggressive panhandlers in the downtown corridor use the money for drugs rather than the emergencies they describe.

The exit from these interactions is never through explanation — engagement only extends them. Say 'no' once without breaking stride and keep moving, because stopping to justify your decision signals that the request is still under negotiation. If someone is following you or blocking your path, walk directly into the nearest open business and ask staff to help — they know the routine.

Red Flags

  • The person has a very specific, detailed story involving an exact dollar amount they need
  • They carry props — a gas can, a baby car seat, printed medical bills — to make the story convincing
  • They follow you after you say no or block your path on the sidewalk or at a doorway
  • You see the same person telling the same story at different locations throughout the day
  • They approach your car at a red light and stand at the window, making it feel unsafe to drive away

How to Avoid

  • A firm 'No, sorry' and keep walking — do not stop, do not engage, do not make eye contact if you feel unsafe.
  • Avoid walking on Colfax Avenue east of Broadway, especially at night — this is Denver's highest-crime corridor.
  • Do not pull out your wallet or phone in response to a panhandler's request — you become a target.
  • If someone is blocking a doorway or following you, walk into the nearest open business and ask staff for help.
  • Donate to Denver's established organizations like the Denver Rescue Mission instead of giving cash on the street.
Scam #5
Cannabis Dispensary Tourist Traps
🔶 Medium
📍 Broadway dispensary strip, Denver downtown dispensaries, and unlicensed delivery services

Tourist-targeted dispensaries near Denver's 16th Street Mall mark up products two to three times local rates and add illegal 'tourist processing fees,' while fake online delivery services collect Cash App payments for cannabis that never arrives or arrives as a counterfeit with no THC.

Denver's legal cannabis industry draws millions of curious first-time visitors, and some dispensaries near the tourist core are priced specifically for people who have no local reference point. A visitor walking into a shop near the mall and asking for recommendations gets steered toward premium edibles at $60 per package — the same product available for $25 at a dispensary two miles away on Broadway. The budtender adds a 'tourist processing fee' that has no basis under Colorado state law, and the visitor pays $120 for what a local resident would buy for $45.

The overprice problem is the most common, but not the most harmful. My 420 Tours, once Denver's most prominent cannabis experience operator, shut down without warning or refunds, leaving visitors who had prepaid hundreds of dollars completely stranded. Online delivery services proliferate claiming hotel dropoff of edibles — they collect payment via Cash App or Zelle, then disappear. Some victims received packages of fake edibles with chemical tastes and no detectable THC. Denver's marijuana enforcement division found some dispensaries selling products that would fail the state's mandatory mold-testing standards. The BBB flagged marijuana dispensary scams as a growing category, noting that once payment goes through an untraceable app, recovery is nearly impossible.

Legal recreational cannabis delivery is not permitted in Denver, which means any service offering hotel dropoff is operating illegally or fraudulently. Check prices on Weedmaps or Leafly before entering any dispensary, and visit shops on Broadway between Alameda and I-25 rather than tourist-zone storefronts near the mall — competitive local pricing on that strip reflects what residents actually pay, not what visitors will accept.

Red Flags

  • The dispensary is within two blocks of a major tourist attraction and charges noticeably higher prices than listed on Weedmaps
  • A 'budtender' steers you exclusively toward the most expensive products and adds fees not on the menu board
  • An online service offers cannabis delivery to your hotel via Cash App or Venmo — recreational delivery is not legal in Denver
  • A cannabis tour company requires full prepayment with no refund policy and has few recent reviews
  • Products lack proper state-mandated labeling, testing stickers, or universal THC symbol

How to Avoid

  • Check prices on Weedmaps or Leafly before visiting any dispensary — know the going rate for what you want.
  • Visit dispensaries away from the tourist core — Broadway between Alameda and I-25 has competitive local shops.
  • Never buy cannabis through delivery services or social media — recreational delivery is illegal in Denver.
  • Only buy products with official Colorado MED testing labels and the universal THC symbol on the packaging.
  • Book cannabis tours only through operators with recent, verified reviews on Google or TripAdvisor.
Scam #6
Rideshare Impersonators at Denver Airport and Events
🔶 Medium
📍 Denver International Airport (DEN) arrivals level, Red Rocks Amphitheatre, Ball Arena, and Empower Field at Mile High

Fake rideshare drivers at Denver International Airport and post-concert exits at Red Rocks Amphitheatre approach weary travelers by calling out first names overheard nearby, then demand $100–$150 cash once the destination is reached — sometimes threatening to return riders to the airport if they refuse.

At DEN after a late flight, the rideshare pickup area at Level 5 of the Jeppesen Terminal is busy and signs are confusing for first-time visitors. A man near the arrivals exit calls out a first name — it matches. He already seems to know the destination. He looks like the right Uber. Fake drivers position themselves in the arrivals area, listen for passengers naming destinations or repeating a driver's name into their phone, and approach the moment someone is visibly searching for their ride.

At the hotel, the driver demands $150 in cash — triple the app rate. When the passenger objects, the driver offers to take them back to the airport instead. The Bank of Colorado's consumer safety guide specifically warns about rideshare impersonators at DEN and notes that late-arriving passengers are the most tired and hardest to reach quickly. The same tactic is reported at Red Rocks Amphitheatre after concerts, where thousands of people pour out simultaneously, cell service is poor, and the parking situation makes confirming your actual driver's location on a small screen genuinely difficult.

The verification ritual takes twenty seconds and eliminates the risk completely. Ask the driver 'Who are you here to pick up?' before saying your name or approaching the vehicle — a real driver will tell you; an impersonator will guess. Confirm the name, car make, color, and plate against your app screen before opening the door, and at DEN follow the signed Level 5 rideshare pickup zone rather than accepting any approach outside that area.

Red Flags

  • A driver approaches you and says your name before you've confirmed their identity in the app
  • The car doesn't match the make, model, or color shown in your Uber or Lyft app
  • There's no rideshare placard or decal visible on the windshield or dashboard
  • The driver asks where you're going before you've confirmed the ride — real drivers already have the destination
  • The driver asks for cash payment or says the app isn't working and they'll charge you directly

How to Avoid

  • Always confirm the driver's name, car model, color, and license plate in the app before approaching any vehicle.
  • Ask the driver 'Who are you picking up?' — never say your name first; make them tell you.
  • At DEN, follow signs to the official Level 5 rideshare pickup area and use the designated waiting zone.
  • Never get into a vehicle that asks for cash payment — legitimate rideshare drivers are paid through the app.
  • Share your ride details with a friend or family member using the in-app safety feature.
Scam #7
Vehicle Break-Ins at Trailheads and Tourist Parking
🔶 Medium
📍 Red Rocks Park parking lots, Denver International Airport long-term parking, trailheads along I-70 corridor, and downtown parking garages

Smash-and-grab crews target rental cars at Denver trailheads and DEN airport long-term parking lots — Colorado's auto theft rate is among the highest nationally, and a single operation from 2022 to 2024 resulted in 17 defendants indicted for stealing 190 vehicles from DEN parking alone.

A tourist parks a rental car at an I-70 trailhead lot for a morning hike, tucking a laptop bag under the back seat. The lot feels safe — other cars, mountain views, bright sunshine. But rental company stickers on the bumper and an out-of-state plate make the vehicle easy to identify as belonging to someone non-local, likely with valuables inside, who will be gone for at least an hour.

The rear window is smashed and everything is gone before the hike ends. Smash-and-grab crews work in coordinated teams and can break a window, clear the interior, and drive off in under thirty seconds. The Colorado Auto Theft Authority reports that Denver-area break-ins concentrate at I-70 trailhead lots and at DEN airport long-term parking. A grand jury indicted 17 defendants for stealing at least 190 vehicles from DEN parking between 2022 and 2024, with stolen cars driven across the border. At trailheads, rental cars are the primary target — the combination of obvious tourist identity and a predictable extended absence makes them the highest-yield opportunity in the lot.

The only reliable protection is leaving nothing to take. Remove all valuables from the vehicle before parking at any trailhead or airport lot — take everything on the trail or leave it at the hotel, because covered items still signal 'something worth smashing for.' Remove rental company stickers and airport receipts from the dashboard, and park as close to ranger stations or high-traffic entry points as possible.

Red Flags

  • You're parking at a remote trailhead with no ranger station, security cameras, or other cars nearby
  • Your rental car has company branding stickers, out-of-state plates, or airport rental receipts visible on the dash
  • You're leaving visible bags, electronics, or shopping bags in the car — even covered items signal 'something worth stealing'
  • Broken glass on the ground near other parking spots indicates recent break-ins at that location
  • Someone is sitting in a parked car watching people leave the lot — they may be scouting for targets

How to Avoid

  • Take all valuables with you on the trail or leave them at your hotel — never leave anything visible in the car.
  • Remove rental company stickers and airport receipts from the dashboard and glove box.
  • Park in well-lit, high-traffic areas and close to trailhead buildings or ranger stations when possible.
  • Use DEN's short-term garage close to the terminal rather than remote economy lots if your trip is short.
  • Consider a steering wheel lock for overnight airport parking — visible deterrents reduce break-in risk significantly.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Denver Police Department station. Call 720-913-2000. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at Denver Police Online Reporting.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

U.S. does not require a passport for domestic travel. International visitors who lose their passport should contact their country's nearest consulate in Denver or the embassy in Washington, D.C.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
