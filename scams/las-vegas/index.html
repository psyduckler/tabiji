The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Costumed Character Photo Shakedown
🔶 Medium
📍 Las Vegas Strip (especially near Caesars Palace and the LINQ)

Costumed showgirls, superheroes, and character performers on the Las Vegas Strip initiate unsolicited physical contact with tourists, have an accomplice snap an unrequested photo, then demand $20 per person — using verbal abuse and physical blocking for anyone who declines to pay.

On the sidewalk near Caesars Palace and the LINQ, costumed performers work a deliberate entrapment choreography. One approaches a tourist and throws an arm around their shoulder with a cheerful 'let's get a photo,' while a second crew member with a camera materializes immediately to capture the moment. The entire sequence completes before the visitor has processed that it started — the photo exists before any agreement was reached.

The demand arrives the moment the shutter clicks: $20 per person, per performer. A group of two costumed characters means $40 for a photo no one requested. Former Strip security staff have documented these performers following tourists inside casino properties when refused, continuing the pressure campaign indoors. A widely circulated traveler forum report described them as 'getting really nasty and downright mean if you don't pay them,' with some physically grabbing wallets and blocking exits. The scam activates the moment a visitor slows down or makes eye contact — hesitation is treated as implied consent to the whole transaction.

Physical distance before contact is the only clean defense. Keep walking and avoid eye contact with any costumed performers on the Strip — the moment you stop, look, or allow physical contact, you have entered their operating space. If you genuinely want a photo with a performer, agree on the price before any posing begins. You are not legally required to pay for any photo taken without your prior agreement.

Red Flags

  • Performer approaches you and initiates physical contact without invitation
  • A second person with a camera materializes immediately to 'capture the moment'
  • No prices are shown before the photo is taken
  • Performer becomes aggressive or blocks your path when you try to walk away
  • Multiple costumed characters suddenly surround you

How to Avoid

  • Keep walking and say 'no thank you' firmly without making eye contact.
  • Never let someone put something in your hands or around your shoulders.
  • If you want a photo, agree on a price before any posing begins.
  • Walk into a nearby casino if you're being followed or harassed.
  • Remember: you owe nothing to unsolicited performers on the Strip.
Scam #2
Timeshare Vacation Club Pitch
⚠️ High
📍 Las Vegas Strip sidewalks, hotel lobbies, and around the Convention Center

Strip sidewalk recruiters offer free show tickets and buffet credits in exchange for a '90-minute' vacation club presentation that routinely stretches to 4–6 hours of coordinated high-pressure sales — designed to produce a timeshare contract worth $15,000–$40,000 before visitors are allowed to leave.

Walking back from dinner on the Strip, a smiling person with a clipboard intercepts a couple: 'Congratulations — you've been selected for a free buffet and two show tickets, just come to a brief 90-minute presentation tomorrow morning.' Vegas visitors are already primed for extras, and free tickets in a city where headline shows run $100–$400 sounds like a genuine windfall. The recruiter is pleasant, non-pushy, and easy to say yes to at the door of a restaurant.

The 90-minute presentation becomes a 4–6 hour ordeal in a hermetically sealed conference room, where trained salespeople cycle through the visitors in shifts specifically to deplete resistance over time. Visitors can legally leave at any moment, but the room is designed to disorient, the social pressure is calibrated to make walking out feel like a failure, and by hour three signing a vacation club membership at $15,000–$40,000 just to end the session feels like a relief rather than a decision. Reddit travel forums have documented the pipeline as internationally organized: timeshare presentations pitched in Mexico and the Caribbean are often traced back to Las Vegas-headquartered sales organizations.

The trap activates entirely at the moment you accept the street invitation. Never accept free tickets, buffet credits, or casino chips from clipboard-holding strangers on the Strip — any such offer is a timeshare recruitment and the value of the gift will be recaptured many times over. If you are already inside a presentation and want to leave, stand up and walk out — you can leave at any time and owe nothing. Ask before attending whether it is a timeshare presentation; they are legally required to answer honestly.

Red Flags

  • Someone offers you free tickets, buffet credits, or casino chips on the street
  • They ask for just '90 minutes of your time' for a 'brief presentation'
  • You're taken to an off-Strip location or a nondescript hotel conference room
  • Sales staff rotate through you in shifts and the session keeps getting extended
  • You feel unable to leave and the room seems designed to disorient you

How to Avoid

  • Never accept free gifts from strangers on the Strip — the value will be recaptured tenfold.
  • If you're already inside, you can legally leave at any time; stand up and walk out.
  • Ask upfront: 'Is this a timeshare presentation?' — they're legally required to answer honestly.
  • Never sign anything under time pressure; take any contract to read overnight.
  • Research the company name on Google before agreeing to anything.
Scam #3
Fake Dispensary Overcharging
⚠️ High
📍 Areas adjacent to the Strip; unlicensed storefronts in tourist corridors

Unlicensed and predatory cannabis storefronts near the Las Vegas Strip sell products to tourists at $60–$80 per gram — four to six times the $10–$20 licensed dispensary rate — using professional branding that mimics legitimate operations and adding unlawful 'recommendation' and 'packaging' fees that only appear at checkout.

Nevada's legal cannabis market draws many visitors who have never purchased recreational cannabis before and have no reference point for what products should cost or what a compliant storefront looks like. A visitor wanders into what appears to be a legitimate dispensary — professional branding, organized product displays, glass cases, a budtender in a black polo shirt — and assumes that any visible cannabis shop must be properly licensed. The professional presentation is the scam's primary tool, and it is deliberately indistinguishable from legitimate operations to the uninformed eye.

The visitor pays $80 for a gram of mid-grade flower that any Nevada Cannabis Control Board–licensed dispensary would sell for $10–$20. A traveler warning that went viral in 2023 stated plainly: 'There are fake dispensaries — I felt it was my duty to protect unsuspecting tourists from this deceptive trap.' Some operations use names confusingly similar to well-known legitimate brands. Others are entirely unlicensed with no product testing, and mislabeled or contaminated products have caused severe reactions in inexperienced users. 'Recommendation fees,' 'packaging fees,' and 'delivery minimums' make the true total clear only at checkout — after the social awkwardness of walking out mid-transaction feels worse than paying.

Every licensed Nevada dispensary is required to display its state license number prominently — an unlicensed operation has no license to show. Verify any dispensary on the Nevada Cannabis Control Board's public license list or use Weedmaps' verified listings before visiting, and treat any per-gram price above $20 as a strong signal to leave and find a licensed alternative. If staff resist showing you a posted price menu or push back on seeing state test results (COA), walk out.

Red Flags

  • Store is located in a strip mall far from major casino corridors
  • No Nevada Marijuana Establishment License number displayed prominently
  • Prices are not clearly labeled or vary dramatically from what was quoted
  • Staff push you toward expensive products and resist questions
  • The 'dispensary' name sounds similar to a well-known legitimate brand

How to Avoid

  • Only visit dispensaries licensed by the Nevada Cannabis Control Board — check the list online.
  • Use apps like Weedmaps or Leafly to find verified, reviewed dispensaries near the Strip.
  • Prices in legit dispensaries run roughly $10–$20 per gram; anything higher is a red flag.
  • Ask to see the product's state-issued test results (COA) before purchasing.
  • If a deal sounds too good to be true or staff seem aggressive, walk out.

Scam #4
Free CD / Mixtape Hustle
🟢 Low
📍 Las Vegas Strip pedestrian walkways, especially near Planet Hollywood and MGM

On Las Vegas Strip pedestrian walkways near Planet Hollywood and MGM, aspiring 'musicians' thrust unsolicited CDs into tourists' hands and immediately solicit $10–$20 'donations,' exploiting the social obligation created by physical possession before any exchange was agreed.

Walking along the Strip near Planet Hollywood, a man rushes forward with a CD extended: 'Yo, take this — it's free, just check out my music.' Before the visitor finishes processing the words, the disc is in their hand. The physical transfer is the mechanism: once you are holding something someone gave you, social norms make returning it feel hostile, and the sense of obligation that creates is the product being sold, not the CD.

The moment the CD lands, the life story begins — the recording studio, the kids, the dream, the one chance he is asking you to believe in with a small donation. The ask starts at $10 and escalates with persistent storytelling. A travel advice post that circulated widely among Vegas visitors put the countermeasure bluntly: 'Keep your hands busy — in your pockets, holding your partner's hand, holding your drink. Keep your hands busy so you can't take anything from anybody on the Strip.' A parallel version runs with street musicians who perform directly in your walking path, make eye contact, and demand payment when you stop for more than a few seconds to watch.

The scam requires your hands to be available at the moment of approach. Keep your hands in your pockets or visibly occupied whenever you are walking the Strip's pedestrian areas — if your hands cannot hold the CD, the transaction never begins. If something is placed in your hand anyway, return it and keep walking without engaging with the backstory or the donation request; you are not obligated to respond.

Red Flags

  • A stranger rushes toward you with something in their outstretched hand
  • They place the object in your hand before you've agreed to take it
  • The word 'free' is used to lower your guard
  • A sad or compelling backstory follows immediately after the 'gift'
  • Multiple people nearby seem to be watching the interaction

How to Avoid

  • Keep your hands in your pockets or visibly occupied while walking the Strip.
  • Don't make extended eye contact with people who are trying to flag you down.
  • If something is placed in your hand, simply hand it back and keep walking.
  • Say 'I'm good' loudly and keep your stride — hesitation invites escalation.
  • Remember: accepting something from a stranger on the Strip is never truly free.
Scam #5
Resort Fee / Hidden Hotel Charges
🔶 Medium
📍 Hotels across Las Vegas, especially on and near the Strip

Las Vegas Strip hotels advertise rooms at $49–$79 per night while adding mandatory 'resort fees' of $30–$50 per night at checkout — inflating the real nightly cost by 60–140% — with no opt-out available at check-in and no meaningful disclosure on most comparison booking sites.

A visitor books what appears to be a strong Strip deal: $49 a night at a major casino-hotel. The room is real. The casino is real. But the advertised rate is not the total rate. Nevada law requires resort fees to be disclosed somewhere in the booking flow, but the disclosure is typically buried in fine print well below the headline nightly rate, and many comparison platforms historically showed the lower number as the default listing price.

At checkout, the three-night bill shows $189 instead of $147 — the difference is the mandatory resort fee, typically $30–$50 per night, covering the pool, gym, Wi-Fi, and parking regardless of whether any were used. Beyond the resort fee, the Las Vegas hospitality infrastructure has refined the surrounding charge framework: casino ATMs run $8–$12 per withdrawal, minibar sensors charge guests just for lifting items without purchasing, show tickets carry 'facility fees' separate from face value, and restaurants add 'service charges' on top of standard gratuity expectations. A Las Vegas resident with twenty years in commercial finance described the pattern directly: 'I've never seen more normalized fraud than in Vegas.'

The fee is disclosed — it is just disclosed deliberately badly, after the booking decision is made. Before booking any Strip hotel, search the total nightly price including resort fees on ResortFeeChecker.com or a transparent comparison tool, and budget at least $40–$50 per night above any listed room rate to account for mandatory charges that cannot be declined at check-in. Some hotel loyalty programs and premium credit cards carry resort fee waivers — worth confirming before booking rather than disputing at the front desk on arrival.

Red Flags

  • Hotel advertises a rate that seems dramatically lower than comparable properties
  • Terms like 'resort fee,' 'destination fee,' or 'amenity fee' appear in small text
  • Check-in staff casually mention fees as though they're standard
  • ATM machines inside the casino lobby charge $8–$12 per transaction
  • Minibar has a sensor base that charges you if you lift items for more than a few seconds

How to Avoid

  • Search hotel total price including fees on sites like ResortFeeChecker.com before booking.
  • Ask hotel front desk to waive the resort fee if you're a rewards member or arrived via a cashback card.
  • Use a bank ATM outside the casino (7-Elevens nearby often have low-fee ATMs).
  • Do not touch the minibar unless you intend to buy — even lifting items can trigger a charge.
  • Budget at least $40–$50/night above the listed room rate for mandatory fees.
Scam #6
Three-Card Monte and Rigged Street Games
⚠️ High
📍 Downtown Las Vegas (Fremont Street area), less frequented side streets off Strip

Three-card monte operators and shell game crews near Fremont Street and Strip side streets use planted accomplices as visible 'winners' to lure tourists into street games that are mathematically impossible to win, extracting $50–$200 before victims realize the game is fixed and the crew has already scattered.

Near Fremont Street Experience after dark, a crowd has gathered around a portable folding table where a fast-talking dealer flips three cards. Two bystanders bet in quick succession and win easily, each one pointing out where the queen ended up with encouraging confidence. The movements are visible, the logic looks sound, and the dealer appears to be making trackable mistakes. The setup is the trap, because every winning bystander in the crowd is a paid crew member whose job is to make the game look beatable.

The moment a tourist places real money down, winning becomes impossible. The dealer uses sleight-of-hand that makes the card appear to land where it was tracked while it goes somewhere else. Victims lose $50–$200 before accepting that the game was fixed from the first bet — by that point the cash is gone, the operator has pocketed it, and the folding table has disappeared. Operators use lookouts to signal when police are approaching, and the entire crew can relocate in under sixty seconds. A first responder who worked the Strip summarized it simply: 'If a stranger engages you with an offer for quick money, it's probably a scam.'

Any street game with a crowd of visible winners on a public sidewalk is a coordinated con. Walk past without slowing down or making eye contact — lingering for even a few seconds signals willingness to play, and the crew will redirect their full attention to you immediately. Legal gambling in Las Vegas happens inside licensed casinos with transparent house odds; anything played outdoors on a portable table is a rigged operation with no legitimate version.

Red Flags

  • A crowd has formed around a portable table or cardboard box on the street
  • Multiple people seem to win easily before you place a bet
  • The dealer talks very fast and uses misdirection hands
  • Someone in the crowd enthusiastically encourages you to play
  • The game is set up in a location with easy escape routes

How to Avoid

  • Never gamble on the street — legal gambling in Vegas happens inside licensed casinos.
  • Understand that anyone winning 'too easily' is part of the operation.
  • Walk past without slowing down or engaging — lingering signals willingness.
  • Report street gambling operations to casino security or Metro Police at 702-828-3111.
  • If you want card games, the casinos offer the real thing with house-edge odds that are at least transparent.
Scam #7
Overpriced Limousine / Party Bus Bait-and-Switch
🔶 Medium
📍 McCarran International Airport arrivals, and Strip pickup zones

Unlicensed limo drivers and party bus promoters at Harry Reid International Airport and Strip pickup zones quote low flat fares, provide unrequested champagne and amenities en route, then present bills two to three times the agreed amount on arrival — citing charges that were never disclosed before boarding.

Just arrived at Harry Reid International Airport and primed for the trip, a visitor in the arrivals hall is approached by a man in a black suit offering a limo ride to their hotel at a rate that sounds reasonable. The vehicle is clean, the driver is professional-looking, and the excitement of the moment overrides the instinct to get a price in writing. The primary targets are bachelor and bachelorette party groups arriving in celebratory mode — the demographic least likely to pause for paperwork.

Champagne appears unbidden inside the vehicle. At the hotel, the bill arrives at three times what was verbally agreed: a 'champagne charge,' 'waiting time,' 'automatically added gratuity,' and a 'fuel surcharge' never mentioned before boarding. By this point the guests are already out of the car and disputing in front of their traveling companions — the social awkwardness of the moment is the final lever. A party bus variant runs from the Strip itself: promoters hand out flyers for 'free limo rides to the best clubs' that convert to club entry packages, bottle service minimums, and undisclosed transportation fees once everyone is two drinks in and already rolling toward an unspecified destination.

A written price confirmation before boarding is the only protection that survives arrival. Use Uber or Lyft from designated airport rideshare zones — rates are set in the app before you enter the car — or, when booking a car service, get a written total including all fees and charges before stepping inside the vehicle. Ask to see the driver's Nevada Transportation Authority permit; licensed operators carry one, and any driver who becomes evasive about credentials or total price is a reason to walk away.

Red Flags

  • Driver doesn't show you a written price quote before you get in the vehicle
  • Drinks or amenities appear without you requesting them
  • The vehicle is unmarked or the driver doesn't have a visible license/permit
  • Promoters on the Strip are handing you flyers for 'free transportation'
  • The driver is evasive when you ask for the per-mile or per-hour rate

How to Avoid

  • Use rideshares (Uber/Lyft) from designated airport zones — rates are transparent and fixed upfront.
  • If using a car service, get a written quote including all fees before boarding.
  • Do not consume anything in an unofficial vehicle that you didn't bring yourself.
  • Reputable limo companies are licensed by the Nevada Taxicab Authority — ask for credentials.
  • Your hotel's bell desk can arrange legitimate transportation at known rates.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Local Police Department station. Call 911. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at usa.gov/crimes.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Visit the nearest US Passport Agency. For international visitors, contact your country's consulate or embassy directly. US State Department emergency line: +1-888-407-4747 (from US) or +1-202-501-4444 (international).

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
