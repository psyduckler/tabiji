Counterfeit currency, back as your change.

A 1000-peso note in a Buenos Aires taxi. A 200-pound note from a Khan el-Khalili shop. A 200-dirham note in a Marrakech souk. The same four mechanics — taxi bill swap, small-shop change padding, cambio counterfeit mix, private-ATM dispense — running across 43 countries. The hand-counting moves that defeat them are the same in every currency.

A scene · Buenos Aires Microcentro · 11pm Saturday

"Señor, this 1000-peso bill is fake — you'll have to give me another."

You step out of a Microcentro restaurant on Avenida Corrientes at 11pm and flag down a yellow-and-black radio taxi for a ten-minute ride back to your Palermo apartment. The meter shows ARS 4,200 when you pull up at the door; you hand the driver a crisp 5,000-peso note from the Western Union envelope you collected this morning at Plaza San Martín. The driver holds the bill up to his dashboard light, frowns, turns it over, and shakes his head. "Señor, este billete no sirve — está falso." This bill doesn't work; it's fake.

He hands it back to you. You stare at it. The note is rough to the touch, a fraction wider than the others in your wallet, and the "5000" numeral in the lower-right doesn't shift color when you tilt it. It is fake. But you didn't get it that way. The bill the driver just handed you is not the bill you handed him.

The palm-swap happened in the two seconds between when your bill went toward the dashboard light and when his hand came back. He had a counterfeit ready in his left palm; the swap is muscle memory after a thousand attempts. Now you're at midnight in front of your apartment with a fake bill in your hand and a driver waiting for a "real" replacement. You give him another 5,000-peso note. He drives away. The original genuine bill is in his pocket. The total loss is 5,000 pesos — about $4 USD at 2026 rates, but ten times that if it had been the new 20,000-peso note Argentina introduced in 2024.

That is the taxi bill swap, the highest-friction of four documented channels by which counterfeit currency moves from a local operator into a tourist's wallet. The other three are a small shop returning counterfeit notes mixed into your change, an unofficial 'cambio' on Florida Avenue handing you a stack containing one or two fakes, and a private ATM in a pharmacy or mini-market dispensing counterfeit-mixed cash. Each runs the same way in 43 countries with the same defenses: small bills matched to the fare, hand-checks before pocketing, named-bank ATMs only.

Key Takeaways

  • 💵Pay with small bills matched to the price. The taxi bill swap and the small-shop change scam both fail when there's no high-denomination note to swap or pad.
  • 🔍Hold any high-denomination note to the light for 5 seconds before pocketing it. Watermarks, color-shifting numerals, and tactile relief defeat counterfeits in every currency.
  • 👀Count change audibly while the merchant is still watching. Disputes resolve immediately; once you've left the venue, the loss stays with you.
  • 💳Use cards above the counterfeit-risk threshold. ARS 50,000 in Argentina, EGP 500 in Egypt, MAD 200 in Morocco, VND 500,000 in Vietnam, TRY 200 in Turkey.
  • 🏦ATMs only at named major-bank main branches. Pharmacy, mini-market, and street-kiosk ATMs are the highest-risk channel for counterfeit-mixed dispensing.

The hand-counting routine

Counterfeit-return scams depend on you not noticing for the seconds it takes to leave. The swap happens in two seconds; the change-padding in five; the ATM dispense doesn't reveal itself until you spend the bill somewhere else. The defensive routine is a single 60-second sequence that runs once per high-value cash transaction, in any country.

  1. Pay in small bills, matched to price. The taxi swap and the small-shop change-pad both depend on a high-denomination bill leaving your hand. Eliminate the bill, eliminate the scam. For taxis, pay with notes within 20% of the fare; for market stalls and small shops, pay with the smallest bill that covers the price plus reasonable change. The single most effective preventive move; defeats two of the four channels at zero cost.
  2. Hold each high-denomination note to the light. Watermark first: the genuine portrait should appear as a soft shadow shifting in the paper itself, not printed on top. Color-shifting ink second: tilt the bill and the denomination numeral should shift hue (gold-to-green, copper-to-purple). Tactile relief third: run your fingernail across the portrait or numeral; raised intaglio printing means genuine. The 5-second light check kills every variant where the bill is in your hand long enough to inspect.
  3. Count change audibly while the merchant watches. Don't pocket the change and walk. Count it aloud, slowly, with the merchant or driver still in eye contact. Any short-count or counterfeit gets challenged before the transaction is functionally over. Confrontation is far cheaper while you're still in the venue than two streets later when the merchant denies everything.
  4. Card the high-value transactions. Above ARS 50,000 in Argentina, EGP 500 in Egypt, MAD 200 in Morocco, VND 500,000 in Vietnam, TRY 200 in Turkey: use a credit or debit card. Card transactions cannot be counterfeited at the point of sale; the friction of a foreign-transaction fee is far less than the cost of a counterfeit bill in your wallet. Cards also create a chargeback path for any disputed transaction.
  5. ATMs only at named major-bank main branches. Argentina: Banco Nación, Santander, BBVA, HSBC, Galicia, Macro main branches. Egypt: NBE, CIB, QNB. Morocco: Attijariwafa, BMCE, Banque Populaire. Vietnam: Vietcombank, BIDV, Agribank. Turkey: Garanti, İşbank, Akbank. Avoid ATMs in pharmacies, mini-markets, hotel lobbies, and standalone street kiosks where private operators stock the cassette themselves. The dispenser is the supply chain; choose the supply chain.

Four channels of counterfeit return

Different markets lean on different mechanics within the same family. Here are the four channels we've documented globally. Each has a recognition tell, a primary geography, and the routine step that defeats it.

Taxis · Late-night fares · Microcentro returns

1. Taxi Bill Swap

The driver takes your large-denomination bill, pretends to inspect it under the dashboard light, palm-swaps it for a counterfeit ready in their other hand, then "discovers" the fake and demands you replace it with a real note. The mechanic depends on the inspection being theatrical enough that you don't notice the bill that comes back is not the bill you handed over. Highest-friction variant; commonly runs at ride-end when you're tired and stationary outside your destination.

What it feels like: a slightly-too-long inspection of a bill you know was real when you handed it over.

Most reported in: Buenos Aires Microcentro returns; Cairo Giza-area drivers; Hanoi Old Quarter routes; Istanbul Sultanahmet–Taksim runs; Phnom Penh tuk-tuks.

Souks · Markets · Small shops · Late-night corners

2. Small-Shop Change Padding

You buy a small souvenir, snack, or scarf for a low price; you pay with a high-denomination bill; the shopkeeper returns change that includes one or two counterfeit notes mixed into a stack of genuine ones. The math is engineered: change is mostly real to pass quick visual inspection, but the high-value notes inside are counterfeits. By the time you spend one and the next merchant refuses it, you're streets away with no recourse.

What it feels like: a friendly transaction, a thicker stack of bills than expected, a too-good price that just paid for itself.

Most reported in: Marrakech and Fez souks; Khan el-Khalili Cairo; Istanbul Grand Bazaar; Hanoi Old Quarter; Phnom Penh Russian Market.

Street cambios · Florida Ave · Souk-edge changers

3. Cambio Counterfeit Mix

An unofficial money-changer offers a rate that beats the official banking rate. They count a stack of local-currency bills face-down in front of you, fast, often using a "counting board" or sleight-of-hand to short-count or substitute. The handed bundle contains one or two counterfeits buried in the middle. By the time you spot them at your hotel, the changer is two corners away or has handed off to a colleague. In some markets (Buenos Aires Florida Avenue, Marrakech souk edges) the practice runs continuously despite police presence because the catchment is structurally renewable.

What it feels like: a fast-paced transaction with rates and patter that signal urgency; you don't get to count slowly because the changer wants you out of view of police.

Most reported in: Buenos Aires Florida Avenue 'arbolitos'; Marrakech Place Jemaa el-Fna edges; Cairo Khan el-Khalili shops; Tashkent and Dushanbe street markets.

Pharmacy · Mini-market · Hotel lobby · Street kiosk ATMs

4. Private-ATM Counterfeit Dispense

Independent ATMs operated by private cash-management companies (often visible by branding that's not a major bank logo) are stocked manually by an operator who controls the cassette. A small percentage of high-denomination notes — often 1 in 5 or 1 in 10 — are counterfeit, mixed in deliberately. The tourist withdraws cash, doesn't check, and only realizes when a counterfeit gets refused at a later transaction. By then the ATM operator has rotated stock and the chain of custody is impossible to prove.

What it feels like: nothing at the moment of withdrawal. The realization comes hours later, often at a different city or different country.

Most reported in: tourist-area pharmacy ATMs in Buenos Aires, Cairo, Istanbul, Hanoi, Phnom Penh; hotel-lobby ATMs in package-tour destinations; standalone street-kiosk ATMs in Old Town pedestrian zones.

Four more cities, four more channels

The Buenos Aires scene above showed the taxi-bill-swap variant. Here are four more cities where different counterfeit channels dominate. Each links to the full city scam guide.

Cairo, Egypt · Khan el-Khalili & Giza Small-Shop Change Padding · Driver Bill Swap

You walk into Khan el-Khalili at golden hour and step into a stall on Sikka el-Khan offering a small alabaster Anubis for 80 Egyptian pounds. You hand over a 200-pound note from this morning's NBE withdrawal. The shopkeeper hands back a stack of mixed denominations: a 100, two 20s, one 10, and a five. You glance, pocket, and walk on through the smoke and the calls of the spice-market alley toward Bab Zuweila. Two stalls later, when you try to pay 50 pounds for a paper cone of dukkah, the second shopkeeper holds your 100-pound note up to the light and shakes his head. The watermark is missing; the security thread is printed-on rather than embedded. The first shop is half a kilometer back through the warren, and the shopkeeper there smiles, denies, and offers tea. Khan el-Khalili runs the small-shop-change-pad continuously through tourist season; the same alley also runs the Giza-driver bill-swap variant on rides back to Zamalek or Garden City. Defense: at any Cairo shop, count high-denomination change to the light before leaving the stall; for taxis, use Careem or Uber to pay app-side and never hand cash to a Giza-area driver returning to your hotel.

Marrakech, Morocco · Souks & Place Jemaa el-Fna Cambio Counterfeit Mix · Souk Change Padding

You wander into the leather souk north of Place Jemaa el-Fna at midday and find a small slipper shop offering a hand-stitched pair for 200 dirhams. You pay with a 500-dirham note from the souk-edge cambio you used at noon. The shopkeeper counts back a stack of 50s and 20s, slowly enough to feel honest; you pocket and walk to the next stall. By the third shop, when you try to pay 80 dirhams for a tea-glass set, the merchant holds your 50-dirham note up to the medina light filtering through the slats and shakes his head: cette banque ne marche pas. The note has no watermark and the color-shifting "50" is a flat-printed imitation. Marrakech runs the cambio-mix variant on the souk edges (where street-changers operate adjacent to legitimate exchange offices) and the change-pad variant inside the souks themselves. Place Jemaa el-Fna at night runs an additional sleight-of-hand variant where the cambio uses a "counting board" to display rates and bills, then swaps a portion of the count under the cover of evening crowd noise. Defense: exchange currency only at Bank Al-Maghrib licensed offices (not street cambios); inside souks, hold every high-denomination note returned as change to the alley's edge light before pocketing. Real lost-and-found in Marrakech goes to the Brigade Touristique near Place Jemaa el-Fna.

Hanoi, Vietnam · Old Quarter & Tour Booking Taxi Bill Swap · Tour-Office Counterfeit Stack

You catch a taxi from Hoan Kiem Lake back to your Old Quarter hotel at 10pm after a Bia Hoi crawl. The fare meter shows 80,000 dong; you hand the driver a 500,000-dong note (Vietnam's largest commonly-circulating denomination, equivalent to about $20 USD in 2026). He holds it up to the dashboard light, frowns, turns it over, and tells you in mixed English-Vietnamese that the note is fake. He hands back something that is fake — but it is not the note you handed him. The taxi-bill-swap mechanic in Hanoi is identical to Buenos Aires: same inspection-and-palm-swap, same demand for replacement, same deep-pocket loss. The Old Quarter variant also runs through unofficial tour-booking offices that hand back "change" in stacks containing one or two counterfeit 500,000-dong notes after a Halong Bay tour purchase. The Vietnam State Bank issued public guidance in 2023 specifically about counterfeit 500,000-dong notes circulating through tourist channels in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City. Defense: use Grab or Be (Vietnamese Uber equivalents) to bypass cash entirely; for tour bookings, use only verified operators (Hoi An Express, Klook, Get Your Guide) where payment is card-side and no cash counterfeit can enter the chain.

Istanbul, Turkey · Grand Bazaar & Sultanahmet Souk Change Padding · Sultanahmet Taxi

You walk through the Kapalı Çarşı (Grand Bazaar) at midafternoon and step into a small textile shop on the Cevahir Bedesteni alley. You buy a hand-loomed scarf for 350 lira; you pay with a 500-lira note from a Garanti Bank ATM near Sultanahmet. The shopkeeper counts back a 100, a 50, and three 20s — clean denominations, fast count. You pocket and walk on. Three shops later, when you try to pay 80 lira for a small ceramic tile, the merchant returns your 50-lira note: it has no watermark, no color-shifting "50," no embedded thread, and the paper feels papery. The Grand Bazaar runs the small-shop-change-pad continuously, particularly at the Cevahir Bedesteni's antique alleys where carpet and textile shops handle higher-value transactions. The Polis Tourism Bureau in Sultanahmet (just off Yerebatan Caddesi) accepts reports in English; recovery rates are low but the report number is required for travel-insurance claims. Defense: count change to bazaar-vault light before leaving any shop; for Sultanahmet–Beyoğlu taxis, use BiTaksi or iTaksi apps to pay app-side and bypass cash entirely.

Red flags

If two or more of these signals fire during a cash transaction in a tourist zone, slow the transaction down and run the hand-counting routine before any bills change hands. The compounding rule: a single signal can be a coincidence; two signals are a script.

  • Driver or merchant insists on inspecting your high-denomination bill before completing the transaction
  • Inspection takes longer than two seconds and involves both hands
  • Bill returned for "verification" feels different from the bill you handed over
  • Change comes back as a thicker stack than you expected for the transaction
  • Cambio offers a rate that beats the official banking rate by more than 5%
  • Cambio counts notes face-down or fast enough that you can't follow each bill
  • ATM is in a pharmacy, mini-market, hotel lobby, or standalone street kiosk
  • ATM screen lacks the major-bank logo or has an unfamiliar branding
  • Small shop in a tourist zone with no posted prices or no card reader visible
  • Transaction happens late at night, in poor light, or with the vendor visibly hurried

The phrases that shut it down

Most counterfeit-return scams die with a hand-counting routine and don't require a verbal refusal. But when a driver or shopkeeper insists your bill is fake, a clear local-language phrase reverses the social pressure and forces the transaction back to legitimate channels.

"Devuélvame el billete original."
"Give me back the original bill." Said firmly to a taxi driver claiming your bill is fake; reverses the swap pressure.

If you got hit

You're back at your hotel and a counterfeit bill is in your wallet, or you spent one and the next merchant refused it. The recovery options for counterfeit-return scams are unfortunately limited; the actionable response is preventive for the next encounter, not recovery for this one.

Within the first hour: report the counterfeit at the local central-bank counterfeit hotline. The bank will not reimburse you, but the report contributes to enforcement and improves the chances that the operator gets caught by aggregate-pattern analysis. The act of reporting is also psychologically useful: it converts the loss from "I got scammed" into "I logged a data point that helps the next traveler."

  • Argentina: BCRA (Banco Central) 0800-999-6663 (24/7); Comisaría Turística +54 11 4346 5748 (Av. Corrientes 436, English-speaking).
  • Egypt: Tourist Police +20 126 (24/7 short code); Central Bank counterfeit reporting via NBE branches; National Police 122.
  • Morocco: Brigade Touristique (Marrakech, Place Jemaa el-Fna area); Bank Al-Maghrib counterfeit deposits at any main branch; Police +212 19 (national).
  • Vietnam: Vietcombank or State Bank counterfeit reporting; Tourist Information Center Hanoi +84 24 3826 5077.
  • Turkey: Polis Tourism Bureau (Yerebatan Caddesi, Sultanahmet); Garanti or İşbank counterfeit reporting at main branches; Polis 155 (national).

For travel-insurance claims, the central-bank report number is sufficient documentation; most policies cover counterfeit losses under "currency fraud" up to a per-incident cap (typically $200–500). American Express and Chase Sapphire premium cards include cash-replacement assistance; a counterfeit loss often qualifies for partial reimbursement under the Lost & Stolen Card Cash Advance terms — check your card directory before paying out of pocket. The most actionable response is to update your hand-counting routine and prevent the next encounter; banks rarely reimburse counterfeits returned by individuals, and the loss usually stays with whoever holds the bill.

Three structural reasons. First, tourists are walking inventory of high-denomination notes that have never been counted by a local — perfect targets for swap or short-change. Second, tourists usually don't speak the language well enough to negotiate or argue mid-transaction; the moment of confrontation favors the local. Third, by the time a tourist realizes a counterfeit is in their wallet, they're often kilometers away and likely to write off the loss rather than file a report; the operator faces near-zero downside. The defense is structural: use small bills, use cards above the counterfeit-risk threshold, ATM only at named banks, hold every note to the light before pocketing it.
