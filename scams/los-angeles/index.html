The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Hollywood Walk of Fame Costume Character Photo Scam
🔶 Medium
📍 Hollywood Walk of Fame, Hollywood Boulevard

Costumed characters on Hollywood Boulevard — Spider-Man, Supergirl, and others — initiate unsolicited physical contact with tourists, have a 'manager' accomplice snap a photo, then demand $20–$50 per person, following and blocking anyone who declines to pay.

On Hollywood Boulevard near the Walk of Fame star plaques, costumed performers in superhero and character outfits work a practiced entrapment sequence. One approaches a tourist photographing the sidewalk, throws an arm around their shoulder with a cheerful 'let's get a photo,' and the character's 'manager' — who has materialized from the surrounding crowd — snaps the shot immediately. The entire sequence completes in seconds before the visitor has processed that it started or agreed to any terms.

The demand arrives right after the shutter: $20, $30, sometimes $50 per character, per person. Two characters in one photo means $60–$100 for an encounter no one agreed to initiate. A tourist documented the experience precisely: 'A woman dressed as Supergirl started following us around the Walk of Fame, then suddenly demanded payment when we tried to leave.' The characters operate in a legal gray area — courts have upheld their right to work the boulevard as buskers — and that protection gives them confidence to follow, block paths, and verbally pressure anyone who refuses. The pressure escalates in direct proportion to how long the visitor hesitates or tries to negotiate.

Physical proximity before contact is where the encounter is won or lost. Keep moving and avoid eye contact with costumed performers on Hollywood Boulevard — the moment you stop, look, or allow physical contact, you've entered their operational space. If you genuinely want a photo with a performer, establish and agree on the exact price per person before any posing, touching, or framing begins. You owe nothing for any photo taken without your prior agreement.

Red Flags

  • Costumed character approaches you unprompted or makes physical contact
  • Photo is taken without your explicit agreement on pricing first
  • A separate 'manager' appears immediately after the photo
  • They block your path or follow you when you try to walk away
  • The demanded price jumps dramatically once you're already in the photo

How to Avoid

  • Keep walking and do not make eye contact with costumed characters.
  • If you want a photo, negotiate the exact price BEFORE any contact or picture.
  • Stay on the move — stopping near these characters signals you're interested.
  • Use your phone to take photos of the star plaques, not the performers.
  • Know that these encounters are entirely optional — you owe them nothing if you didn't agree.
Scam #2
Fake Mixtape / Free CD Hustle
🔶 Medium
📍 Hollywood Walk of Fame, Hollywood & Highland area

Hustlers near the TCL Chinese Theatre and Hollywood & Highland press unsolicited CDs into tourists' hands, display a Venmo QR code immediately afterward, and demand $10–$20 in 'donations' — becoming increasingly aggressive toward anyone who tries to return the disc or walk away.

Near the TCL Chinese Theatre on Hollywood Boulevard, a man approaches a tourist walking alone, makes direct eye contact, and presses a CD into their hands: 'That's for you — free. I'm an up-and-coming artist.' Before the visitor can decline, the physical transfer is complete. The word 'free' is used to reduce resistance to accepting the object, because the entire mechanism depends on the social obligation created by physical possession before any price has been disclosed.

The moment the CD is in hand, a phone appears showing a Venmo QR code. The donation ask starts at $10 and escalates with persistence and volume — the life story of the recording studio, the family, the one chance the visitor is being asked to believe in. Reddit travel forums documented this hustle in Hollywood as far back as 2014, with consistent descriptions across years: the approach, the forced handoff, the escalating ask. The CDs are often blank or contain filler audio — the product has no value. Hustlers sometimes work in pairs, with one distracting from the front while another approaches from the side.

The scam requires your hands to be empty at the moment of approach. Keep your hands in your pockets or visibly holding something whenever you walk Hollywood Boulevard — if your hands are occupied, the CD has nowhere to land and the transaction never begins. If something is placed in your hands anyway, return it immediately without looking at it and keep moving. Do not say 'I don't have cash' — that sentence invites a negotiation about alternative payment methods. Report aggressive hustlers to the LAPD officers posted on the Walk of Fame.

Red Flags

  • Someone approaches specifically targeting you and places a physical item in your hands
  • They use the word 'free' before you've agreed to anything
  • They switch to demanding money immediately after you accept the item
  • There are multiple people nearby watching the interaction
  • The 'artist' becomes aggressive or follows you when you try to return the CD

How to Avoid

  • Never accept anything handed to you by a stranger on Hollywood Boulevard.
  • If something gets placed in your hands, immediately hand it back without looking at it.
  • Say 'no thank you' clearly and keep walking without slowing down.
  • Do not engage with their conversation — even saying 'I don't have cash' invites argument.
  • Report aggressive hustlers to the LAPD officers stationed on the Walk of Fame.
Scam #3
Fake Celebrity Home / Studio Tour Vans
🔶 Medium
📍 Hollywood Boulevard, Sunset Strip, Beverly Hills adjacent areas

Street recruiters near the Walk of Fame sell $30 celebrity home tours from unmarked vans that add undisclosed 'fuel surcharges' on arrival, navigate visitors past distant unidentified mansions with fabricated celebrity connections, and have no operating license or verifiable review history.

Standing near the Walk of Fame, a visitor is approached by an enthusiastic recruiter with a clipboard offering a 'celebrity homes tour' — only $30, departing in ten minutes, guaranteed to see A-list mansions including specific named celebrities. The pitch is quick, the van is parked nearby, and the 'leaving soon' urgency is designed to prevent the visitor from comparing alternatives or looking up the operator. The price and named celebrities make the offer sound like a bargain.

The van is beat-up and carries no company branding or operating license. The guide improvises facts throughout, and the 'celebrity homes' are distant glimpses of generic gated mansions that could belong to anyone — the guide supplies famous names confidently and without any verification. The advertised $30 becomes $40 by the end, with a 'fuel surcharge' that was not mentioned during the street pitch. Legitimate celebrity home tours operate from official stops at Hollywood & Highland and similar venues, have professionally branded vehicles, and appear on TripAdvisor and Viator with verifiable multi-year review histories — none of which applies to the street van operation.

A legitimate tour operator does not need sidewalk recruitment. Book celebrity home and studio tours only through established companies with verifiable reviews on TripAdvisor or Viator, at licensed booking counters rather than from someone with a clipboard and a 'leaving in ten minutes' urgency. Pay by credit card at a proper terminal for chargeback protection, and look up the company name on Google before paying anything.

Red Flags

  • Tours are sold aggressively on the street rather than at a proper ticket booth
  • No visible company name, license, or professional branding on the vehicle
  • Price quoted verbally doesn't match what you're actually charged
  • Departure time keeps changing — 'just 10 more minutes' — to gather more marks
  • No written receipt or confirmation of what the tour includes

How to Avoid

  • Book celebrity tours through established companies with verified reviews on TripAdvisor or Viator.
  • Look for licensed tour operators with proper signage and official vehicles.
  • Never pay cash on the street — use a credit card with a legitimate booking.
  • Check Google reviews for the specific company name before committing.
  • The real tours depart from official stops like Hollywood & Highland, not random sidewalks.

Scam #4
Fake Parking Meter QR Code Scam
⚠️ High
📍 Tourist parking areas across Los Angeles including near beaches and attractions

Fraudulent QR code stickers placed on parking meters near Venice Beach, Griffith Park, and other LA tourist areas redirect visitors to phishing sites that harvest credit card numbers — the same structure used by legitimate city meters makes the fakes visually undetectable once placed.

Pulling into a parking spot near Venice Beach or Griffith Park, a visitor notices a QR code on the meter post with a 'SCAN TO PAY' instruction. The presentation looks official — many LA meters and lots legitimately use QR-code payment systems, and the fraudulent sticker is designed to be indistinguishable from the genuine hardware by appearance alone. The visitor scans, enters their card details on a page that looks like a standard parking payment interface, and leaves.

The QR code was a fraudulent sticker placed over or adjacent to the real meter hardware, redirecting to a phishing site that captured card number and CVV in real time. The charge may not appear immediately — some harvested card details are sold in batches and exploited days or weeks later, making the parking meter connection harder to identify when reviewing statements. The scam specifically targets tourist parking zones near popular attractions, where out-of-town visitors are unfamiliar with which payment apps and which domain URLs belong to the City of Los Angeles.

The fraudulent sticker is visually indistinguishable from a legitimate one — the only safe approach is bypassing the physical meter entirely. Always pay using the official PayByPhone or ParkMobile apps downloaded directly from your device's app store rather than scanning any QR code found on physical meter hardware, because there is no reliable visual way to distinguish a fraudulent sticker. If you need to use the meter's own interface, call the phone number printed on the metal housing directly.

Red Flags

  • QR code appears as a sticker that doesn't look like part of the original meter hardware
  • The payment website URL looks unusual or doesn't match the city's official parking domain
  • The site asks for more information than just payment — name, full address, etc.
  • There's no city logo or official branding on the payment confirmation page
  • Other meters nearby look different from the one you're using

How to Avoid

  • Use the official LA city parking app (PayByPhone or ParkMobile) downloaded from the App Store.
  • Check that the QR code sticker appears to be factory-applied, not hand-placed over something.
  • Call the phone number printed on the meter directly if you're unsure.
  • Pay using coins if the meter accepts them — no digital risk.
  • Check your bank statements after parking in tourist areas.
Scam #5
Venice Beach Aggressive Vendor / Bracelet Scam
🔶 Medium
📍 Venice Beach Boardwalk

Vendors on the Venice Beach Boardwalk grab tourists' wrists without permission and tie braided bracelets onto them, then demand $20 and follow anyone who refuses — publicly calling them thieves in front of other visitors until they pay simply to escape the scene.

Walking the Venice Beach Boardwalk among street artists and performers, a visitor slows to look at artwork when a vendor grabs their wrist and begins tying a braided bracelet while speaking warmly about 'good luck' and 'friendship.' The physical action precedes any conversation about terms or price. By the time the visitor processes what is happening, the bracelet is knotted on and the demand follows immediately.

The ask is $20. When the visitor refuses, the vendor follows and calls out loudly, accusing them of 'taking a gift without paying' and 'stealing' in front of the surrounding boardwalk crowd. Other nearby vendors may turn to watch. The public shaming dynamic is the mechanism: paying $20 becomes the socially easiest exit from a scene that is drawing attention. Multiple visitors have documented the same pattern at Venice — the unprompted wrist grab, the tied bracelet, the public confrontation — noting that vendors work in loose coordination across the boardwalk zone so departing in one direction leads to encountering another.

Nothing placed on your body without permission creates any obligation. Step back immediately and put your hands in your pockets if any vendor on the Venice Boardwalk reaches toward you — physical distance before contact is far easier to maintain than untying a bracelet mid-confrontation. If a bracelet is already on your wrist, remove it calmly and set it on the nearest surface; walk away without engaging with the shaming. Report physically aggressive vendors to beach patrol officers on the boardwalk.

Red Flags

  • Vendor makes physical contact with you without asking permission first
  • They use the words 'free gift' or 'blessing' before attempting to tie something on you
  • Price is only revealed after the bracelet is already on your wrist
  • Multiple vendors are watching the interaction
  • They follow you or call out loudly to embarrass you when you try to leave

How to Avoid

  • Keep both hands in your pockets or crossed while walking the boardwalk.
  • Say 'no' firmly and clearly from a distance — don't let them get close.
  • If a bracelet gets placed on you, it's okay to remove it and hand it back.
  • Do not engage with the 'shaming' if they get loud — just walk away.
  • Report physically aggressive vendors to beach patrol officers on the boardwalk.
Scam #6
Rental Car Break-In (Smash and Grab)
⚠️ High
📍 Griffith Park, Universal Studios area, tourist parking lots throughout LA

Smash-and-grab crews target tourist rental cars parked at Griffith Park trailheads, observatory lots, and Universal Studios adjacent parking — breaking windows in under 30 seconds — and LA's vehicle break-in rate is among the highest of any major US city, with rental cars bearing out-of-state plates as the primary targets.

A visitor parks their rental car at the Griffith Observatory lot for a quick hike, leaving a suitcase in the trunk under a blanket. The lot is busy, the day is bright, and the trunk is locked. Rental company stickers on the bumper and an out-of-state plate identify the vehicle as almost certainly carrying all of a visitor's travel belongings — luggage, electronics, travel documents — unattended for at least an hour.

The visitor returns to a smashed rear window and an empty trunk — laptop, passport, clothes, and camera gone. Smash-and-grab crews can break a window, clear the interior, and be out of the lot in under thirty seconds. LA's vehicle break-in rate is among the highest of any major US city. Thieves specifically target rental cars at tourist-heavy locations: Griffith Park lots, Universal Studios adjacent parking, beach lots near Santa Monica and Venice, and Walk of Fame parking structures. Out-of-state plates combined with rental company branding are the primary selection criteria — they signal maximum probable contents and minimum owner response speed.

Removing the target is the only protection that works. Never leave any valuables in the rental car — not in the trunk, not under seats, not covered — take everything with you or leave it at the hotel before driving to any tourist attraction. Remove rental company stickers from the bumper if possible, and park in attended garages at busy attraction sites rather than remote trailhead lots where cars sit unmonitored for hours at a stretch.

Red Flags

  • Rental car stickers or plates that identify the vehicle as a rental to opportunistic thieves
  • Parking in isolated spots away from foot traffic
  • Leaving visible luggage, bags, or electronics on seats or in the trunk
  • Parking lots near popular tourist overlooks with high turnover
  • Maps or travel accessories visible through the window signaling a tourist

How to Avoid

  • Never leave any valuables in the car — not in the trunk, not under seats, nowhere.
  • Remove rental car stickers if possible or park in busy, well-lit areas.
  • Use hotel storage for luggage while exploring rather than car trunk.
  • Park in attended lots when visiting tourist attractions.
  • Report any suspicious individuals loitering in parking areas to lot attendants.
Scam #7
Fake 'Psychic Reading' or Spiritual Blessing Pressure
🟢 Low
📍 Hollywood Boulevard, Santa Monica Pier, Venice Beach

Self-described psychics near Santa Monica Pier, Hollywood Boulevard, and Venice Beach offer free palm or tarot readings, then diagnose a fabricated 'curse' that only they can remove for $100–$500 — escalating the curse's severity at every follow-up visit to extract additional payments across multiple sessions.

Near the Santa Monica Pier, a woman behind a velvet-draped table calls out to a passing visitor: 'You have a special energy — let me do a quick free reading.' The offer is brief and non-threatening, and flattery about one's personal energy is easy to accept on a sunny afternoon. She settles into palm reading or a tarot draw, building rapport with observations calibrated to feel specific while remaining broadly applicable to almost anyone.

Then comes the diagnosis: a 'curse' or 'dark energy' surrounding the visitor that she has sensed, with a vague but concerning origin story — a jealous person, a spiritual imbalance, something following the visitor from the past. The only remedy is a 'spiritual cleansing' that costs $100. If the visitor pays, the follow-up session reveals the cleansing was incomplete — the curse was stronger than initially detected and the next stage costs $200. Some tourists have been extracted for several hundred dollars across multiple visits before the escalation pattern becomes recognizable. The operation relies on sunk-cost pressure from prior payments combined with manufactured anxiety about an unresolved spiritual threat to health, relationships, or finances.

The entire operation depends on a manufactured problem only the reader can solve. Never engage with unsolicited psychic offers near tourist attractions — a free reading is always bait for a paid upsell, and the 'curse' diagnosis is the mechanism of the upsell, not an observation about reality. Walk away immediately when any reader identifies a spiritual problem only they can fix. If you want a reading for entertainment, book through a verified parlor with a proper business address and multi-year online reviews.

Red Flags

  • Offers a 'free' reading that quickly pivots to identifying a problem only they can fix
  • Mentions curses, dark energy, or bad luck as a setup for more payments
  • Asks increasingly personal questions to use against you psychologically
  • Price escalates with each visit — 'one more session' to complete the cure
  • Operates from an informal setup without any business registration visible

How to Avoid

  • Never engage with unsolicited psychic offers on the street.
  • Understand that 'free' readings are always bait for paid upsells.
  • If you want a reading, book through a verified parlor with online reviews.
  • Never pay for multiple sessions to 'remove a curse' — this is a classic con.
  • Walk away immediately if they claim to have identified a spiritual problem.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Local Police Department station. Call 911. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at usa.gov/crimes.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Visit the nearest US Passport Agency. For international visitors, contact your country's consulate or embassy directly. US State Department emergency line: +1-888-407-4747 (from US) or +1-202-501-4444 (international).

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
