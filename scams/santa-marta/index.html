The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Taxi Bill-Switch and Counterfeit Change
⚠️ High
📍 Taxis throughout Santa Marta, especially outside hotels in El Rodadero, the Centro Histórico, and near Parque de los Novios at night

In Santa Marta taxis, drivers palm your high-denomination bill and claim you handed a much smaller one, or swap your genuine note for a counterfeit while pretending there is no change — a sleight-of-hand that costs you 10,000–50,000 pesos per ride.

Taxis without meters are the norm throughout Santa Marta, from hotel queues in El Rodadero to the Centro Histórico and Parque de los Novios runs at night. Fares typically run 8,000–15,000 pesos for local trips, and most drivers prefer large notes because they can claim a shortage of change.

You hand the driver a 20,000-peso note. He turns briefly toward the dashboard, then holds up a 2,000-peso note, insisting that is all you gave him and the larger bill never existed. In the counterfeit variant, he returns your 50,000-peso note saying it is bad or that he lacks change — but the note he hands back is a fake he had ready. You discover the switch the next day when a shop refuses the bill.

The break in eye contact when the driver turns away is the tell — at that moment the swap happens. Carry exact fare in small bills and state the denomination aloud as you hand it over, so the exchange is witnessed and undeniable. For rides between El Rodadero and the Centro Histórico, InDriver, Didi, and Uber handle payment digitally and remove the cash-handling risk entirely.

Red Flags

  • The driver briefly turns away or distracts you after you hand over your payment
  • The driver claims you gave a much smaller bill than you actually handed over
  • Your change includes old, worn bills that are difficult to verify as genuine
  • The driver returns your large bill claiming it is 'bad' or there is no change
  • The driver becomes immediately agitated or aggressive when questioned about the fare or change

How to Avoid

  • Carry small bills (5,000 and 10,000 peso notes) and pay with exact change whenever possible.
  • State the denomination out loud as you hand it to the driver: 'Here is twenty thousand pesos.'
  • Agree on the fare before entering the taxi and have the exact amount ready.
  • Use ride-hailing apps like InDriver, Didi, or Uber where payment is handled digitally.
  • Familiarize yourself with Colombian bill denominations and security features before arriving — check returned bills for watermarks.
Scam #2
Fake Police Document Inspection
⚠️ High
📍 Streets around the Centro Histórico, Parque de los Novios, walkways near Taganga, and roads near popular tourist sites

Near Parque de los Novios and the Centro Histórico, men in replica police uniforms stop tourists for a 'counterfeit bill check,' pocketing real cash while appearing to inspect the wallet — a coordinated theft that can cost every note you are carrying.

Santa Marta's tourist corridors attract teams working the fake-police scam, particularly around Parque de los Novios, the cathedral square, and the road to Taganga. Two or three men wearing uniforms that closely replicate the Colombian Policía Nacional approach on foot, flashing laminated badges too quickly to read.

They cite a narcotics spot-check or a new anti-counterfeiting directive, demanding to see your passport and to inspect the bills in your wallet for fakes. While one officer monopolizes your attention with questions and paperwork, the other rifles through the wallet, palming notes. They hand you a stamped receipt and direct you to a police station to recover your money — real police there explain there was no operation and no money to recover.

Real Colombian police conducting a legitimate ID check will not ask to hold your wallet or remove its contents. Keep your wallet in your own hands, call 123 to verify the officer's badge number, and offer to walk with them to the nearest police station — scam teams dissolve the moment you make that offer. Carry only a photocopy of your passport's photo page and leave the original locked at your hotel.

Red Flags

  • Officers in plain clothes or slightly off-looking uniforms approach and flash badges too quickly to read
  • They ask to inspect your wallet or cash — real police in Colombia do not check for counterfeit bills during street stops
  • They approach you on foot or motorcycle in a quiet area rather than from a marked police vehicle
  • They demand cash payment for an 'infraction' without issuing proper official documentation
  • They threaten jail, deportation, or severe consequences if you do not comply immediately

How to Avoid

  • Leave your passport at the hotel safe and carry only a photocopy of the photo page and entry stamps.
  • If approached by supposed police, politely request to see their official photo ID and badge number.
  • Suggest walking to the nearest police station to verify their identity — real officers will agree, scammers will not.
  • Never hand over your wallet — if asked for ID, hold it yourself and let them look at it in your hands.
  • Call 123 (Colombian police emergency) to verify any officer's identity before complying with demands.
Scam #3
Beach Distraction Theft
⚠️ High
📍 Playa El Rodadero, Taganga Beach, Playa Blanca, and beach areas around Parque Nacional Natural Tayrona

At El Rodadero, Taganga, and Tayrona beaches, coordinated teams use vendor distractions or water-side offers to strip unattended bags of phones, wallets, and cameras — a loss that typically runs 300,000–1,500,000 pesos in a single theft.

El Rodadero beach runs shallow and calm, easy to wade far into, and the sand fills with day-trippers who leave belongings on towels within meters of the water. Taganga's small cove and Tayrona's forest-backed beaches have the same pattern — high tourist density, no bag storage, and few bag-watchers.

A coordinated team works the shoreline: one member distracts nearby beachgoers with conversation, an accidentally thrown ball, or a beach-radio setup, while a second passes behind your towel and lifts the most valuable items without stopping. In a water-approach variant, a vendor wades toward you with drinks or sunglasses and keeps your attention on the transaction while a partner on the sand works your bag. The whole operation takes under twenty seconds.

The bag sitting alone on a towel is the target, not the person — remove that target and the team moves on. Take turns swimming with your companion so your belongings are always watched, or bring only a waterproof pouch you can wear into the water and leave everything else at the hotel. At Tayrona, the park entrance road has a locker facility near the main gate where valuables can be stored for a small fee.

Red Flags

  • You are leaving belongings unattended on the beach while swimming or snorkeling
  • A vendor or stranger approaches your belongings while you are in the water or distracted
  • Someone creates an unusual distraction near your stuff — a fight, loud music, a thrown object
  • A beach vendor engages you in prolonged conversation while you have belongings spread on the sand nearby
  • You notice someone walking very close to beach bags and towels belonging to various beachgoers

How to Avoid

  • Never leave valuables unattended on the beach — bring only what you need for the day and leave everything else at the hotel.
  • Use a waterproof phone pouch that you can take into the water with you.
  • Take turns swimming with a travel companion so someone always watches the belongings.
  • Use a portable beach safe or lockbox that you can secure to a fixed object like a beach chair.
  • At Tayrona Park or Taganga, bring only essentials: cash for food, a cheap phone, and sunscreen.

Scam #4
Scopolamine (Devil's Breath) Drink Spiking
⚠️ High
📍 Bars and nightclubs in El Rodadero, late-night establishments in the Centro Histórico, and encounters initiated through dating apps

In El Rodadero bars and nightlife venues in the Centro Histórico, scammers — often an attractive woman acting as lure — spike drinks with scopolamine (burundanga), leaving victims compliant and amnesiac while their hotel room is cleaned of electronics, cash, and cards.

El Rodadero's beach bars and the Centro Histórico's late-night strip are active social scenes with high traveler turnover. Operatives — often an attractive woman who initiates contact — work specific venues, selecting targets who are drinking alone, are visibly foreign, or have been matched on dating apps in advance.

The drink arrives looking and tasting normal, and a single glass is enough. Scopolamine, extracted from the borrachero tree and known locally as burundanga, is colorless, odorless, and renders victims deeply suggestible within twenty minutes. The victim appears functional and walks willingly back to their hotel. When they regain memory, their phone, laptop, wallet, watch, and sometimes passport are gone, and they have no recollection of events — making police reports difficult and recovery nearly impossible.

The spike happens during any unguarded moment — a step to the bathroom, a phone glance, a turn to talk to someone else. Never leave your drink unattended and never accept a drink you did not see poured from a sealed bottle; if you step away, order a new one. Avoid taking back to your room anyone you met that night at a bar, and never meet a new dating-app contact at an unfamiliar venue without sharing your location with someone you trust.

Red Flags

  • A stranger offers to buy you a drink or your drink is left unattended for any period of time
  • A new acquaintance from a dating app is unusually eager to meet at a specific bar or come to your hotel room
  • You feel suddenly dizzy, confused, or excessively compliant after only one or two drinks
  • A woman you met at a bar or on a dating app suggests going back to your hotel very quickly after meeting
  • You notice someone reaching over or near your drink while you are distracted

How to Avoid

  • Never accept drinks from strangers and never leave your drink unattended — get a new one if you stepped away.
  • Avoid bringing strangers to your hotel room, especially people met at bars or through dating apps.
  • Go out with trusted friends and watch out for each other — if someone seems suddenly out of it, get them to safety immediately.
  • Use drink covers or bottle toppers to prevent anything being dropped into your beverage.
  • If you feel suddenly disoriented after a drink, tell a trusted person immediately and get to a safe location.
Scam #5
Overpriced Unofficial Tour Guide
🔶 Medium
📍 Outside the entrance to Parque Nacional Natural Tayrona, Minca coffee farm tours, Lost City (Ciudad Perdida) trek departures, and around Santa Marta's harbor

At Tayrona park entrances, Minca trail heads, and the Lost City trek departure points, unlicensed guides approach with plausible patter and charge 60,000–100,000 pesos for a tour that delivers minimal guiding — two to four times the cost of self-guided entry or a comparable licensed operator.

Tayrona National Park sees several thousand visitors a day at peak season, and the entrance corridor is thick with men who approach arriving vehicles and tuk-tuks offering private guided entry. The same pattern repeats at Minca coffee-farm trails and at the departure point on Calle 22 in Santa Marta city where Lost City treks begin.

The guide quotes a round number — 80,000 or 100,000 pesos — that sounds plausible to a first-time visitor, demands full cash payment before entering, provides minimal trail information inside, and exits early, declaring the tour complete. Self-guided park entry costs 65,000 pesos with the standard foreign surcharge, and the handful of licensed operators for the Lost City trek — Expotur, Wiwa Tour, Turcol, Magic Tour — are the only legally authorized agencies.

Unlicensed guides cannot produce an official SENA tourism-guide credential when asked — that single question ends the approach. Book park access and nature tours through licensed agencies verified on TripAdvisor or the Parques Nacionales official site, and for the Lost City trek confirm your operator is one of the four authorized companies before handing over any money. Research the current entry fee before arriving so you know the baseline.

Red Flags

  • A guide approaches you at a park entrance or tourist site rather than being booked through an office or website
  • They cannot show an official guide license or certification when asked
  • The price is unusually low or high compared to what you researched online for the same tour
  • They demand full payment upfront in cash before the tour begins with no receipt or written agreement
  • Other tourists or park officials do not seem to recognize or interact with the guide

How to Avoid

  • Book tours through licensed agencies with verified reviews on TripAdvisor, Google, or GetYourGuide.
  • For the Lost City trek, only book with one of the few licensed operators (Expotur, Wiwa Tour, Turcol, or Magic Tour).
  • Ask for official guide credentials and verify them with the tourism office if in doubt.
  • Get a written agreement including the itinerary, inclusions, and refund policy before paying.
  • Research the standard price for any tour before arriving so you can spot both overcharging and suspiciously low offers.
Scam #6
Bird Poop and Bracelet Distraction Theft
🔶 Medium
📍 Centro Histórico around the cathedral, Parque de los Novios, beachfront promenades, and busy pedestrian streets near Rodadero

In the Centro Histórico and around Parque de los Novios, coordinated teams squirt a fake stain on you and help clean it while lifting your phone and wallet, or tie a bracelet on your wrist as a free gift then demand 50,000–100,000 pesos while accomplices work your pockets.

The Centro Histórico's cathedral square and Parque de los Novios are popular midday walking zones for tourists and the primary territory for distraction-theft teams. Teams of three to four people divide roles: a squirter or bracelet-tier, a helpful cleaner who touches your bag, and a pocket-dipper working from behind.

In the poop variant, someone taps your shoulder and points to a white paste on your jacket — sometimes applied from behind moments earlier. A second person arrives immediately with tissues, leans in close to blot the stain, and slides a hand into your bag or pocket. In the bracelet variant, a person ties a colorful woven bracelet on your wrist before you can refuse — a gift from Colombia — then demands 50,000 pesos. While you argue, the third team member is already behind you at your bag.

The moment someone touches your clothing unexpectedly, your attention is the real target, not the stain. Step away from any unexpected contact, keep moving, and check your pockets only once you are well clear of the interaction — inspecting on the spot tells the team which pocket the phone is in. In the bracelet approach, decline loudly and immediately before the tie is completed; once it is on, accept the small loss rather than standing and arguing while your bag is worked.

Red Flags

  • A stranger points out a stain on your clothing that appeared from nowhere — bird poop, mustard, or a white substance
  • Someone immediately materializes with napkins or tissues, touching your clothing and bag
  • A person ties a bracelet or gift on your wrist without asking, then demands payment
  • You feel people positioning themselves unusually close to you during the interaction
  • The 'helpful' people seem coordinated and overly attentive to each other's positions

How to Avoid

  • If someone points out a stain, do not stop — walk away to a safe area before inspecting your clothing.
  • Do not accept 'free' bracelets, flowers, or gifts from strangers — decline firmly and keep walking.
  • Wear your bag on your front in the Centro Histórico and keep your phone in a zipped inner pocket.
  • Travel with a companion when possible so someone can watch your back during unexpected interactions.
  • Be especially alert near the cathedral and Parque de los Novios where these teams operate most actively.
Scam #7
Attempted Knifepoint Robbery Near Historic Center
⚠️ High
📍 Side streets off the Centro Histórico at night, dark streets near Taganga, and poorly lit areas between bars and hotels

On side streets off Santa Marta's Centro Histórico and near Taganga after dark, robbers working in pairs demand phones, wallets, and watches at knifepoint — a confrontation that is over in under a minute and, if resisted, escalates to violence.

Santa Marta's main tourist corridors — Parque de los Novios square, El Rodadero beachfront, and Carrera 1 — are well-patrolled and relatively safe at night. One block off these corridors, however, the lighting drops, foot traffic thins, and the risk profile changes sharply. The blocks between the tourist zone and budget hotel clusters, and the dark stretches connecting Taganga's village center to its outskirts, are the primary territory for street robbery.

A typical approach: two men emerge from a doorway or parked vehicle on a quiet side street. One positions himself behind you, one in front, and a knife appears. The demand is for your phone, wallet, and any visible watch or jewelry. The encounter lasts under sixty seconds. If you pause, argue, or appear to be signaling for help, the threat escalates. Victims who comply leave the scene uninjured; those who resist risk being struck or cut.

The side street is the risk, and the solution is not to take it. Use Uber, InDriver, or a hotel-arranged taxi for any journey after dark, even for a distance that would take three minutes on foot — the fare is almost always under 8,000 pesos and removes the exposure entirely. Before you go out, ask hotel staff which specific streets are safe at night; the answer is usually to stay on the main strip.

Red Flags

  • You are walking on a poorly lit side street outside the main tourist zone after dark
  • The street has no other pedestrians and is not well-trafficked
  • Groups of young men are loitering in doorways, alleys, or near parked cars on your route
  • You are visibly carrying expensive items — camera, phone, jewelry, or designer clothing
  • You are walking between bars and hotels through unfamiliar streets rather than on main roads

How to Avoid

  • Never walk on side streets in Santa Marta after dark — use Uber, InDriver, or a hotel-arranged taxi even for short distances.
  • Stick to well-lit, well-trafficked streets like Carrera 1 and the Parque de los Novios area when out at night.
  • Do not wear expensive jewelry, watches, or visible electronics when walking around the city.
  • If confronted, hand over your belongings without resistance — nothing is worth your safety.
  • Ask your hotel which specific streets are safe to walk at night and which to avoid.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Colombian National Police (Policía Nacional) station. Call 123 (Emergency) or 112. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at policia.gov.co.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The US Embassy in Bogotá is at Calle 24 Bis No. 48-50, Bogotá. For emergencies: +57 1-275-2000.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
