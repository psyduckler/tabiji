The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Taxi Bill-Switch and Counterfeit Change
⚠️ High
📍 Taxis throughout Santa Marta, especially outside hotels in El Rodadero, the Centro Histórico, and near Parque de los Novios at night

Taxi drivers in Santa Marta palm your large-denomination note and claim you handed them a smaller bill, or return your note claiming it is counterfeit while keeping the real one — losses of 20,000 to 50,000 pesos per ride, with no recourse once you leave the vehicle.

Unofficial taxis and some licensed drivers outside El Rodadero hotels and near Parque de los Novios use a sleight-of-hand perfected across Colombian coastal cities. You agree on a fare — say, 10,000 pesos — and hand the driver a 20,000-peso note. He glances at it, turns briefly toward the window, and then presents a 2,000-peso note with apparent confusion, insisting that is what you gave him.

In the counterfeit variant, you hand over a 50,000-peso note and the driver returns it, claiming it is fake or that he has no change. The note he hands back looks identical but is a forgery — you discover the problem the next day when a shop refuses it. He has already pocketed your genuine note. Both techniques exploit the moment of distraction created by the handoff itself.

Announcing the denomination out loud as you hand it over — "Here is twenty thousand pesos" — removes the driver's ability to claim otherwise; witnesses in the car and the verbalized amount make the switch far harder to sustain. The safest approach is to use InDriver, Didi, or Uber where payment is digital and the dispute never arises, but if you take an unofficial taxi, carry exact fare in small bills and state each note's value clearly.

Red Flags

  • The driver briefly turns away or distracts you after you hand over your payment
  • The driver claims you gave a much smaller bill than you actually handed over
  • Your change includes old, worn bills that are difficult to verify as genuine
  • The driver returns your large bill claiming it is 'bad' or there is no change
  • The driver becomes immediately agitated or aggressive when questioned about the fare or change

How to Avoid

  • Carry small bills (5,000 and 10,000 peso notes) and pay with exact change whenever possible.
  • State the denomination out loud as you hand it to the driver: 'Here is twenty thousand pesos.'
  • Agree on the fare before entering the taxi and have the exact amount ready.
  • Use ride-hailing apps like InDriver, Didi, or Uber where payment is handled digitally.
  • Familiarize yourself with Colombian bill denominations and security features before arriving — check returned bills for watermarks.
Scam #2
Fake Police Document Inspection
⚠️ High
📍 Streets around the Centro Histórico, Parque de los Novios, walkways near Taganga, and roads near popular tourist sites

Men in police-style uniforms approach tourists near Parque de los Novios claiming to check wallets for counterfeit bills — one handles your passport while the other pockets cash, with losses of tens of thousands of pesos and no official record of the stop.

Near the Centro Histórico and on walking routes between Parque de los Novios and the waterfront, pairs of men in uniforms that closely resemble Colombian National Police attire approach foreign tourists on foot. They flash badges quickly — too fast to read the name or number — and cite a counterfeit-bill operation in the area as grounds for an inspection of your documents and cash.

One officer takes your passport to examine it while the other handles your wallet, pulling out bills and palming cash while appearing to check denominations. They produce a printed "receipt" and direct you to the nearest station to recover anything held — at the real station, officers confirm no counterfeit sweep was underway and that the stop never happened in their system.

Real Colombian police do not stop pedestrians to inspect wallets for counterfeit bills — the procedure does not exist. If anyone in uniform demands to see your wallet on the street, offer instead to walk together to the nearest marked police station; any legitimate officer will agree without hesitation, and the impersonators will disengage immediately rather than risk exposure at a real checkpoint.

Red Flags

  • Officers in plain clothes or slightly off-looking uniforms approach and flash badges too quickly to read
  • They ask to inspect your wallet or cash — real police in Colombia do not check for counterfeit bills during street stops
  • They approach you on foot or motorcycle in a quiet area rather than from a marked police vehicle
  • They demand cash payment for an 'infraction' without issuing proper official documentation
  • They threaten jail, deportation, or severe consequences if you do not comply immediately

How to Avoid

  • Leave your passport at the hotel safe and carry only a photocopy of the photo page and entry stamps.
  • If approached by supposed police, politely request to see their official photo ID and badge number.
  • Suggest walking to the nearest police station to verify their identity — real officers will agree, scammers will not.
  • Never hand over your wallet — if asked for ID, hold it yourself and let them look at it in your hands.
  • Call 123 (Colombian police emergency) to verify any officer's identity before complying with demands.
Scam #3
Beach Distraction Theft
⚠️ High
📍 Playa El Rodadero, Taganga Beach, Playa Blanca, and beach areas around Parque Nacional Natural Tayrona

Two-person teams at Playa El Rodadero and Tayrona beaches use beach vendors and staged distractions to rifle through unattended bags while tourists swim — phones, wallets, and cameras gone in under a minute.

Playa El Rodadero draws large crowds of domestic and international tourists, and organized theft teams work the waterline regularly. You set your bag on the sand and wade in — a vendor passes close to your belongings, and moments later an accomplice wades toward you in the water with sunglasses or cold drinks for sale, drawing your attention away from the shore.

While the waterside vendor has your focus, the first person on shore goes quickly through your bag, takes your phone, wallet, and camera, and continues walking as if browsing the beach normally. At Tayrona, the same pattern runs along the entry trails — bags left at tree roots or entry points are lifted while owners are rounding the next bend. The whole operation takes under a minute and leaves no obvious moment of theft to report.

Taking turns swimming in pairs — one person always staying with the bags — eliminates nearly all exposure. Bring only a cheap waterproof phone, small cash for the day, and sunscreen to the beach, and lock passports, cards, and hotel keys in the room safe before leaving; there is nothing to steal if valuables never reach the sand.

Red Flags

  • You are leaving belongings unattended on the beach while swimming or snorkeling
  • A vendor or stranger approaches your belongings while you are in the water or distracted
  • Someone creates an unusual distraction near your stuff — a fight, loud music, a thrown object
  • A beach vendor engages you in prolonged conversation while you have belongings spread on the sand nearby
  • You notice someone walking very close to beach bags and towels belonging to various beachgoers

How to Avoid

  • Never leave valuables unattended on the beach — bring only what you need for the day and leave everything else at the hotel.
  • Use a waterproof phone pouch that you can take into the water with you.
  • Take turns swimming with a travel companion so someone always watches the belongings.
  • Use a portable beach safe or lockbox that you can secure to a fixed object like a beach chair.
  • At Tayrona Park or Taganga, bring only essentials: cash for food, a cheap phone, and sunscreen.

Scam #4
Scopolamine (Devil's Breath) Drink Spiking
⚠️ High
📍 Bars and nightclubs in El Rodadero, late-night establishments in the Centro Histórico, and encounters initiated through dating apps

Attractive strangers at El Rodadero bars and dating-app contacts in Santa Marta dose victims' drinks with scopolamine (burundanga), leaving them compliant and amnesiac while wallets, phones, watches, and laptops are cleaned out — victims wake hours later with no memory of what happened.

El Rodadero's bar strip and Santa Marta's downtown nightlife attract scopolamine-dosing operations that target solo tourists and dating-app visitors. An attractive stranger starts a conversation at the bar, offers to buy a round, and the drink arrives looking and tasting completely normal. Within twenty to thirty minutes, the victim feels unusually relaxed, then compliant, then loses the ability to form new memories.

Scopolamine — called burundanga locally — suppresses memory formation and makes victims highly suggestible. You may walk to your own hotel, open the door, and hand over cash, your phone, your watch, and your laptop with no awareness that anything is wrong. You wake hours later with all your valuables gone and no recollection of the events. The drug is colorless and odorless, undetectable in a drink, and can also be transferred on paper or blown as powder.

Never accept a drink you did not personally collect from the bar counter or pour yourself, and never leave your glass unattended even for a moment — if you feel suddenly dizzy or unusually sedated after a single drink, tell a trusted companion immediately and move to a busy, public space; the symptoms themselves are the only warning the drug gives.

Red Flags

  • A stranger offers to buy you a drink or your drink is left unattended for any period of time
  • A new acquaintance from a dating app is unusually eager to meet at a specific bar or come to your hotel room
  • You feel suddenly dizzy, confused, or excessively compliant after only one or two drinks
  • A woman you met at a bar or on a dating app suggests going back to your hotel very quickly after meeting
  • You notice someone reaching over or near your drink while you are distracted

How to Avoid

  • Never accept drinks from strangers and never leave your drink unattended — get a new one if you stepped away.
  • Avoid bringing strangers to your hotel room, especially people met at bars or through dating apps.
  • Go out with trusted friends and watch out for each other — if someone seems suddenly out of it, get them to safety immediately.
  • Use drink covers or bottle toppers to prevent anything being dropped into your beverage.
  • If you feel suddenly disoriented after a drink, tell a trusted person immediately and get to a safe location.
Scam #5
Overpriced Unofficial Tour Guide
🔶 Medium
📍 Outside the entrance to Parque Nacional Natural Tayrona, Minca coffee farm tours, Lost City (Ciudad Perdida) trek departures, and around Santa Marta's harbor

Unlicensed men posing as guides at Tayrona park entrances and Ciudad Perdida trek departures charge 80,000 pesos for tours worth a fraction of that — delivering rushed, uninformative experiences while self-guided entry costs 20,000 pesos and legitimate guides cost 50,000–60,000 through official channels.

Parque Nacional Natural Tayrona is one of the most visited sites in northern Colombia, and its entrance draws unlicensed touts who position themselves just outside the ticket office. A confident man approaches as you arrive, offering a private guided tour for 80,000 pesos — a price that seems reasonable for a full-day experience in a national park. He accepts cash upfront, and the tour begins.

Once inside, the guide provides almost no information, rushes through the trails without stopping at viewpoints, and spends most of the walk on his phone. He declares the tour finished at the first beach, well before the park's most scenic areas. The same dynamic plays out on Ciudad Perdida treks — only four operators are legally authorized to run the multi-day route to the Lost City, and any guide soliciting at the trailhead is working outside that system, typically delivering an under-resourced experience with no safety net if something goes wrong on the trail.

Booking through verified agencies on TripAdvisor or GetYourGuide before you reach the park puts you in contact with accountable operators. For the Ciudad Perdida trek specifically, only Expotur, Wiwa Tour, Turcol, and Magic Tour hold government authorization — any guide approaching you at the departure point operates outside that list, regardless of how official their pitch or gear appears.

Red Flags

  • A guide approaches you at a park entrance or tourist site rather than being booked through an office or website
  • They cannot show an official guide license or certification when asked
  • The price is unusually low or high compared to what you researched online for the same tour
  • They demand full payment upfront in cash before the tour begins with no receipt or written agreement
  • Other tourists or park officials do not seem to recognize or interact with the guide

How to Avoid

  • Book tours through licensed agencies with verified reviews on TripAdvisor, Google, or GetYourGuide.
  • For the Lost City trek, only book with one of the few licensed operators (Expotur, Wiwa Tour, Turcol, or Magic Tour).
  • Ask for official guide credentials and verify them with the tourism office if in doubt.
  • Get a written agreement including the itinerary, inclusions, and refund policy before paying.
  • Research the standard price for any tour before arriving so you can spot both overcharging and suspiciously low offers.
Scam #6
Bird Poop and Bracelet Distraction Theft
🔶 Medium
📍 Centro Histórico around the cathedral, Parque de los Novios, beachfront promenades, and busy pedestrian streets near Rodadero

Teams of two to four people working Santa Marta's Centro Histórico squirt a white liquid on tourists' clothing, then use a "helpful" napkin-holder to block the bag while a third person picks pockets — or tie bracelets on wrists and demand 50,000–100,000 pesos while accomplices move on nearby valuables.

Near the cathedral and along the pedestrian routes around Parque de los Novios, organized teams of two to four people run distraction theft with tight coordination. Someone taps your shoulder and points urgently to a white stain on your jacket — bird poop, mustard, or a white paste. The substance appeared from nowhere; the "spotter" already knows where it came from because a teammate applied it moments ago.

Within seconds, a second friendly stranger materializes with napkins, leaning in to help clean the mess and placing hands near your bag. A third person behind you slips into your pocket or unzips the bag while your attention is on the stain. In the bracelet variant, someone ties a colorful woven band on your wrist without asking, calls it a free gift, and then demands 50,000–100,000 pesos. The argument over payment pins your attention forward while accomplices position themselves on your valuables from behind.

The moment someone points out a stain on your clothing in the street, keep walking without looking down. Move to the nearest open shopfront or busy café entrance to check yourself privately — the instant you stop on the pavement, the rest of the team moves into position, and the few extra meters of walking are the only window you have to avoid them.

Red Flags

  • A stranger points out a stain on your clothing that appeared from nowhere — bird poop, mustard, or a white substance
  • Someone immediately materializes with napkins or tissues, touching your clothing and bag
  • A person ties a bracelet or gift on your wrist without asking, then demands payment
  • You feel people positioning themselves unusually close to you during the interaction
  • The 'helpful' people seem coordinated and overly attentive to each other's positions

How to Avoid

  • If someone points out a stain, do not stop — walk away to a safe area before inspecting your clothing.
  • Do not accept 'free' bracelets, flowers, or gifts from strangers — decline firmly and keep walking.
  • Wear your bag on your front in the Centro Histórico and keep your phone in a zipped inner pocket.
  • Travel with a companion when possible so someone can watch your back during unexpected interactions.
  • Be especially alert near the cathedral and Parque de los Novios where these teams operate most actively.
Scam #7
Attempted Knifepoint Robbery Near Historic Center
⚠️ High
📍 Side streets off the Centro Histórico at night, dark streets near Taganga, and poorly lit areas between bars and hotels

After dark on side streets off Santa Marta's Centro Histórico and near Taganga, pairs of men emerge from doorways demanding phones, wallets, and watches at knifepoint — the robbery takes under a minute and resistance leads to immediate physical escalation.

Santa Marta's well-lit main tourist corridors — El Rodadero beachfront, the circuit around Parque de los Novios, the main road to Taganga — are relatively safe with regular foot traffic and police presence. The risk is concentrated in the transitional zones: the side streets between those corridors and the hotels, the narrow lanes behind the cathedral, and the unlit back routes through Taganga after midnight. Tourists taking shortcuts after dinner are the most common targets.

Two men step out from a doorway or parked car, and within seconds one has a knife visible. They give a single demand — phone, wallet, watch — and mean it. The robbery is over in under a minute. Hesitation or verbal pushback leads to physical force; the risk is far higher than the value of whatever is in your pocket. Taganga's guesthouse strip carries the same threat after 11 pm, particularly for solo travelers walking between bars without a plan for the return route.

Staying on the lit main corridors after dark removes almost all exposure. Taking a taxi or ride-hailing app for any route that would cross an unlit side street after 9 pm is the single rule that prevents most knifepoint robberies in Santa Marta — the fare is always cheaper than replacing your phone, and the detour is never more than five minutes.

Red Flags

  • You are walking on a poorly lit side street outside the main tourist zone after dark
  • The street has no other pedestrians and is not well-trafficked
  • Groups of young men are loitering in doorways, alleys, or near parked cars on your route
  • You are visibly carrying expensive items — camera, phone, jewelry, or designer clothing
  • You are walking between bars and hotels through unfamiliar streets rather than on main roads

How to Avoid

  • Never walk on side streets in Santa Marta after dark — use Uber, InDriver, or a hotel-arranged taxi even for short distances.
  • Stick to well-lit, well-trafficked streets like Carrera 1 and the Parque de los Novios area when out at night.
  • Do not wear expensive jewelry, watches, or visible electronics when walking around the city.
  • If confronted, hand over your belongings without resistance — nothing is worth your safety.
  • Ask your hotel which specific streets are safe to walk at night and which to avoid.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Colombian National Police (Policía Nacional) station. Call 123 (Emergency) or 112. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at policia.gov.co.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The US Embassy in Bogotá is at Calle 24 Bis No. 48-50, Bogotá. For emergencies: +57 1-275-2000.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
