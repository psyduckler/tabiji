The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Taxi Bill-Switch and Counterfeit Change
⚠️ High
📍 Taxis throughout Santa Marta, especially outside hotels in El Rodadero, the Centro Histórico, and near Parque de los Novios at night

Taxi drivers in Santa Marta — especially on El Rodadero and Centro Histórico routes — palm your large-denomination note and claim you handed them a smaller one, or swap your genuine 50,000-peso bill for a counterfeit, costing victims 20,000–50,000 pesos before they leave the car.

Taxis throughout Santa Marta run without enforced meters, so fares are negotiated before boarding and settled in cash at the end. The vulnerable moment is payment: you hand the driver a 20,000-peso note for a 10,000-peso fare and wait for change, or you pass over a 50,000-peso note for a longer run. The driver has practiced this sleight-of-hand on tourists and knows exactly how to hold your attention elsewhere for the two seconds the switch requires.

In the bill-switch variant the driver briefly turns away, palms your 20,000-peso note, and then produces a 2,000-peso note — insisting that is what you handed him. By the time you object, traffic is moving or the street is busy, and proving what denomination you gave is nearly impossible. In the counterfeit variant the driver accepts your 50,000-peso note, complains it is "malo" or that he has no change, and returns what looks like your same bill — but it is a fake. You discover the forgery the next day when a shopkeeper refuses it.

Pay with exact change and say the denomination aloud as you hand it over — announcing "twenty thousand" before the driver's hand closes around the note makes a later denial implausible. Use InDriver or Didi where payment is digital and no cash changes hands; for cash rides, keep a pocket of 10,000-peso notes so you never need to hand over anything larger than the agreed fare.

Red Flags

  • The driver briefly turns away or distracts you after you hand over your payment
  • The driver claims you gave a much smaller bill than you actually handed over
  • Your change includes old, worn bills that are difficult to verify as genuine
  • The driver returns your large bill claiming it is 'bad' or there is no change
  • The driver becomes immediately agitated or aggressive when questioned about the fare or change

How to Avoid

  • Carry small bills (5,000 and 10,000 peso notes) and pay with exact change whenever possible.
  • State the denomination out loud as you hand it to the driver: 'Here is twenty thousand pesos.'
  • Agree on the fare before entering the taxi and have the exact amount ready.
  • Use ride-hailing apps like InDriver, Didi, or Uber where payment is handled digitally.
  • Familiarize yourself with Colombian bill denominations and security features before arriving — check returned bills for watermarks.
Scam #2
Fake Police Document Inspection
⚠️ High
📍 Streets around the Centro Histórico, Parque de los Novios, walkways near Taganga, and roads near popular tourist sites

Men in quasi-police uniforms stop tourists near Parque de los Novios and the Centro Histórico, claiming to check wallets for counterfeit bills, then pocket cash during the "inspection" — victims lose 50,000–300,000 pesos before realizing the officers were fake.

Two or three men approach on foot — near a park bench, a popular café, or a pedestrian street. Their uniforms are close but not exact: slightly wrong colors, mismatched patches, no clearly visible badge number. They speak with authority, flash laminated IDs too quickly to read, and move into the stop without giving you a moment to process whether this is official.

They claim to be running a routine check for counterfeit currency — plausible enough in Colombia. One officer holds your passport and asks questions while a second spreads your bills across a clipboard to "inspect" them. What looks like an official count is a skilled lift: bills fold into the notepad, slip under the clipboard, or are simply palmed. They issue a receipt and direct you to "the station" to collect any confiscated funds. No station exists. Nothing is ever returned.

Real Colombian police do not stop tourists on the street to inspect wallets for counterfeit bills — that procedure does not exist, and any officer who describes it is running a scam. Say clearly "I will call 123 to verify your ID" and reach for your phone; legitimate officers will not object, but scammers back off immediately. Never hand over your wallet — hold your ID yourself and let them look at it while it stays in your hand.

Red Flags

  • Officers in plain clothes or slightly off-looking uniforms approach and flash badges too quickly to read
  • They ask to inspect your wallet or cash — real police in Colombia do not check for counterfeit bills during street stops
  • They approach you on foot or motorcycle in a quiet area rather than from a marked police vehicle
  • They demand cash payment for an 'infraction' without issuing proper official documentation
  • They threaten jail, deportation, or severe consequences if you do not comply immediately

How to Avoid

  • Leave your passport at the hotel safe and carry only a photocopy of the photo page and entry stamps.
  • If approached by supposed police, politely request to see their official photo ID and badge number.
  • Suggest walking to the nearest police station to verify their identity — real officers will agree, scammers will not.
  • Never hand over your wallet — if asked for ID, hold it yourself and let them look at it in your hands.
  • Call 123 (Colombian police emergency) to verify any officer's identity before complying with demands.
Scam #3
Beach Distraction Theft
⚠️ High
📍 Playa El Rodadero, Taganga Beach, Playa Blanca, and beach areas around Parque Nacional Natural Tayrona

Two-person teams at Playa El Rodadero and Taganga target tourists who swim offshore: one creates a close-range distraction near unattended bags while a partner rifles through them, taking phones, wallets, and cameras in under two minutes.

The setup requires only a gap in your attention. You go into the water and leave a bag on the sand — even a ten-minute swim is long enough. The team has already identified your bag from the shoreline. One person positions near your belongings while you are in the water; another either approaches nearby beachgoers to chat, kicks a ball toward your things, or strikes up a conversation with you from the waterline itself.

While you are focused on the distraction — watching the ball land, listening to the person talking — the first person crouches at your bag for ten seconds and removes whatever is most accessible: phone on top of the towel, wallet in the side pocket, camera in the mesh pocket. When you return, the bag looks undisturbed and neither person is nearby. An alternate version involves someone approaching you in the water to sell drinks or sunscreen while a partner hits your things on shore.

Leave your phone, cards, and passport at the hotel and bring only the day's cash in a cheap waterproof pouch you take into the water — if there is nothing worth taking, the team moves on to the next target. If you are traveling with someone, take turns swimming so one person always watches the bags. At Tayrona and Taganga, treat your beach bag like an unsupervised locker: bring only what you are willing to lose.

Red Flags

  • You are leaving belongings unattended on the beach while swimming or snorkeling
  • A vendor or stranger approaches your belongings while you are in the water or distracted
  • Someone creates an unusual distraction near your stuff — a fight, loud music, a thrown object
  • A beach vendor engages you in prolonged conversation while you have belongings spread on the sand nearby
  • You notice someone walking very close to beach bags and towels belonging to various beachgoers

How to Avoid

  • Never leave valuables unattended on the beach — bring only what you need for the day and leave everything else at the hotel.
  • Use a waterproof phone pouch that you can take into the water with you.
  • Take turns swimming with a travel companion so someone always watches the belongings.
  • Use a portable beach safe or lockbox that you can secure to a fixed object like a beach chair.
  • At Tayrona Park or Taganga, bring only essentials: cash for food, a cheap phone, and sunscreen.

Scam #4
Scopolamine (Devil's Breath) Drink Spiking
⚠️ High
📍 Bars and nightclubs in El Rodadero, late-night establishments in the Centro Histórico, and encounters initiated through dating apps

Scammers at El Rodadero and Centro Histórico bars — often women posing as new acquaintances — dose tourists' drinks with scopolamine (burundanga), a colorless, odorless drug that causes deep compliance and wipes memory, then rob hotel rooms of phones, cash, and laptops while the victim is incapacitated.

The encounter feels like a good night: a friendly, attractive woman approaches at a bar, conversation flows easily, and she offers to buy a round. The setting is normal — a crowded El Rodadero terrace, a bar near the Centro, or a meeting arranged through a dating app earlier that day. Nothing about the person or the drink appears suspicious.

Scopolamine, known locally as burundanga, is colorless, odorless, and dissolves without taste. It does not cause the drowsiness you would notice — instead it produces a compliance state where you follow instructions without resistance and form no memories. Within thirty to forty minutes you will agree to things, hand over PINs, open doors, and help carry valuables out of your own room. The next morning you remember nothing from a multi-hour window. Phone, wallet, watch, laptop, and passport are gone.

Never accept a drink you did not personally watch being poured, and get a fresh drink if you stepped away from your glass for any reason — there is no safe way to check a doctored drink at the bar. Bringing strangers back to your hotel room is the single highest-risk step in this chain; avoid it entirely regardless of how the night is going. Colombia estimates scopolamine affects 50,000 people per year, and Santa Marta's nightlife zone is an active target area.

Red Flags

  • A stranger offers to buy you a drink or your drink is left unattended for any period of time
  • A new acquaintance from a dating app is unusually eager to meet at a specific bar or come to your hotel room
  • You feel suddenly dizzy, confused, or excessively compliant after only one or two drinks
  • A woman you met at a bar or on a dating app suggests going back to your hotel very quickly after meeting
  • You notice someone reaching over or near your drink while you are distracted

How to Avoid

  • Never accept drinks from strangers and never leave your drink unattended — get a new one if you stepped away.
  • Avoid bringing strangers to your hotel room, especially people met at bars or through dating apps.
  • Go out with trusted friends and watch out for each other — if someone seems suddenly out of it, get them to safety immediately.
  • Use drink covers or bottle toppers to prevent anything being dropped into your beverage.
  • If you feel suddenly disoriented after a drink, tell a trusted person immediately and get to a safe location.
Scam #5
Overpriced Unofficial Tour Guide
🔶 Medium
📍 Outside the entrance to Parque Nacional Natural Tayrona, Minca coffee farm tours, Lost City (Ciudad Perdida) trek departures, and around Santa Marta's harbor

Unlicensed touts at the entrance to Tayrona National Park and Ciudad Perdida trek departure points intercept tourists before the ticket window, charging 80,000–150,000 pesos for tours that official operators run for 20,000–60,000 pesos, then deliver a rushed, near-silent walk once you are inside.

You arrive at the entrance to Tayrona National Park or a Ciudad Perdida staging area and a confident man approaches before you reach the ticket office. He quotes a private guided tour at a rate that sounds plausible — 80,000 pesos — and is fluent enough about trail times and wildlife to seem credible. The price is not outrageous and he appears official, so you pay before comparing it against the posted rates at the gate.

Once inside the park, the guide's knowledge becomes thin. He rushes through trail sections, spends long stretches on his phone, and skips context about flora, fauna, and site history. When you reach the beaches, he announces the tour is over. You later discover that self-guided entry costs only 20,000 pesos and that licensed guides charge 50,000–60,000 pesos through official channels. For Ciudad Perdida, only four licensed agencies — Expotur, Wiwa Tour, Turcol, and Magic Tour — are authorized to run the route; anyone outside those companies cannot provide the registered campsites and safety infrastructure the multi-day trek requires.

Book through a licensed agency with verified TripAdvisor or GetYourGuide reviews before you leave your hotel — a confirmed booking with a named company makes it impossible for street touts to intercept you at the gate. Ask any guide who approaches to show their official license, then verify the entry-fee structure at the ticket window before any money changes hands. Research the standard price range for any tour in advance so you can identify both overcharging and suspiciously low offers.

Red Flags

  • A guide approaches you at a park entrance or tourist site rather than being booked through an office or website
  • They cannot show an official guide license or certification when asked
  • The price is unusually low or high compared to what you researched online for the same tour
  • They demand full payment upfront in cash before the tour begins with no receipt or written agreement
  • Other tourists or park officials do not seem to recognize or interact with the guide

How to Avoid

  • Book tours through licensed agencies with verified reviews on TripAdvisor, Google, or GetYourGuide.
  • For the Lost City trek, only book with one of the few licensed operators (Expotur, Wiwa Tour, Turcol, or Magic Tour).
  • Ask for official guide credentials and verify them with the tourism office if in doubt.
  • Get a written agreement including the itinerary, inclusions, and refund policy before paying.
  • Research the standard price for any tour before arriving so you can spot both overcharging and suspiciously low offers.
Scam #6
Bird Poop and Bracelet Distraction Theft
🔶 Medium
📍 Centro Histórico around the cathedral, Parque de los Novios, beachfront promenades, and busy pedestrian streets near Rodadero

Two- to four-person teams in the Centro Histórico use a fake bird-poop "accident" or a forcibly tied friendship bracelet to create close-contact distraction, then pickpocket phones and wallets while a tourist's attention is on the stain or the payment argument.

You are walking near the cathedral or down a pedestrian street when someone taps your shoulder and points urgently at a white, yogurt-like blob on the back of your jacket. Almost immediately a second person materializes with napkins and begins dabbing sympathetically. The exchange feels like a helpful bystander resolving a minor mishap. In the bracelet version, someone steps in front of you and swiftly ties a colorful thread bracelet around your wrist, calling it a "free gift" before you can pull back.

While the first person dabs at your jacket and the second commiserates about the mess, a third person behind you unzips your day bag or reaches into the back pocket of your shorts. The entire operation takes fifteen to twenty seconds and is rehearsed. In the bracelet version, while you argue about whether you requested the bracelet and whether you owe 50,000 or 100,000 pesos, the person arguing with you positions a companion near your bag or pocket. The stain or the bracelet is a prop — the theft is the point.

If anyone touches your clothing or body unexpectedly, step back immediately and put your hand on your bag — the unexpected touch is the alert, not the reason given for it. Do not stop to inspect the "stain" or debate the bracelet on the street; walk away into a shop or café and check your clothing once your back is against a wall. In the Centro Histórico and around Parque de los Novios, wear your bag across your front and keep your phone in a zipped inner pocket rather than a back pocket.

Red Flags

  • A stranger points out a stain on your clothing that appeared from nowhere — bird poop, mustard, or a white substance
  • Someone immediately materializes with napkins or tissues, touching your clothing and bag
  • A person ties a bracelet or gift on your wrist without asking, then demands payment
  • You feel people positioning themselves unusually close to you during the interaction
  • The 'helpful' people seem coordinated and overly attentive to each other's positions

How to Avoid

  • If someone points out a stain, do not stop — walk away to a safe area before inspecting your clothing.
  • Do not accept 'free' bracelets, flowers, or gifts from strangers — decline firmly and keep walking.
  • Wear your bag on your front in the Centro Histórico and keep your phone in a zipped inner pocket.
  • Travel with a companion when possible so someone can watch your back during unexpected interactions.
  • Be especially alert near the cathedral and Parque de los Novios where these teams operate most actively.
Scam #7
Attempted Knifepoint Robbery Near Historic Center
⚠️ High
📍 Side streets off the Centro Histórico at night, dark streets near Taganga, and poorly lit areas between bars and hotels

Armed robbers — typically two young men — target tourists on dark side streets near the Centro Histórico and Taganga after dark, completing phone-wallet-watch robberies in under sixty seconds and escalating threat level quickly if victims hesitate.

After dinner near Parque de los Novios the route back through the well-lit main plaza feels fine — but you take a side-street shortcut to shave three minutes off the walk. The street looks quiet and nothing initially seems wrong. At night in Santa Marta, the difference between safe corridors and risky ones is a single block and a missing streetlight.

Two young men step out of a recessed doorway or from behind a parked car. One brandishes a knife. The demand is fast and specific: phone, wallet, watch. There is no negotiation or buildup. The robbery completes in under sixty seconds. Resistance or delay is met with escalating aggression. In some reported incidents a third person watches from the street end in case the victim tries to flee or call out to passersby.

Take Uber or InDriver between any bar or restaurant and your hotel after dark, even for a two-block distance — the fare is under 5,000 pesos and removes the entire risk environment. Stick to Carrera 1 and the streets immediately around Parque de los Novios when walking at night, and ask your hotel to name specifically which blocks are safe after dark. Do not carry visible watches or jewelry at night, and keep your phone in a pocket rather than in your hand while walking.

Red Flags

  • You are walking on a poorly lit side street outside the main tourist zone after dark
  • The street has no other pedestrians and is not well-trafficked
  • Groups of young men are loitering in doorways, alleys, or near parked cars on your route
  • You are visibly carrying expensive items — camera, phone, jewelry, or designer clothing
  • You are walking between bars and hotels through unfamiliar streets rather than on main roads

How to Avoid

  • Never walk on side streets in Santa Marta after dark — use Uber, InDriver, or a hotel-arranged taxi even for short distances.
  • Stick to well-lit, well-trafficked streets like Carrera 1 and the Parque de los Novios area when out at night.
  • Do not wear expensive jewelry, watches, or visible electronics when walking around the city.
  • If confronted, hand over your belongings without resistance — nothing is worth your safety.
  • Ask your hotel which specific streets are safe to walk at night and which to avoid.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Colombian National Police (Policía Nacional) station. Call 123 (Emergency) or 112. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at policia.gov.co.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The US Embassy in Bogotá is at Calle 24 Bis No. 48-50, Bogotá. For emergencies: +57 1-275-2000.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
