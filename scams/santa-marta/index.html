The 7 Scams

Scam #1
Taxi Bill-Switch and Counterfeit Change
⚠️ High
📍 Taxis throughout Santa Marta, especially outside hotels in El Rodadero, the Centro Histórico, and near Parque de los Novios at night

Santa Marta taxi drivers in El Rodadero and the Centro Histórico run two bill-switch plays: they palm your 20,000-peso note and replace it with a 2,000-peso bill while you're distracted, or return your genuine 50,000-peso note with a counterfeit copy — costing you 18,000 to 50,000 pesos per ride.

Taxis are the main way to move between Santa Marta's zones — El Rodadero, the Centro Histórico, Taganga, and Tayrona. Most drivers are honest and the official rate for an El Rodadero-to-Centro run is around 10,000 pesos, but a subset of drivers at hotel taxi stands and outside busy restaurants run a consistent bill-manipulation play on tourists unfamiliar with the denominations.

The first variant works on distraction. You hand the driver a 20,000-peso note for a 10,000-peso fare. He adjusts his mirror, takes a quick phone call, or turns to check the road — and in that fraction of a second palms your note and replaces it with a 2,000-peso bill from his other hand. He holds it up with a confused expression: "You only gave me 2,000." The second variant targets end-of-trip payment. You hand over a 50,000-peso note; the driver studies it, shakes his head, and returns what looks like the same bill: "no change" or "this bill is bad." The next morning you try that note at a convenience store and the clerk hands it back — it's counterfeit. The driver kept your real 50,000 and gave back a fake.

The simplest block is to carry only small bills — 5,000 and 10,000-peso notes — and pay the exact fare while naming the denomination out loud as you hand it over: "here is ten thousand pesos." Naming the amount removes plausible deniability for a switch. A stronger option is to skip street taxis entirely: InDriver, DiDi, and Uber operate in Santa Marta with digital fares and no cash handling. If you do use a street taxi, agree the fare before getting in, keep the exact amount visible to both of you, and place the bills on the driver's seat rather than passing them hand-to-hand.

Red Flags

  • The driver briefly turns away or distracts you after you hand over your payment
  • The driver claims you gave a much smaller bill than you actually handed over
  • Your change includes old, worn bills that are difficult to verify as genuine
  • The driver returns your large bill claiming it is 'bad' or there is no change
  • The driver becomes immediately agitated or aggressive when questioned about the fare or change

How to Avoid

  • Carry small bills (5,000 and 10,000 peso notes) and pay with exact change whenever possible.
  • State the denomination out loud as you hand it to the driver: 'Here is twenty thousand pesos.'
  • Agree on the fare before entering the taxi and have the exact amount ready.
  • Use ride-hailing apps like InDriver, Didi, or Uber where payment is handled digitally.
  • Familiarize yourself with Colombian bill denominations and security features before arriving — check returned bills for watermarks.
Scam #2
Fake Police Document Inspection
⚠️ High
📍 Streets around the Centro Histórico, Parque de los Novios, walkways near Taganga, and roads near popular tourist sites

Two men in police-style uniforms stop you near Parque de los Novios and demand to inspect your wallet for counterfeit bills, then pocket your cash during the inspection — real Colombian police do not conduct street wallet checks.

The fake-police scam concentrates in Santa Marta's pedestrian tourist areas — Parque de los Novios, the streets around the Centro Histórico, and the walking approach to Taganga. The actors wear clothing that mimics official National Police uniforms: dark trousers, navy or olive tops, and laminated badge lanyards that flash too quickly to read. They target solo tourists or small groups, preferably mid-afternoon when foot traffic is lower and fewer bystanders are watching.

They approach with authority, citing an official-sounding protocol: "We are checking for counterfeit currency. Please show your documents and open your wallet." One takes your passport and examines it carefully — pulling your full attention toward the document. While you watch the passport check, the second officer handles your wallet, removes bills, and returns it folded. If you hesitate or ask to see credentials, the pressure escalates: "We can take you to the station for an inspection." They issue a handwritten "receipt" and instruct you to collect your money from the local station. There is no such money to collect.

Real Colombian police do not conduct street wallet inspections for counterfeit bills — that procedure does not exist, and any officer claiming otherwise is running a scam. If stopped, ask to see official photo ID and write down the badge number shown; genuine officers will comply, scammers will move on. Never hand over your wallet — if ID verification is requested, hold your passport yourself and let them view it in your hands. Call 123 in front of them if the pressure doesn't stop. A useful pre-trip habit: leave your real passport in the hotel safe and carry only a photocopy of the photo page and entry stamps.

Red Flags

  • Officers in plain clothes or slightly off-looking uniforms approach and flash badges too quickly to read
  • They ask to inspect your wallet or cash — real police in Colombia do not check for counterfeit bills during street stops
  • They approach you on foot or motorcycle in a quiet area rather than from a marked police vehicle
  • They demand cash payment for an 'infraction' without issuing proper official documentation
  • They threaten jail, deportation, or severe consequences if you do not comply immediately

How to Avoid

  • Leave your passport at the hotel safe and carry only a photocopy of the photo page and entry stamps.
  • If approached by supposed police, politely request to see their official photo ID and badge number.
  • Suggest walking to the nearest police station to verify their identity — real officers will agree, scammers will not.
  • Never hand over your wallet — if asked for ID, hold it yourself and let them look at it in your hands.
  • Call 123 (Colombian police emergency) to verify any officer's identity before complying with demands.
Scam #3
Beach Distraction Theft
⚠️ High
📍 Playa El Rodadero, Taganga Beach, Playa Blanca, and beach areas around Parque Nacional Natural Tayrona

Two-person teams target unattended bags at El Rodadero and Taganga: one distracts nearby beachgoers with vendor patter or conversation while an accomplice rifles through your bag for phone, wallet, and camera — your moment of entering the water is the signal to move.

Santa Marta's main beaches — El Rodadero, Taganga, and Playa Blanca — draw steady tourist crowds, and theft teams work the zones where valuables are most likely to be left unguarded. The target is the moment you enter the water: bag on a towel, phone in a sandal, camera under a shirt. Standard teams run two or three people who coordinate without appearing to know each other, cycling through the beach repeatedly across the day.

The vendor approach is the most common setup. One person — carrying drinks, sunglasses, or snacks — walks past your belongings slowly while you're in the water. An accomplice on the beach strikes up a conversation with the nearest beachgoer, or creates a small distraction: a beach ball thrown near your towel, a dropped cooler. In the moment your things are unobserved, the vendor crouches and pockets your phone and wallet in under five seconds. The Taganga variant runs the distraction in the water itself — someone swims up offering to sell you something, or asks where you're from — while the retrieval happens thirty feet away on shore. You come back to a towel, a sandal, and nothing else.

The reliable block is simple: take nothing to the beach you aren't prepared to lose, and never enter the water without a trusted companion watching the bag. Use a waterproof phone pouch you wear in the water; leave real cash and extra cards at the hotel. In a group, take turns swimming so someone always has eyes on belongings. If you're solo at Taganga or Tayrona, treat your phone as a day-tripper item — a cheap backup phone, or none at all. Beachside restaurants at El Rodadero will often watch a bag for a small order if you ask.

Red Flags

  • You are leaving belongings unattended on the beach while swimming or snorkeling
  • A vendor or stranger approaches your belongings while you are in the water or distracted
  • Someone creates an unusual distraction near your stuff — a fight, loud music, a thrown object
  • A beach vendor engages you in prolonged conversation while you have belongings spread on the sand nearby
  • You notice someone walking very close to beach bags and towels belonging to various beachgoers

How to Avoid

  • Never leave valuables unattended on the beach — bring only what you need for the day and leave everything else at the hotel.
  • Use a waterproof phone pouch that you can take into the water with you.
  • Take turns swimming with a travel companion so someone always watches the belongings.
  • Use a portable beach safe or lockbox that you can secure to a fixed object like a beach chair.
  • At Tayrona Park or Taganga, bring only essentials: cash for food, a cheap phone, and sunscreen.

Scam #4
Scopolamine (Devil's Breath) Drink Spiking
⚠️ High
📍 Bars and nightclubs in El Rodadero, late-night establishments in the Centro Histórico, and encounters initiated through dating apps

Scammers in El Rodadero bars and via dating apps spike tourist drinks with scopolamine (burundanga) — a colorless, odorless drug that renders victims compliant and erases memory — then empty the hotel room of phone, wallet, watch, and cash while the victim is incapacitated.

The scopolamine risk is real and specific to Colombia at a scale not found in most other countries — tens of thousands of incidents per year nationally. In Santa Marta it concentrates in El Rodadero bars at night and in encounters arranged through dating apps where someone suggests meeting at a specific bar or coming to your hotel. The approach is indistinguishable from a genuine social encounter: an attractive person starts a conversation, buys you a drink, expresses interest. Nothing feels wrong until it is.

The drug is tasteless and odorless in a cocktail. Within fifteen to thirty minutes of exposure you feel unusually relaxed and cooperative — a pharmacological effect, not the normal social loosening of a second drink. You may remember entering the taxi and reaching the hotel, then remember nothing else. You wake up hours later with everything gone: phone, wallet, watch, laptop, any cash not locked in the hotel safe. The drug can also be rubbed onto paper you handle or, in rarer cases, blown as powder in crowded spaces. Dating app setups are a variant where the perpetrator has identified you as a target before you ever walk into the bar.

Never leave a drink unattended and never accept a drink you didn't personally watch being poured — both rules applied together cut exposure to near zero. If you feel suddenly disoriented or unusually compliant after one or two drinks, tell a trusted companion immediately and get to a safe location; don't accept an offer to "help you back to your room" from someone you just met. Keep your watch, extra cash, and backup cards in the hotel safe before going out — a worst-case scenario then means losing your pockets, not your whole room. For dating app encounters, meet in a bar you chose yourself, not a venue they suggested.

Red Flags

  • A stranger offers to buy you a drink or your drink is left unattended for any period of time
  • A new acquaintance from a dating app is unusually eager to meet at a specific bar or come to your hotel room
  • You feel suddenly dizzy, confused, or excessively compliant after only one or two drinks
  • A woman you met at a bar or on a dating app suggests going back to your hotel very quickly after meeting
  • You notice someone reaching over or near your drink while you are distracted

How to Avoid

  • Never accept drinks from strangers and never leave your drink unattended — get a new one if you stepped away.
  • Avoid bringing strangers to your hotel room, especially people met at bars or through dating apps.
  • Go out with trusted friends and watch out for each other — if someone seems suddenly out of it, get them to safety immediately.
  • Use drink covers or bottle toppers to prevent anything being dropped into your beverage.
  • If you feel suddenly disoriented after a drink, tell a trusted person immediately and get to a safe location.
Scam #5
Overpriced Unofficial Tour Guide
🔶 Medium
📍 Outside the entrance to Parque Nacional Natural Tayrona, Minca coffee farm tours, Lost City (Ciudad Perdida) trek departures, and around Santa Marta's harbor

Touts at Tayrona's entrance and around Lost City trek departure points sell "private guided tours" at 80,000 pesos — inside the park they rush the route and spend the time on their phones, while self-guided entry costs 20,000 pesos and certified guides charge 50,000–60,000 pesos through official channels.

Santa Marta is the gateway to Parque Nacional Natural Tayrona and the Ciudad Perdida (Lost City) trek — two of Colombia's most compelling natural experiences. Both attract confident, fast-talking touts who position themselves at key decision points: the Tayrona bus stop, the park entrance gate, and the departure area for multi-day Lost City treks. They look the part — cargo trousers, lanyards, clipped knowledge of trail names — and approach before you've had a chance to ask at the official ticket window.

The Tayrona setup runs like this: you arrive at the park entrance and a man in a park-style vest offers a "private guided tour, 80,000 pesos, includes everything." He mentions real trail names and the correct beach names to sound credible. Inside the park, the service evaporates — he walks fast, points vaguely at trees, and spends most of the time on his phone. At the first beach he announces the tour is complete. You later discover that self-guided entry was 20,000 pesos and a certified guide runs 50,000–60,000 pesos through channels you can verify at the park office. For Lost City treks, unauthorized operators offer prices below the market rate and frequently deliver food and accommodation far below what was promised, or simply take a deposit and become unreachable.

For Tayrona, buy your entry ticket at the official park window and, if you want a guide, ask the park office for their certified guide roster — you'll pay around 50,000–60,000 pesos for someone with a name the office recognizes and credentials you can verify. For the Lost City trek, only four operators hold official authorization from Colombia's tourism authority: Expotur, Wiwa Tour, Turcol, and Magic Tour — any other entity offering the trek is unlicensed. For Minca coffee tours, check TripAdvisor or Google reviews and confirm payment by card to an established agency, not cash to someone you met at the bus stop.

Red Flags

  • A guide approaches you at a park entrance or tourist site rather than being booked through an office or website
  • They cannot show an official guide license or certification when asked
  • The price is unusually low or high compared to what you researched online for the same tour
  • They demand full payment upfront in cash before the tour begins with no receipt or written agreement
  • Other tourists or park officials do not seem to recognize or interact with the guide

How to Avoid

  • Book tours through licensed agencies with verified reviews on TripAdvisor, Google, or GetYourGuide.
  • For the Lost City trek, only book with one of the few licensed operators (Expotur, Wiwa Tour, Turcol, or Magic Tour).
  • Ask for official guide credentials and verify them with the tourism office if in doubt.
  • Get a written agreement including the itinerary, inclusions, and refund policy before paying.
  • Research the standard price for any tour before arriving so you can spot both overcharging and suspiciously low offers.
Scam #6
Bird Poop and Bracelet Distraction Theft
🔶 Medium
📍 Centro Histórico around the cathedral, Parque de los Novios, beachfront promenades, and busy pedestrian streets near Rodadero

Teams of two to four work the Centro Histórico and Parque de los Novios with two plays: the fake bird-poop distraction, where "helping" hands rifle your bag while you inspect the stain, and the bracelet press, where a colorful bracelet is tied on your wrist then sold at 50,000–100,000 pesos while accomplices work your valuables.

The Centro Histórico streets around the cathedral, Parque de los Novios, and the beachfront promenade are the primary operating zones for organized distraction-theft teams in Santa Marta. These are two-to-four-person operations where each member has a specific role: the contact, the distractor, and the retrieval person. They target tourists carrying visible electronics, walking with a map out, or distracted by conversation — conditions that signal divided attention.

In the bird-poop play, someone taps your shoulder: "You have something on your jacket." You look down and there's a white substance on your sleeve. Within seconds a second person materializes with napkins, apologizing and dabbing at the stain — their hands moving near your front while your attention is down. Behind you, a third person unzips your bag or slips a hand into your back pocket. The whole sequence takes under fifteen seconds. The bracelet play is simpler: a person ties a colorful bracelet on your wrist with a gesture of friendship before you can pull away, then demands 50,000–100,000 pesos. While you argue or try to remove it, the accomplices drift close to your bag.

The defense for both plays is the same: walk away without stopping — if you don't engage, the script can't run. If you notice something on your clothing, keep moving until you reach a café or shop doorway before looking at it. Decline any bracelet, flower, or "gift" with a firm headshake and don't break stride. Wear your bag across your front in the Centro Histórico and keep your phone in a zipped inner pocket. If a team does close around you, step into the nearest open shop and let the staff know what's happening.

Red Flags

  • A stranger points out a stain on your clothing that appeared from nowhere — bird poop, mustard, or a white substance
  • Someone immediately materializes with napkins or tissues, touching your clothing and bag
  • A person ties a bracelet or gift on your wrist without asking, then demands payment
  • You feel people positioning themselves unusually close to you during the interaction
  • The 'helpful' people seem coordinated and overly attentive to each other's positions

How to Avoid

  • If someone points out a stain, do not stop — walk away to a safe area before inspecting your clothing.
  • Do not accept 'free' bracelets, flowers, or gifts from strangers — decline firmly and keep walking.
  • Wear your bag on your front in the Centro Histórico and keep your phone in a zipped inner pocket.
  • Travel with a companion when possible so someone can watch your back during unexpected interactions.
  • Be especially alert near the cathedral and Parque de los Novios where these teams operate most actively.
Scam #7
Attempted Knifepoint Robbery Near Historic Center
⚠️ High
📍 Side streets off the Centro Histórico at night, dark streets near Taganga, and poorly lit areas between bars and hotels

Armed robbers on foot — typically two men, one with a knife — target tourists taking side-street shortcuts between Centro Histórico bars and hotels at night, demanding phone, wallet, and watch in encounters that last under a minute.

Santa Marta's well-lit tourist corridors — El Rodadero beachfront, Carrera 1, and the area immediately around Parque de los Novios — are patrolled and relatively safe after dark. The risk is in the transitional zones: the side streets tourists use to shortcut between a restaurant and their hotel, the back approach to Taganga, and the quieter blocks at the edges of the Centro Histórico where foot traffic drops off past 9 PM. These are not random encounters; teams observe patterns and wait for tourists walking alone or in pairs through low-traffic areas.

The approach is fast and coordinated. Two men emerge from a doorway or parked car as you pass — one to your front, one stepping from the side. One has a knife, held low and visible. The demand is immediate: "Phone. Wallet. Watch." No conversation, no negotiation. The transaction takes under sixty seconds. If you hesitate, the threat escalates. The moto-robbery variant — two men on a motorbike, the pillion rider grabbing a visible phone from your hand while the driver accelerates — operates on the same streets but at speed, making resistance physically impossible.

Use a ride-hailing app for any movement after dark — Uber, InDriver, or a hotel-arranged car costs 5,000–10,000 pesos and removes the exposure entirely. There is no shortcut worth taking on foot after 9 PM on Santa Marta's side streets. If you must walk, stay on Carrera 1 and the well-lit sections of the Centro Histórico, keep your phone in a zipped inner pocket, and avoid wearing visible jewelry or watches. If confronted, comply immediately and hand over what is demanded — nothing you're carrying is worth escalating a knife confrontation.

Red Flags

  • You are walking on a poorly lit side street outside the main tourist zone after dark
  • The street has no other pedestrians and is not well-trafficked
  • Groups of young men are loitering in doorways, alleys, or near parked cars on your route
  • You are visibly carrying expensive items — camera, phone, jewelry, or designer clothing
  • You are walking between bars and hotels through unfamiliar streets rather than on main roads

How to Avoid

  • Never walk on side streets in Santa Marta after dark — use Uber, InDriver, or a hotel-arranged taxi even for short distances.
  • Stick to well-lit, well-trafficked streets like Carrera 1 and the Parque de los Novios area when out at night.
  • Do not wear expensive jewelry, watches, or visible electronics when walking around the city.
  • If confronted, hand over your belongings without resistance — nothing is worth your safety.
  • Ask your hotel which specific streets are safe to walk at night and which to avoid.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest Colombian National Police (Policía Nacional) station. Call 123 (Emergency) or 112. Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at policia.gov.co.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The US Embassy in Bogotá is at Calle 24 Bis No. 48-50, Bogotá. For emergencies: +57 1-275-2000.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
