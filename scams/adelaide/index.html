6 Tourist Scams in Adelaide

Six Adelaide scams — sourced from traveler reports with real incidents. Taxi overcharge, DiDi cancel-distance trick, fake wine tours, and rental fraud. Know before you go.

By Bernard Huang
Editor, tabiji.ai
📍 Adelaide, Australia 📅 Updated April 2026 💬 6 scams documented ⭐ Reddit-sourced & verified

Key Takeaways

Adelaide is physically one of the safest state capitals in Australia, but three financial scam clusters hit tourists hard: (1) taxi and DiDi/Uber overcharging on short CBD routes (traveler reports 118wkpo calls taxis 'a scam'), (2) fake Barossa / McLaren Vale wine tour bookings from cloned sites, and (3) short-stay rental fraud on Facebook Marketplace. International students attending Adelaide colleges also face dedicated visa/education scams worth knowing about for anyone traveling long-term.

⚡ Quick Safety Tips

  • At Adelaide Airport use Uber or DiDi from the rideshare pickup zone — taxis have been charging inflated short-run fares documented across traveler reports.
  • Check every DiDi receipt for 'driver canceled mid-trip' phantom charges — documented in traveler reports 1r2hxxy.
  • Book Barossa, Clare and McLaren Vale wine tours only with operators on the South Australian Tourism Commission register.
  • Never pay a rental deposit before in-person viewing — traveler reports 1k2rdvc documents current fake-Airbnb invoice scams.
  • Use Adelaide Metro's Metrocard or contactless on trams and buses — never pay 'transport officers' directly.
  • International students should verify any 'visa consultant' against the MARA register at mara.gov.au before paying.

The 6 Scams

Scam #1
Adelaide Taxi Short-Run Overcharge
⚠️ High
📍 Adelaide Airport taxi rank, CBD (King William, Rundle Mall), North Terrace hotel ranks

You land at Adelaide Airport and grab a cab for the 7-km trip to your North Terrace hotel. The meter ticks fast, there's a fuel surcharge, a late-night rate, and an unspecified 'airport fee.' At drop-off you're asked for $65 for what Uber quotes at $28.

Adelaide Airport's taxi rank sits 7 km from the North Terrace hotel strip — a ride that runs $28–$35 by rideshare and should cost $30–$40 by metered taxi. The overcharge problem is specific to taxis: 13CABS (the yellow fleet) and some independent operators apply surcharges that aren't displayed inside the cab or quoted before departure. Tourists arriving after an international flight, unfamiliar with South Australian taxi regulations, are the natural target.

The overcharge comes in layers. A "fuel surcharge" of $3–$5 appears at journey's end without prior disclosure. An airport levy of $3.40 is legal but not always mentioned before the trip. A "late-night rate" activates after 9 PM. The real manipulation happens in the meter increment: the legal rate clicks in $0.35 or smaller increments; a tampered or misset meter clicks at $0.40 per increment — small enough to feel normal but adding $10–$20 to a short run. Combine all three layers and a $28 rideshare trip becomes a $65 taxi bill that feels legitimate because the meter was running.

Use Uber or DiDi from the Adelaide Airport rideshare pickup bay — the fare to CBD is $25–$40, confirmed on booking, with no surprise surcharge layers. The Adelaide Metro JetExpress buses (J1, J2, J3, J7, J8) run airport-to-CBD for $5.40 with a Metrocard and are the scam-proof option. If you do take a taxi, insist on the meter, watch the increments on the display, and pay by card on the in-car terminal. Photograph the taxi plate and driver ID before departure; overcharging complaints go to the SA Department for Infrastructure and Transport (dit.sa.gov.au).

Red Flags

  • Meter clicks up faster than the legal rate of $0.35 or smaller increments
  • 'Fuel surcharge' or 'airport surcharge' not displayed inside the cab
  • 'Card reader is broken' cash-only demand
  • Flat rate quoted above $55 for a CBD run from ADL
  • Driver takes circuitous route via West Terrace or Port Road

How to Avoid

  • Use Uber or DiDi from Adelaide Airport rideshare pickup — fare $25–$40 to CBD.
  • Take JetExpress bus (J1/J2) to CBD — $5.40 with Metrocard.
  • Pay by card on the in-car terminal and screenshot the receipt.
  • Photograph taxi plate before the ride.
  • Report overcharging via adelaidemetro.com.au.
Scam #2
DiDi / Uber 'Cancel Mid-Trip' Charge Scam
🔶 Medium
📍 Adelaide CBD, DiDi and Uber platform

You book a DiDi from Rundle Mall to the Adelaide Oval — a $9 trip. The driver pulls up, waits 30 seconds, then moves the car 100m down the road and ends the ride in the app. Your card is charged $15 for a 'cancellation fee' and 'part-trip fare.' The driver collects a full trip payout while driving off to do it again.

The cancel-scam is a systematic exploit of rideshare platform payment structures. DiDi and Uber both compensate drivers for genuinely interrupted or cancelled trips — a reasonable protection against passengers who no-show after making a driver wait. Scammer drivers exploit this by positioning the car near the pickup point, pretending the trip started, and ending it after driving 100 metres. The platform's automated system processes a cancellation fee and a "part-trip fare" and charges your card before a human reviews the trip.

Your app shows the ride as completed. The receipt lists a distance of 0.1 km and a charge of $12–$15. The driver is already gone. In a related variant, the driver calls after being assigned: "My app has an error — can you cancel and rebook me?" If you cancel at a driver's request, you pay the cancellation fee and the driver collects it. Adelaide-specific reports consistently identify DiDi drivers in the CBD as the predominant offenders — the platform's slower dispute resolution creates a lower-risk environment for the scam than Uber.

If your app shows a trip ended before you were picked up or before you reached your destination, screenshot the in-app trip map immediately and file a refund request citing the discrepancy — attach the screenshot. Uber resolves these disputes faster than DiDi; if DiDi stalls, escalate with a credit-card chargeback citing non-delivery of service. Never cancel a trip at a driver's request — they're seeking the cancellation fee; if the driver has a genuine issue, let the platform handle the cancellation automatically.

Red Flags

  • Driver sits at pickup spot then 'moves' the car 100–500m and ends the trip
  • Receipt shows a fare disproportionate to the trip distance (e.g. $15 for 100m)
  • Driver claims 'app issue' and wants cash or direct bank payment
  • Multiple cancel-charges on your account from different drivers
  • Driver calls asking you to 'cancel for me' so they get a cancellation fee

How to Avoid

  • Never cancel at the driver's request — they're seeking a cancellation fee.
  • Screenshot the in-app map the moment you realize the ride is being ended early.
  • Request an in-app refund with the screenshot attached.
  • Chargeback on your credit card if the platform denies the refund.
  • Prefer Uber over DiDi in Adelaide for cleaner customer service.
Scam #3
Fake Barossa / McLaren Vale Wine Tour Booking
🔶 Medium
📍 Online via Google Ads, Facebook Ads, cloned tour booking sites

You search 'Barossa wine tour from Adelaide' and a polished site offers a full-day for $79 — pickup from your hotel, four wineries, lunch included, cellar-door purchases optional. You pay by bank transfer per their instructions 'to avoid card fees.' On tour day, no minibus arrives. The phone number disconnects.

The Barossa Valley and McLaren Vale are among Australia's most famous wine regions, both within 90 minutes of Adelaide, and the wine-tour market attracts a proportional number of fraudulent operators. Fake sites are built to look like legitimate Adelaide tour companies — with trip photos, reviews (often cloned from real operators), and professional design. They appear prominently in Google Ads above genuine results. Their pricing is the tell: $79–$99 for a full-day Barossa tour with pickup, four wineries, and lunch, when legitimate operators charge $150–$200 for a comparable experience.

The scam completes on payment. The fake operator insists on bank transfer or PayPal Friends & Family "to avoid card fees" — this is the single clearest red flag, since no legitimate Australian tourism business requires bank transfer for a tour booking. You transfer the money; a confirmation email arrives with plausible-looking winery names. Tour day comes and no vehicle arrives. The phone number is disconnected. The website may still be live for the next booking cycle, or it disappears completely. Credit-card chargebacks are impossible because you paid by bank transfer.

Pay for any wine tour by credit card to an operator with a verifiable ABN — search it at abn.business.gov.au to confirm it belongs to an active Adelaide-based business. Legitimate operators with multi-year review histories include Groovy Grape Tours (groovygrape.com.au), Taste the Barossa (tastethebarossa.com.au), and Barossa Daimler (barossadaimler.com.au). Read the 50 most recent TripAdvisor reviews and check the dates: fake operators have clustered review dates around a single launch period with no reviews before that point. Never pay by bank transfer or PayPal Friends & Family for any tour.

Red Flags

  • Price is 30–40% below comparable Barossa full-day tours with lunch
  • Operator asks for bank transfer or PayPal F&F to 'avoid card fees'
  • Website has no physical Adelaide office address or ABN
  • Confirmation email doesn't name specific wineries on the itinerary
  • Google ad leads to a site with.com.co domain (not.com.au)

How to Avoid

  • Book with Groovy Grape, Taste the Barossa, Barossa Daimler or Adelaide Wine Tours.
  • Verify ABN at abn.business.gov.au.
  • Pay by credit card for chargeback protection.
  • Read 50 most recent TripAdvisor reviews — fake operators have clumped review dates.
  • Confirm exact winery names in the itinerary before paying.
Scam #4
Adelaide Fake Rental / Facebook Airbnb Invoice Scam
⚠️ High
📍 CBD, North Adelaide, Unley, Prospect — short-stay and sharehouse market

You arrange a short-stay on Facebook Marketplace — a one-bedroom in North Adelaide for $120/night. The 'host' sends what looks like an official Airbnb invoice PDF for $840, asks you to bank-transfer the amount. The email has Airbnb branding. You pay. Airbnb itself has no record of the booking.

Facebook Marketplace and Gumtree are active platforms for Adelaide short-term rentals and sharehouses, and scammers post listings for properties they don't own. The listings use real property photos (often stolen from legitimate Airbnb or real-estate postings), quote prices 20–40% below market to attract quick interest, and respond rapidly and helpfully to enquiries. The initial contact feels entirely normal — the person is responsive, the photos look authentic, the price is attractive, and the North Adelaide or CBD location is right.

The trap closes on payment. The "host" says they use Airbnb to handle the transaction securely, and an email arrives with a convincing PDF bearing the Airbnb logo, a booking reference number, and your name. The instructions say to pay by bank transfer to "confirm the booking." You transfer $700–$1,000. A few days later you try to find the booking in the Airbnb app — it doesn't exist. The host's number is disconnected. Airbnb's fraud team confirms no booking was made. The money is gone, and bank-transfer recalls succeed only when the receiving bank acts within 24 hours of the transfer.

A real Airbnb booking is confirmed entirely inside the Airbnb app — no external invoice, no email link, no bank transfer. If a host sends you a PDF invoice and asks for bank transfer, it is a scam regardless of how authentic the Airbnb branding looks. Book only through the Airbnb app directly, using a URL you typed or bookmarked yourself. For Facebook Marketplace rentals, inspect the property in person before paying any deposit. If you've already paid by bank transfer, call your bank immediately and request a recall — report the listing to Facebook Marketplace and to ScamWatch at scamwatch.gov.au.

Red Flags

  • 'Airbnb invoice' received as PDF via email rather than inside the Airbnb app
  • Host requests bank transfer, Wise, or crypto instead of in-app payment
  • Listing is on Facebook Marketplace or Gumtree with photos that look too professional
  • Price is 30–50% below comparable Adelaide short-stays
  • Host refuses a 1-minute video call to show the property live

How to Avoid

  • Book only through the Airbnb or Booking.com app — never from an email link.
  • A real Airbnb payment runs entirely in-app; external invoices are always fake.
  • Never pay by bank transfer outside the platform.
  • Reverse image-search listing photos in Google Images.
  • Report scam listings to Airbnb and Facebook Marketplace immediately.
Scam #5
International Student Visa / Education Agent Fraud
⚠️ High
📍 Online / WeChat / WhatsApp ads targeting South Asian and Southeast Asian students

You're a prospective international student or recent graduate in Adelaide. A 'migration agent' advertises on social media: $8,000 for a visa pathway or PR sponsorship via an obscure Adelaide-based college. The agent takes cash upfront, enrols you in a fake or unaccredited course, and the pathway doesn't exist.

Adelaide has a large international student population — particularly from South Asia and Southeast Asia — and the city's lower-cost education market compared to Sydney and Melbourne has attracted a disproportionate concentration of fraudulent migration agents and unregistered education providers. They advertise on WeChat, WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram with polished graphics promising "guaranteed PR pathways," "fast-track student visas," and "enrollment in registered courses." The target is prospective students or recent graduates facing post-study visa uncertainty.

The agent takes $5,000–$15,000 cash upfront, presenting it as an enrollment fee or "visa processing package." They enroll the student in a course at an obscure college that may be unregistered with CRICOS — Australia's official register of institution approvals for international students — or registered under a different name than advertised. The visa pathway turns out not to exist. In some cases the college itself is a front that accepts enrollment fees and never delivers instruction. When the student's visa expires or is denied, there is no recourse because the payment was in cash and the agent had no MARA registration.

Use only MARA-registered migration agents — check mara.gov.au — and enroll only in CRICOS-registered institutions — verify at cricos.education.gov.au. Both registers are free and publicly searchable; a legitimate agent and a legitimate institution will be on both. Never pay cash for visa or education services: always pay by card or bank transfer to a registered ABN with a written receipt. Any agent promising "guaranteed PR" or "100% visa approval" is lying — no legitimate agent can guarantee Australian government immigration outcomes. Report suspected fraud to the Department of Home Affairs at immi.gov.au.

Red Flags

  • Agent promises 'guaranteed PR' or '100% visa approval' impossible
  • Payment requested in cash with no receipt
  • Institution isn't on the CRICOS register (cricos.education.gov.au)
  • Agent has no MARA Migration Agents Registration Authority number
  • Recruitment is via WeChat, WhatsApp, or Facebook without a physical Adelaide office

How to Avoid

  • Use only MARA-registered agents — check mara.gov.au public register.
  • Enrol only in CRICOS-registered institutions — verify at cricos.education.gov.au.
  • Pay by card or bank transfer with receipt — never cash.
  • Verify agent's ABN at abn.business.gov.au.
  • Report suspected fraud to the Department of Home Affairs at immi.gov.au.
Scam #6
Fake 'SA Tourism Voucher' / Booking Voucher Scam
🟡 Low
📍 Email / SMS / Facebook ads claiming South Australian government tourism vouchers

You receive an email claiming a 'South Australian Tourism Commission' voucher — $100 off any hotel stay this winter. The link leads to a site that looks legitimate, asks for personal details and card info to 'verify your voucher.' You enter your card. The voucher never materialises; small test transactions appear on your card.

South Australia's tourism authority ran a genuine Great State Voucher program during the 2020–2022 tourism-recovery period, and scammers piggyback on that legitimate precedent to add credibility to phishing campaigns. The emails and SMS messages mimic the South Australian Tourism Commission's visual branding — correct color scheme, realistic logos, and similar domain names (satc-voucher.com rather than tourism.sa.gov.au). They arrive with urgency: "Your $100 SA Tourism Voucher expires in 48 hours."

The link leads to a site requesting card details to "verify your voucher" or "secure your discount." The process looks like a standard booking form. You enter your name, email, and card number. The voucher never materialises — instead, small test transactions of $1–$2 appear on your statement within 24 hours as the scammer verifies the card before charging larger amounts or selling the details to a third party. The same card data is often used for purchases in other countries within days.

Never click a voucher link in an email or SMS — open a browser, type southaustralia.com or tourism.sa.gov.au directly, and check whether any voucher program is currently running. Real South Australian government promotions are only announced through official .gov.au channels and never require card details to "verify" a voucher. If you have entered card details on a suspicious site, contact your bank immediately to reissue the card. Report phishing attempts to ScamWatch at scamwatch.gov.au, which maintains an active alert list for SA tourism voucher variants.

Red Flags

  • Unsolicited email or SMS claiming an SA government tourism voucher
  • Link goes to a site that isn't southaustralia.com or tourism.sa.gov.au
  • Voucher 'verification' requires your card details
  • Urgency — 'voucher expires in 24 hours'
  • Sender domain is similar to but not exactly an SA government domain

How to Avoid

  • Verify any voucher program directly at southaustralia.com or tourism.sa.gov.au.
  • Never click voucher links in unsolicited emails.
  • Book hotels via Booking.com, Stayz, or calling hotels directly.
  • If you've entered card details into a suspect site, reissue your card.
  • Report phishing to ScamWatch at scamwatch.gov.au.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest South Australia Police station. Call 000 (emergency) or 131 444 (non-emergency). Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at police.sa.gov.au.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The closest US Consulate is in Melbourne: 553 St Kilda Road, Melbourne VIC 3004 (+61 3-9526-5900). The UK High Commission is in Canberra (+61 2-6270-6666). Report scams to Consumer and Business Services SA or ScamWatch at scamwatch.gov.au.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
