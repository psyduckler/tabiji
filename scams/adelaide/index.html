6 Tourist Scams in Adelaide

Six Adelaide scams — sourced from traveler reports with real incidents. Taxi overcharge, DiDi cancel-distance trick, fake wine tours, and rental fraud. Know before you go.

By Bernard Huang
Editor, tabiji.ai
📍 Adelaide, Australia 📅 Updated April 2026 💬 6 scams documented ⭐ Reddit-sourced & verified

Key Takeaways

Adelaide is physically one of the safest state capitals in Australia, but three financial scam clusters hit tourists hard: (1) taxi and DiDi/Uber overcharging on short CBD routes (traveler reports 118wkpo calls taxis 'a scam'), (2) fake Barossa / McLaren Vale wine tour bookings from cloned sites, and (3) short-stay rental fraud on Facebook Marketplace. International students attending Adelaide colleges also face dedicated visa/education scams worth knowing about for anyone traveling long-term.

⚡ Quick Safety Tips

  • At Adelaide Airport use Uber or DiDi from the rideshare pickup zone — taxis have been charging inflated short-run fares documented across traveler reports.
  • Check every DiDi receipt for 'driver canceled mid-trip' phantom charges — documented in traveler reports 1r2hxxy.
  • Book Barossa, Clare and McLaren Vale wine tours only with operators on the South Australian Tourism Commission register.
  • Never pay a rental deposit before in-person viewing — traveler reports 1k2rdvc documents current fake-Airbnb invoice scams.
  • Use Adelaide Metro's Metrocard or contactless on trams and buses — never pay 'transport officers' directly.
  • International students should verify any 'visa consultant' against the MARA register at mara.gov.au before paying.

The 6 Scams

Scam #1
Adelaide Taxi Short-Run Overcharge
⚠️ High
📍 Adelaide Airport taxi rank, CBD (King William, Rundle Mall), North Terrace hotel ranks

Adelaide taxi drivers apply unlisted fuel surcharges, airport fees, and tampered meters — clicking at $0.40 increments instead of the legal $0.35 maximum — to turn a 7-km airport-to-CBD run worth $25–$40 into a $65–$99 bill, with no breakdown on receipt and no displayed rate card inside the cab.

Adelaide Airport sits 7 km west of the CBD, and the metered taxi fare for the run should land between $25 and $40. Drivers applying the overcharge add unlisted fuel surcharges, late-night rate loadings, and unspecified "airport fees" that appear in the total but are not displayed on any card or sign inside the cab and are not authorized under South Australian fare regulations. The practice is systematic enough that Adelaide taxi overcharging has become one of Australia's most-documented transport complaints alongside Melbourne and Sydney airport taxis.

The technical variant is meter tampering: the fare clicks up in $0.40 increments instead of the legally permitted $0.35 or smaller increments — a difference that accumulates quietly through a 15-minute ride and is impossible to detect without knowing the correct rate. Complaints produce a frustrated cycle: the surcharges look ambiguous enough that drivers argue legality, and victims don't have the specific rate-card information to file an enforceable complaint at the time of the dispute.

Uber or DiDi from the Adelaide Airport rideshare bay delivers the same trip for $25–$40 with a fixed digital fare and no surcharge ambiguity. If you take a taxi, say "meter please" before you close the door, photograph the taxi plate and driver ID card before moving, and pay by card on the in-car terminal so you have a dated payment record for any complaint to adelaidemetro.com.au — the JetExpress buses (J1, J2, J3) to the CBD for $5.40 remove the question entirely.

Red Flags

  • Meter clicks up faster than the legal rate of $0.35 or smaller increments
  • 'Fuel surcharge' or 'airport surcharge' not displayed inside the cab
  • 'Card reader is broken' cash-only demand
  • Flat rate quoted above $55 for a CBD run from ADL
  • Driver takes circuitous route via West Terrace or Port Road

How to Avoid

  • Use Uber or DiDi from Adelaide Airport rideshare pickup — fare $25–$40 to CBD.
  • Take JetExpress bus (J1/J2) to CBD — $5.40 with Metrocard.
  • Pay by card on the in-car terminal and screenshot the receipt.
  • Photograph taxi plate before the ride.
  • Report overcharging via adelaidemetro.com.au.
Scam #2
DiDi / Uber 'Cancel Mid-Trip' Charge Scam
🔶 Medium
📍 Adelaide CBD, DiDi and Uber platform

DiDi drivers in Adelaide's CBD accept a booking, move the car 100 metres down the road, and end the trip in the app — charging $15 for a "cancellation fee" and "part-trip fare" while pocketing a full payout for a ride they never completed and immediately booking the next victim.

Adelaide's CBD DiDi market has a documented pattern where drivers accept a booking, appear to approach the pickup point, then drive the vehicle 100 metres past it and end the trip in the app before the passenger boards. The charge that lands on the card is a cancellation fee plus a "part-trip fare" — legitimate fees designed to compensate drivers for genuinely interrupted pickups, exploited here by drivers who manufacture the interruption and collect the payout without completing the job.

A related variant has the driver calling to claim an "app issue" and asking for direct cash payment — that route bypasses the app's consumer protection entirely and removes any chargeback evidence. The scam runs in short cycles: a driver can repeat the ghost-pickup several times an hour across the CBD, accumulating $15 per incident with minimal exposure. Traveler reports consistently identify CBD DiDi as the higher-risk option in Adelaide; Uber's customer service is faster and more responsive when disputes are filed.

Screenshot the ride map in the app the moment you realize a driver has ended a trip without completing it, then dispute the charge in-app immediately with the screenshot attached. If the platform stalls on a refund, escalate via a credit card chargeback citing "service not rendered" — DiDi customer service is notoriously slow and the chargeback is the most reliable recovery path; never cancel at a driver's request, as doing so transfers the cancellation fee responsibility to you.

Red Flags

  • Driver sits at pickup spot then 'moves' the car 100–500m and ends the trip
  • Receipt shows a fare disproportionate to the trip distance (e.g. $15 for 100m)
  • Driver claims 'app issue' and wants cash or direct bank payment
  • Multiple cancel-charges on your account from different drivers
  • Driver calls asking you to 'cancel for me' so they get a cancellation fee

How to Avoid

  • Never cancel at the driver's request — they're seeking a cancellation fee.
  • Screenshot the in-app map the moment you realize the ride is being ended early.
  • Request an in-app refund with the screenshot attached.
  • Chargeback on your credit card if the platform denies the refund.
  • Prefer Uber over DiDi in Adelaide for cleaner customer service.
Scam #3
Fake Barossa / McLaren Vale Wine Tour Booking
🔶 Medium
📍 Online via Google Ads, Facebook Ads, cloned tour booking sites

Cloned Barossa and McLaren Vale wine tour websites rank high on Google through paid ads, collect bank transfer payments for full-day tours that never run, and disappear — the "bank transfer to avoid card fees" payment requirement is the single most reliable indicator separating fake operators from legitimate ones.

Searching "Barossa wine tour from Adelaide" returns a mix of legitimate operators and high-ranked cloned sites that mimic their design, pricing, and itinerary descriptions. A polished page offers a full-day tour — hotel pickup, four wineries, lunch included — for $79 to $150 per person, with bank transfer requested "to avoid card fees." The booking confirmation email arrives promptly with a PDF receipt that looks entirely plausible.

On tour day, no bus arrives. The phone number disconnects or routes to voicemail. The company has no South Australian ABN searchable at abn.business.gov.au and no physical Adelaide office. Legitimate Barossa tour operators have thousands of verified TripAdvisor and Google reviews accumulated over years; the fake sites rely on Google Ad placement for visibility rather than review credibility, which is why they can be outranked by searching with "reviews" appended or by checking the review history for clumped posting dates.

Legitimate Barossa and McLaren Vale operators always accept credit card payment — the "bank transfer to avoid card fees" request is the scam's tell and is sufficient reason to stop immediately. Before paying any tour operator, verify their ABN at abn.business.gov.au and cross-reference their TripAdvisor profile for genuine review history; verified operators include Groovy Grape, Taste the Barossa, and Barossa Daimler — and a credit card payment preserves your chargeback right even on legitimate bookings if something goes wrong.

Red Flags

  • Price is 30–40% below comparable Barossa full-day tours with lunch
  • Operator asks for bank transfer or PayPal F&F to 'avoid card fees'
  • Website has no physical Adelaide office address or ABN
  • Confirmation email doesn't name specific wineries on the itinerary
  • Google ad leads to a site with.com.co domain (not.com.au)

How to Avoid

  • Book with Groovy Grape, Taste the Barossa, Barossa Daimler or Adelaide Wine Tours.
  • Verify ABN at abn.business.gov.au.
  • Pay by credit card for chargeback protection.
  • Read 50 most recent TripAdvisor reviews — fake operators have clumped review dates.
  • Confirm exact winery names in the itinerary before paying.
Scam #4
Adelaide Fake Rental / Facebook Airbnb Invoice Scam
⚠️ High
📍 CBD, North Adelaide, Unley, Prospect — short-stay and sharehouse market

Facebook Marketplace "hosts" in Adelaide send official-looking Airbnb invoice PDFs and request bank transfer payment for short-stay rentals — Airbnb has no record of the booking, the host disappears after the transfer arrives, and the $840+ is unrecoverable because bank transfers carry no chargeback mechanism.

Facebook Marketplace short-stay listings in Adelaide mix genuine hosts with scammers posting realistic rentals at competitive prices. A "host" responds quickly, discusses availability warmly, and sends what looks like an official Airbnb invoice PDF — correct branding, a booking reference number, and payment instructions requesting bank transfer to "confirm the booking" before the property is held for you.

Airbnb does not send PDF invoices via email or Facebook, and it does not process payments through bank transfer to a private account. All legitimate Airbnb bookings are confirmed inside the Airbnb app with payment running through Airbnb's own platform. The scammer's email is convincing enough that victims pay, receive a "confirmation" follow-up, and only discover the fraud on arrival when the Airbnb app shows no booking under their name — at which point the "host" is unreachable and the bank transfer is gone.

Airbnb never asks for bank transfer payment — a PDF invoice requesting one is a scam regardless of how official the branding appears. Book all short-stays directly through the verified Airbnb app or website with payment processed through the platform; if you have already paid by bank transfer, contact your bank within 24 hours and request a payment recall — the speed of that call is the only variable that affects recovery odds, and bank recall success rates drop sharply after 48 hours.

Red Flags

  • 'Airbnb invoice' received as PDF via email rather than inside the Airbnb app
  • Host requests bank transfer, Wise, or crypto instead of in-app payment
  • Listing is on Facebook Marketplace or Gumtree with photos that look too professional
  • Price is 30–50% below comparable Adelaide short-stays
  • Host refuses a 1-minute video call to show the property live

How to Avoid

  • Book only through the Airbnb or Booking.com app — never from an email link.
  • A real Airbnb payment runs entirely in-app; external invoices are always fake.
  • Never pay by bank transfer outside the platform.
  • Reverse image-search listing photos in Google Images.
  • Report scam listings to Airbnb and Facebook Marketplace immediately.
Scam #5
International Student Visa / Education Agent Fraud
⚠️ High
📍 Online / WeChat / WhatsApp ads targeting South Asian and Southeast Asian students

Unlicensed "migration agents" advertising on Facebook and WhatsApp charge Indian and Chinese students in Adelaide $8,000–$13,000 upfront for PR sponsorship pathways through fake or unaccredited Adelaide colleges — the pathway does not exist, cash payments leave no legal trail, and the money is unrecoverable.

Adelaide's lower-cost international education market and concentration of Indian and Southeast Asian students has attracted a disproportionate number of visa fraud operations. Unlicensed "migration agents" advertising on Facebook, WeChat, and WhatsApp groups offer specific promises: subclass 190 or 491 state nomination pathways, PR sponsorship via an Adelaide-based college, or guaranteed post-study work visas — all at $8,000 to $13,000 upfront, usually in cash.

The college is either unaccredited, deregistered, or a paper entity — not listed on CRICOS, the Commonwealth Register of Institutions and Courses for Overseas Students, which is the only valid registration for international student enrollment in Australia. Enrollment paperwork is generated, course fees are collected, and the visa application either fails, is fraudulent, or is never submitted. Students are left with no recognized qualification, a failed or void visa, and no recourse because cash payments create no legal evidence trail.

Verify any migration agent on the public MARA register at mara.gov.au before paying anything — only MARA-registered agents can legally charge for migration advice in Australia. Verify any educational institution on the CRICOS register at cricos.education.gov.au before enrolling; any agent who promises "guaranteed PR" is making a legally false claim — no legitimate agent can guarantee visa outcomes, and that promise alone is sufficient grounds to stop and report to the Migration Agents Registration Authority.

Red Flags

  • Agent promises 'guaranteed PR' or '100% visa approval' impossible
  • Payment requested in cash with no receipt
  • Institution isn't on the CRICOS register (cricos.education.gov.au)
  • Agent has no MARA Migration Agents Registration Authority number
  • Recruitment is via WeChat, WhatsApp, or Facebook without a physical Adelaide office

How to Avoid

  • Use only MARA-registered agents — check mara.gov.au public register.
  • Enrol only in CRICOS-registered institutions — verify at cricos.education.gov.au.
  • Pay by card or bank transfer with receipt — never cash.
  • Verify agent's ABN at abn.business.gov.au.
  • Report suspected fraud to the Department of Home Affairs at immi.gov.au.
Scam #6
Fake 'SA Tourism Voucher' / Booking Voucher Scam
🟡 Low
📍 Email / SMS / Facebook ads claiming South Australian government tourism vouchers

Phishing emails and SMS messages mimicking the South Australian Tourism Commission offer "$100 Great State Vouchers" with a link to a cloned government-looking site — the site harvests card details, the voucher never materializes, and small test transactions appear on the card within days.

The South Australian Tourism Commission ran legitimate "Great State Voucher" promotions during the 2020–2022 tourism-recovery period, and that public familiarity with government-issued accommodation discounts created the brand recognition that scammers now exploit. Phishing emails and SMS messages offer $100 vouchers for hotel stays "this winter" with a link to a site that closely mimics the appearance of southaustralia.com and tourism.sa.gov.au — correct logos, government-style layout, and plausible promotional language.

The link leads to a form requesting personal details and card information "to verify your voucher." Once entered, small test transactions of $1–$2 appear on the card within days to confirm the card is live, followed by larger unauthorized charges. The voucher never arrives. The site rotates to a new domain when flagged and the operation continues. The scam targets volume rather than high per-victim amounts, relying on the government-voucher framing to suppress skepticism that would normally apply to an unsolicited link.

Legitimate SA government voucher programs are announced on southaustralia.com and tourism.sa.gov.au — navigate to those URLs directly by typing them rather than following any link in an email or SMS. Never enter card details on any site reached through an unsolicited voucher link; report phishing attempts to ScamWatch at scamwatch.gov.au, and use established platforms like Booking.com or Wotif for actual SA accommodation discounts that come with platform-level consumer protection and documented chargeback processes.

Red Flags

  • Unsolicited email or SMS claiming an SA government tourism voucher
  • Link goes to a site that isn't southaustralia.com or tourism.sa.gov.au
  • Voucher 'verification' requires your card details
  • Urgency — 'voucher expires in 24 hours'
  • Sender domain is similar to but not exactly an SA government domain

How to Avoid

  • Verify any voucher program directly at southaustralia.com or tourism.sa.gov.au.
  • Never click voucher links in unsolicited emails.
  • Book hotels via Booking.com, Stayz, or calling hotels directly.
  • If you've entered card details into a suspect site, reissue your card.
  • Report phishing to ScamWatch at scamwatch.gov.au.

🆘 What to Do If You Get Scammed

📋 File a Police Report

Go to the nearest South Australia Police station. Call 000 (emergency) or 131 444 (non-emergency). Get an official crime report — you'll need this for insurance claims. You can also report online at police.sa.gov.au.

💳 Cancel Your Cards

Call your bank immediately. Most have 24/7 numbers on the back of the card (keep a photo saved separately). Block any suspicious transactions before the thieves use your details.

🛂 Lost Passport?

Contact your nearest embassy or consulate. The closest US Consulate is in Melbourne: 553 St Kilda Road, Melbourne VIC 3004 (+61 3-9526-5900). The UK High Commission is in Canberra (+61 2-6270-6666). Report scams to Consumer and Business Services SA or ScamWatch at scamwatch.gov.au.

📱 Track Your Device

If your phone was stolen, use Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android) from another device. Don't confront thieves yourself — share the location with police instead.
