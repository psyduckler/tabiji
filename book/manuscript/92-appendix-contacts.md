### Hiroshima, Fukuoka, Nara, Okinawa, Yokohama

Smaller cities have fewer dedicated international clinics. The fallback is to dial 119 for an ambulance and ask for English-language support ("eigo onegai shimasu"), or to ask your hotel's front desk to call on your behalf. Most major city hospitals have at least one English-speaking doctor on staff, particularly in emergency departments.

## Where to find a koban (police box) in each city

Japan's koban system is one of its most practical assets. Every major intersection in a tourist district has one, and many are open twenty-four hours. You do not need an appointment, and you do not need to speak Japanese to be helped. Koban officers will call a language service onto the line with you.

- **Tokyo:** Kabukicho (directly inside the entertainment district), Shibuya Station Hachiko exit, Asakusa temple approach.
- **Kyoto:** Kyoto Station main hall, Gion (near Yasaka Shrine), Arashiyama Togetsukyo Bridge area.
- **Osaka:** Namba Station, Shinsaibashi shopping arcade, Kansai Airport.
- **Fukuoka:** Hakata Station (both exits), Tenjin intersection, Nakasu entrance.
- **Hiroshima:** Hiroshima Station, Peace Memorial Park, Hondori shopping arcade.
- **Nara:** Kintetsu Nara Station, Todai-ji approach.
- **Sapporo:** Sapporo Station south exit, Susukino main intersection, Odori Park.
- **Okinawa:** Naha Airport, Kokusai-dori, Chatan entertainment district.
- **Yokohama:** Yokohama Station central exit, Chinatown Zenrinmon gate, Minato Mirai tourist center.

## A note on calling 110 with limited Japanese

If you dial 110 and cannot explain what is happening in Japanese, say "English please" clearly and wait. Police dispatchers in tourist areas are trained to recognize this request and will bring a translator onto the line. If you can, state your location as a landmark or station name rather than as a street address. "Shibuya Crossing" is more useful than a ward address an English speaker might struggle to pronounce correctly.
