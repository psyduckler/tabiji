# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are ordered by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed, do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. In Japan, a signed credit card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, this is a crime under Japanese law (*kyokanbo*, confinement), and the correct response is to dial 110, say "English please," and stay on the line. Police in entertainment districts are well-practiced at responding to this specific call.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection, the business license (often displayed on the wall), and the card terminal. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest koban (police box). Japan's koban system is one of the country's great practical assets. Most tourist districts have one at every major intersection, usually marked in English as "Police Box." Say "sagi ni aimashita" (*I was scammed*). Ask for an incident report, called a "higai todoke." You do not need perfect Japanese for this. The koban officer will usually call a translator service onto the line with you.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from Japan and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call.

Call the JNTO 24-hour tourist hotline at 050-3816-2787. This service is staffed by English speakers and is specifically designed for situations like this. They can advise on whether a local consumer-affairs line or a specialist police unit would be more appropriate for your case.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the koban report number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. If the merchant used a portable credit card terminal, which is common in scam restaurants, note that in the dispute.

If your passport was photographed or held by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed.

Email your travel insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the koban report number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position.

## Within the first week

File a formal complaint with Japan's Consumer Affairs Agency by dialing 188 from any phone in Japan. This is a national consumer-protection line and does not require an area code.

Submit the full travel insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business's name, the address, the exact transaction details, and the koban report.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps, Tabelog, or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of English-speaking hospitals in each city is in Appendix C. At the hospital, ask specifically for a "drug screen" or "toxicology panel." Keep the hospital report; it is evidence.

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate a replacement. Consular registration (STEP for US citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, and equivalents for other nationalities) is worth doing before you travel, specifically for situations like this.
