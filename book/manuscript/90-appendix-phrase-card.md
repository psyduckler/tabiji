# Appendix A: Japanese Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Japan. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **Japanese in kanji**, a romaji pronunciation guide in italics, and the **English translation**.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. If you can, say the first three or four out loud a few times. You will feel much less awkward using them when the moment comes.

A note on pronunciation: every vowel is short and crisp. "Ei" is pronounced like the English word "eight." An "u" at the end of a word is nearly silent. "R" is closer to a light "d" or "l" than to an English "r." None of this has to be perfect — Japanese listeners will forgive a strong accent as long as the key words are clear.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**
いくらですか？
*Ikura desu ka?*

**Please show me the price list.**
料金表を見せてください。
*Ryoukin-hyou wo misete kudasai.*

**How much is the seat charge and cover fee?**
席料とお通しはいくらですか？
*Sekiryou to otoshi wa ikura desu ka?*

Use this before you sit down at a bar or izakaya. The two words that matter are *sekiryou* (seat charge) and *otoshi* (the small dish that you are charged for whether you ordered it or not). Asking about them on the way in is the single most reliable way to avoid a bottakuri surprise.

**Do you accept credit cards?**
クレジットカードは使えますか？
*Kurejitto kaado wa tsukaemasu ka?*

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**
いいえ、結構です。
*Iie, kekkou desu.*

Said calmly and once, this is understood as an absolute refusal. No elaboration is expected, and repeating it louder is not necessary. A native speaker would understand this as "I am completely done with this conversation."

**I am leaving now.**
もう帰ります。
*Mou kaerimasu.*

**I do not want this.**
これは要りません。
*Kore wa irimasen.*

**Excuse me, please let me through.**
すみません、通してください。
*Sumimasen, tooshite kudasai.*

The *sumimasen* softens it so that the person blocking your path hears a polite request rather than a command. If they do not move, repeat the phrase once and keep walking.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I am calling the police.**
警察を呼びます。
*Keisatsu wo yobimasu.*

This is the single most useful phrase in this entire appendix. Say it clearly. The word *keisatsu* changes the atmosphere in a room immediately, and most scam operators will back down the moment they hear it.

**I would like to call my card company.**
カード会社に電話します。
*Kaado-gaisha ni denwa shimasu.*

**I was scammed. Please help.**
詐欺にあいました。助けてください。
*Sagi ni aimashita. Tasukete kudasai.*

Use this when speaking to a koban officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, or any bystander you want to enlist. *Sagi* is the specific word for fraud or a swindle, and it communicates what happened more precisely than any English translation you could attempt.

**Please show me the itemized breakdown.**
内訳を見せてください。
*Uchiwake wo misete kudasai.*

In Japan, bills at bars and izakaya often arrive as a single total. Asking for the *uchiwake* (breakdown) forces the establishment to account for every item, and it is the most polite version of "I do not accept this charge." A legitimate place will hand it to you without complaint. A bottakuri bar will stall.

**I did not order this.**
これは注文していません。
*Kore wa chuumon shite imasen.*

**This is not what was agreed.**
話が違います。
*Hanashi ga chigaimasu.*

A short, firm phrase that accuses nobody but puts the burden of explanation on the other party.

## Getting help

**Where is the nearest police box (koban)?**
一番近い交番はどこですか？
*Ichiban chikai kouban wa doko desu ka?*

**I would like an official taxi.**
正規のタクシーに乗りたいです。
*Seiki no takushii ni noritai desu.*

*Seiki* means official or legitimate. Use this at airports and stations where unlicensed drivers approach tourists.

**Please call 110.**
110番に電話してください。
*Hyaku-too-ban ni denwa shite kudasai.*

Shouted once in a crowded street, *hyaku-too-ban* is understood by every bystander as "call the police now." Easier to say under stress than the longer sentence.

**Is there someone who speaks English?**
英語が話せる人はいますか？
*Eigo ga hanaseru hito wa imasu ka?*

**Please, I need help.**
すみません、助けてください。
*Sumimasen, tasukete kudasai.*

## Reading yen amounts on a bill

Japanese price writing uses 万 (*man*), which means ten thousand — not one million. This trips up almost every visitor.

| Amount | Kanji | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| ¥1,000 | 千円 | *sen en* |
| ¥5,000 | 五千円 | *go-sen en* |
| ¥10,000 | 一万円 | *ichi-man en* |
| ¥50,000 | 五万円 | *go-man en* |
| ¥100,000 | 十万円 | *juu-man en* |
| ¥1,000,000 | 百万円 | *hyaku-man en* |

If the number on your bill is a multi-digit price like ¥130,000, the kanji will read *juu-san-man en* (literally, thirteen ten-thousands). Any price that ends in *-man en* is in the tens-of-thousands range. If a cashier tells you a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it out:

**Please write the number.**
数字で書いてください。
*Suuji de kaite kudasai.*

They will almost always oblige.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Japan, polite firmness is stronger than raised voices. An *iie, kekkou desu* delivered calmly is an absolute refusal, and no elaboration is expected. If you find yourself needing stronger language, the word *keisatsu* (police) changes the temperature of a room faster than shouting in English ever will.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. The scammer is reading your body language before they read your grammar. Calm and firm beats loud and uncertain every time.
