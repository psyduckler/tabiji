# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The sixty scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. If you learn the patterns, you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: Touch = obligation

Here is one of the most useful habits you can bring with you to Japan: if a stranger offers you something on the street, do not accept it.

This single habit will save you from several of the scams in this book. The fake monk scam, which appears in both the Tokyo and Kyoto chapters, works because a person dressed in religious robes places a small trinket in your hand before you can decline. Once you are holding the object, they open a donation book. You will see names in it alongside large numbers, sometimes ¥5,000 or ¥10,000, and the quiet suggestion is that you match them. The sample-then-force-sell scam in Yokohama's Chinatown works the same way. A vendor offers you a handful of warm chestnuts to try, and once they are in your hand, the vendor begins weighing out a full kilogram for you.

The defense is simple. When someone in robes approaches you near a famous site, or a street vendor offers you a sample, keep your hands in your pockets. If something has already been placed in your hand, hand it back calmly and keep walking. They rarely follow for more than a few steps.

One more thing. You may feel rude refusing, especially in Japan, where politeness is the default in almost every interaction. That feeling is what the scam is counting on. The people running these scams are not being polite to you, and you do not owe them a conversation. A small head shake and a "no thank you" is all you need. You can go back to being your polite self the moment you have walked past.

*You will see this pattern in: the fake monk scam (Tokyo and Kyoto), the Chinatown chestnut shakedown (Yokohama), and occasional variations in other tourist districts.*

## Pattern 2: The friendly local opener

The next pattern is harder to spot because it begins with a kindness.

A well-dressed young person approaches you in a plaza, at a station, or near a famous site and asks, in good English, if you would mind helping them practice. Sometimes they ask where you are from. Sometimes the opener is, "Are you looking for a good bar?" or, "Can I show you around?" The conversation is friendly, and after a few minutes they suggest going somewhere for tea, a drink, or a photograph together.

In a country as famously polite as Japan, it is worth saying this clearly: it is not normal for Japanese strangers to approach foreign visitors on the street to practice English, invite them to bars, or offer to show them around. That is not how Japan works. When it happens, something else is going on.

The scams that follow this opener take many forms. In the Tokyo and Osaka chapters, you will see the English-practice lure, which ends at a ¥15,000 tea bill. In the Kyoto chapter, there is the Arashiyama "Dream Café," where an older woman sweeps the pavement and invites tourists in for a "cultural experience" that turns into a ¥63,000 meeting. In several cities, matching-app connections lead to bars with ¥1,000,000 bills. The Tokyo chapter also documents freelance photographers who take a deposit and disappear before delivering any photos.

The defense is to remember that in Japan, kindness to strangers is usually shown by giving them space, not by approaching them. If someone initiates a conversation in English and then suggests going somewhere together, politely decline and move on. You can always strike up conversations at a restaurant or a bar you have chosen, with people who have come there for the same reason you have.

*You will see this pattern in: the English-practice lure (Tokyo, Osaka), the matching-app bar lures (Tokyo, Fukuoka), the Arashiyama Dream Café (Kyoto), and the Instagram photographer deposit scam (Tokyo).*

## Pattern 3: No visible prices before you sit

The third pattern is the most common and the most expensive. If you cannot see the prices before you sit down or before you order, you should not sit down or order.

This one pattern accounts for roughly half of the most expensive scams in this book. The bottakuri bars in Kabukicho and Roppongi (Tokyo), Dotonbori (Osaka), Nakasu (Fukuoka), Susukino (Sapporo), Nagarekawa (Hiroshima), Gion (Kyoto), Noge (Yokohama), and Matsuyama (Okinawa) all use the same trick. Drinks appear, hostesses sit down uninvited, the door is blocked, and the bill is many times what you would have paid anywhere else. You will see ¥130,000, ¥800,000, and even over ¥1,000,000 in the worst documented cases.

The food version of this scam is equally common. The Dotonbori wagyu bait-and-switch, the Kuromon market tourist-tier pricing, the Hiroshima okonomiyaki overcharge, the Kyoto no-menu restaurants near Kinkaku-ji and Kiyomizu, and the Sapporo Nijo market overcharge all work because you committed to eating before you knew what it would cost.

The defense is a single question, asked before you sit: "ryoukin-hyou wo misete kudasai" (please show me the price list), or the simpler "ikura desu ka?" (how much?). A legitimate business will answer you immediately, often in writing. A scam venue will become vague, change the subject, or pressure you to sit anyway. If the menu you are given has no prices at all, or if the prices are only in English and Chinese but not in Japanese, or if there is no cash register visible in the restaurant, walk out. You are allowed to stand up and leave before you have ordered, and no one can stop you.

*You will see this pattern in: bottakuri bars across every city with a nightlife district, the Dotonbori wagyu bait-and-switch (Osaka), Kuromon market (Osaka), the Kyoto no-menu restaurants, the Hiroshima okonomiyaki overcharge, the Sapporo Nijo market overcharge, the Fukuoka Nakasu yatai, and several others.*

## Pattern 4: The unofficial ride

The fourth pattern starts at the airport.

You have just finished a long flight, you are tired, and somewhere between baggage claim and the official taxi queue, a man in a suit approaches you. He asks where you are going, and offers a "fixed price" that sounds reasonable. His car has a white license plate. An hour later, the bill is double or triple what he quoted.

In Japan, legal taxis have green license plates. An unlicensed taxi, which the Japanese call a *shirotaku* (literally, "white taxi"), has no meter, no insurance, and no legal obligation to honor any quoted price. The driver is often, but not always, linked to organized crime, which is why Japanese police have prioritized arrests of these operators. The Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto, and Okinawa chapters all document airport shirotaku operations. The Yokohama and Tokyo chapters also cover nighttime detour tricks used by some licensed taxis.

The defense is to never take a ride from a driver who approached you first. Walk past anyone who speaks to you in an airport arrival hall, and go directly to the official taxi queue. Every major Japanese airport has clear signs to it in English. If you want to skip taxis entirely, take the train. The Narita Express, the JR Haruka, the Keikyu line, the Yui Rail, and the Nankai Rapi:t will all get you where you are going for a fraction of the price, on a schedule. In town, the Go app and S.RIDE give you upfront pricing and a tracked route. When a licensed taxi does take you somewhere, follow the route on your phone in real time, and speak up the moment it deviates.

*You will see this pattern in: the Narita and Haneda shirotaku (Tokyo), the Kansai and Itami airport shirotaku (Osaka), the Kansai Airport and Kyoto Station shirotaku (Kyoto), the Naha Airport shirotaku (Okinawa), the Tokyo nighttime detour, and the Nara long-route taxi.*

## Pattern 5: The story-driven appeal

The fifth pattern is quieter than the others. It is the stranger with a problem.

A well-dressed young man approaches you on the subway or in a station concourse. His wallet has been stolen. The hotel will not let him in. He has a flight to catch and no way to pay for the taxi. Could you spare ¥5,000? He will pay you back. He knows it sounds strange, but he is desperate. He gives you his email address.

In Japan, this is always a scam. Japanese people who are genuinely homeless or in crisis do not approach foreign tourists on trains for small amounts of money. The social safety net, while imperfect, is real, and stranger-to-stranger panhandling is culturally rare. If someone approaches you with a story like this, it is a script they have used before.

A more alarming variation of this pattern is the "ore-ore" phone fraud, which appears in the Okinawa chapter. A voice on the phone claims to be a police officer, tells you that your bank account has been implicated in a fraud investigation, and asks you to transfer money to a "safe account" while they investigate. The losses from this scam in 2024 topped ¥19 million from a single tourist. The Japanese police never cold-call about accounts. Ever. The moment you receive a call like this, hang up and dial 110 yourself to confirm.

The defense for the street version is kind but short: "sumimasen" (excuse me), without stopping your walk. The defense for the phone version is shorter: hang up.

*You will see this pattern in: the Midosuji Line panhandler (Osaka), the ore-ore police-impersonation fraud (Okinawa), and the Hakata Station tourism-office phishing approach (Fukuoka).*

## Pattern 6: Bait-and-switch after commitment

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch by reading the fine print after you have signed up, so this pattern is really about what to check before you sign.

You book an Airbnb in Kyoto, confirm it, and four days before arrival the host messages you on WhatsApp rather than on the platform. There is a problem. A water leak, a double booking, a family emergency. If you cancel your reservation now, through Airbnb, you pay a partial penalty. They will refund you separately in cash, they promise, if you rebook with them directly, off-platform. The moment you cancel, you lose your money and they vanish.

You arrive at a ryokan in Nara to find that the room in the photographs has become a smaller room with no outdoor bath, and the staff will not offer a refund or a downgrade. A restaurant on Miyajima charges your credit card three times, claims each charge "failed," and refuses to refund you. A Makishi market vendor in Okinawa charges a ¥550 "cooking fee" per person, including the two family members who are not eating. A Chinatown all-you-can-eat restaurant in Yokohama adds a ¥15,000 leftover surcharge that was not on any sign outside.

All of these work because you committed before you knew all the terms. The defense is to pin down every charge before you commit: every fee, every condition, every refund policy, in writing or in a photograph of the menu, the rules, or the price sign. Any conversation that moves off the booking platform (Airbnb, Booking.com, Rakuten) is a red flag; keep the conversation on the app where the platform can intervene. Pay by credit card wherever possible, rather than debit, cash, or transfer. A credit card charge is the one form of payment you can usually reverse if the merchant refuses to refund.

*You will see this pattern in: the Airbnb and minpaku off-platform scams (Tokyo, Kyoto, Fukuoka), the ryokan bait-and-switch (Nara), the Miyajima card-retry triple charge (Hiroshima), the Makishi market cooking fee (Okinawa), the Chinatown all-you-can-eat trap (Yokohama), and the Yokohama Station west-exit hustler deposits.*
