Quick answer

Beijing is the undisputed capital of northern Chinese dumplings — a city where jiaozi are not just food but cultural identity, woven into holidays, family gatherings, and everyday meals.

Best overall
Baoyuan Jiaozi Wu
Top pick
Baoyuan Jiaozi Wu — the colorful dumpling institution with Michelin Bib Gourmand status.

Top verdicts

  • Baoyuan Jiaozi Wu: Colorful, photogenic dumplings with Michelin recognition and incredible variety
  • Mr. Shi's Dumplings: Creative dumpling fillings in an atmospheric hutong setting
  • Xian Lao Man: Authentic traditional Beijing-style jiaozi in a no-frills local setting

From the rainbow-hued creations at Baoyuan to the century-old traditions at Xian Lao Man, from steaming baozi at Qing-Feng (where Xi Jinping famously queued) to the world-class xiaolongbao at Din Tai Fung, Beijing offers the full spectrum of Chinese dumpling culture. The city's hutong neighborhoods hide some of the best jiaozi you will ever eat, while Michelin-recognized kitchens elevate guotie into art.

Frequently asked questions

What kind of dumplings are most popular in Beijing?

In Beijing, the most popular dumplings are jiaozi, typically boiled (shuijiao) or pan-fried (guotie). Fillings commonly include pork and cabbage, pork and chives, lamb and carrot, or various vegetarian combinations. Baozi (steamed buns with various fillings) are also incredibly popular, especially for breakfast. Xiaolongbao (soup dumplings) have also gained a following through chains like Din Tai Fung.

How do I order dumplings in Beijing if I don't speak Chinese?

Many popular dumpling houses, especially those catering to tourists or expats like Mr. Shi's and Din Tai Fung, have English menus or picture menus. In more local establishments, pointing to menu items or dishes on other tables is perfectly acceptable. Most restaurants understand 'jiaozi' for dumplings. You can specify 'shuijiao' (boiled) or 'guotie' (pan-fried). Dumplings are typically ordered by the liang (about 50g) — two or three liang is a normal portion.

What are common dipping sauces for Beijing dumplings?

The most common dipping sauce is a mix of soy sauce, black vinegar (cu), and often a dash of chili oil and minced garlic. Many places provide these ingredients separately at your table, allowing you to create your own blend. Some also offer a ginger-infused vinegar or sesame sauce. At northeastern-style places like Dongbei Ren, you may also find a raw garlic clove to bite between dumplings — a very traditional way to eat them.

How much do dumplings cost in Beijing?

Budget chains like Qing-Feng and Xiaoheng charge CNY 15-40 (about $2-5.50 USD) for a generous portion. Mid-range restaurants like Baoyuan, Mr. Shi's, and Xian Lao Man run CNY 50-100 ($7-14) per person including side dishes. Upscale spots like Din Tai Fung cost CNY 120-200 ($17-28), and Michelin-starred Country Kitchen at Rosewood runs CNY 300-600 ($42-84) for a full dumpling experience.

What is the best time to eat dumplings in Beijing?

Dumplings are enjoyed throughout the day in Beijing. Qing-Feng opens at 6 AM for baozi breakfasts. Lunch (11:30 AM-1:30 PM) is peak time at most sit-down dumpling houses — expect queues at popular spots. Dinner service from 5:30-8 PM is equally busy. For the most relaxed experience, go mid-afternoon (2-5 PM) when crowds thin out. On Chinese New Year's Eve, dumplings are the traditional family meal.

What is the difference between jiaozi, baozi, and xiaolongbao?

Jiaozi are crescent-shaped dumplings made with thin wheat-flour wrappers, either boiled (shuijiao) or pan-fried (guotie). Baozi are larger steamed buns with thicker, fluffy dough and various fillings — Qing-Feng's pork baozi are the classic Beijing version. Xiaolongbao are delicate soup dumplings from Shanghai, with thin skins enclosing a meat filling and hot broth — Din Tai Fung is the gold standard in Beijing. All three are represented on this list.

Are there vegetarian or vegan dumpling options in Beijing?

Yes, most dumpling houses offer vegetarian fillings. Baoyuan Jiaozi Wu is particularly good for vegetarians, with dozens of veggie fillings wrapped in colorful dough. Mr. Shi's offers egg-and-chive and mushroom options. Xian Lao Man has an excellent cabbage and peanut dumpling. Confirm that the dough itself is vegan (some use egg) and that vegetable fillings don't include lard or pork stock, which is common in traditional recipes.

Can I take a dumpling cooking class in Beijing?

Absolutely. Several excellent dumpling cooking classes operate in Beijing's hutong neighborhoods. Mama's Dish near Nanluoguxiang is one of the most popular, offering a hands-on class where you learn to make jiaozi from scratch in a traditional courtyard home. Classes typically last 2-3 hours, include a market visit, and cost CNY 200-400 ($28-56) per person. Book via Viator, GetYourGuide, or directly.

Planning your Beijing dumpling tour

The ideal Beijing dumpling crawl takes you through three distinct experiences in one day.

Start with a breakfast baozi at Qing-Feng (opens 6 AM) or an early snack run at Huguosi for old-Beijing street food. At lunch, head to Baoyuan Jiaozi Wu for the colorful Michelin Bib Gourmand experience, or try the traditional approach at Xian Lao Man in Dongcheng. In the afternoon, walk through the hutongs to Mr. Shi's in Baochao Hutong for creative fillings and a cold beer.

For the upscale experience, book dinner at Country Kitchen at the Rosewood for the finest guotie in Beijing, or queue at Din Tai Fung for world-class xiaolongbao. End with a lively evening at Dongbei Ren near Dongzhimen for northeastern-style dumplings and singing servers.

Budget roughly CNY 200–400 ($28–56 USD) for a full day of dumpling sampling across multiple spots, or CNY 60–100 ($8–14) if sticking to budget chains and street snacks. Most dumpling houses are near subway stations, making navigation easy — download Didi (Chinese Uber) or use the Beijing subway app for seamless transit.
