# The Malaysian-Traveler Quick-Reference Card {-}

Malaysia's lingua franca is Bahasa Malaysia (also called Malay), and while English is widely spoken in KL, Penang, and Langkawi, switching into even a few words of Malay shifts the dynamic of a scam attempt fast — it signals you are not the clueless tourist the script was written for. The phrases below are short, exit-oriented lines tuned to the 60 scams documented in this book. Before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix and keep it one swipe away on your phone. Memorize two numbers: **999** (all emergencies — police, ambulance, fire, reachable in English, and the Tourist Police respond on the same line) and **997** (the National Scam Response Centre, for Macau-scam and financial-fraud reports).

## Exit phrases — English and Bahasa Malaysia

### 1. Taxi and Grab refusals (KLIA, KLIA2, PEN, LGK, KKIA, KCH arrivals)

`"Tak payah, saya ambil Grab"` (No need, I'll take a Grab) — the universal opener at any Malaysian airport curb when a driver approaches you. Said once, firmly, while still walking.

`"Saya guna aplikasi sahaja"` (I use the app only) — the follow-up if the tout persists. Closes the "special price, no meter" pitch and signals that you know Grab exists.

`"Tunjukkan meter"` (Show the meter) — for the rare occasion you do take a metered taxi. A legitimate driver will tap the meter immediately; a scammer will invent a "flat rate."

`"Saya pergi ke kaunter teksi rasmi"` (I'm going to the official taxi counter) — the sentence that ends the KLIA/KLIA2 arrivals-hall tout script. Every major Malaysian airport has a coupon-taxi counter; touts outside that counter are not licensed.

`"Tak boleh, saya sudah tempah Grab"` (Can't, I've already booked Grab) — the fastest way to end a curbside pitch, whether you have actually booked or not. The driver moves on to the next mark.

### 2. Street stranger, tout, and approach refusals (Bukit Bintang, Jonker Street, Gurney Drive, Armenian Street, Pantai Cenang)

`"Tak, terima kasih"` (No thanks) — delivered firmly, at normal volume, without slowing your pace. This ends the overwhelming majority of street-tout approaches on the first try.

`"Saya tak berminat"` (I'm not interested) — one step firmer. Use after the first refusal if the tout persists or switches pitches ("massage?" → "watches?" → "girls?").

`"Tolong beredar"` (Please go away) — escalation. Said once, clearly, without apology. Use for touts who have not disengaged after two softer refusals.

`"Saya tak faham"` (I don't understand) — the strategic refusal. Useful against the Bukit Bintang "friendly local" who wants to practice English before pivoting to a gem-store commission run. Ends the conversation without confrontation.

`"Saya panggil polis"` (I'll call the police) — the final escalation. Reserve it for touts who physically block your path or grab your arm. Step toward the nearest mall, hotel lobby, or 7-Eleven as you say it.

### 3. Fake police, fake authority, and Macau-scam cold-callers

`"Tunjukkan kad identiti rasmi"` (Show your official ID) — the first line of defense against anyone claiming to be a Malaysian police officer, immigration official, or customs agent on the street. Real PDRM officers carry a *kad kuasa* (authority card) and will present it on request.

`"Kita pergi ke balai polis"` (Let's go to the police station) — the sentence that ends the fake-police shakedown. A real officer will happily escort you to the nearest *balai polis*; a scammer will suddenly remember something urgent elsewhere.

`"Saya telefon 999"` (I'll call 999) — say this while reaching for your phone. A real officer will wait; a fake one will disengage within seconds.

`"Saya hendak hubungi konsulat"` (I want to contact the consulate) — the escalation for any "official" demand that feels coercive. Every consulate in Malaysia has a 24-hour duty-officer line; a real Malaysian official has no objection to your making that call.

`"Saya tak akan serahkan pasport"` (I won't hand over my passport) — the non-negotiable line. No Malaysian police officer, hotel clerk, shop owner, or tour operator needs to take custody of your original passport. A photocopy is always sufficient.

`"Saya tak akan bayar tunai di sini"` (I won't pay cash here) — the defense against the Macau-scam in-person collection variant and the fake-fine street shakedown. Real Malaysian fines are paid at a government counter with a printed receipt, never in cash on the street.

### 4. Restaurant bill, hawker-stall, and durian-overcharge pushback

`"Berapa harga sebenar?"` (What's the real price?) — the opener for any stall without posted prices. Especially critical at Gurney Drive durian stalls, SS2 seafood restaurants, and Petaling Street tourist-grade hawker stands.

`"Tunjukkan menu dengan harga"` (Show the menu with prices) — the defense against the "market price" seafood scam and the durian-by-weight overcharge. Legitimate places produce a printed menu on demand.

`"Service charge sudah termasuk?"` (Is service charge included?) — ask this before ordering. Malaysian law requires service charge and SST to be disclosed on the menu; any restaurant stacking a surprise 10 percent service plus 6 percent SST plus a "tourist tip" at the bill stage is running a scam.

`"Saya mahu bercakap dengan tauke"` (I want to speak with the boss/owner) — the escalation phrase. *Tauke* is the universally understood word for the proprietor and carries slightly more weight than asking for the "manager."

`"Saya nak buat laporan KPDN"` (I want to file a KPDN complaint) — the sentence that most reliably ends a durian or seafood overcharge dispute. KPDN (Kementerian Perdagangan Dalam Negeri dan Kos Sara Hidup) is the Ministry of Domestic Trade, and it actively prosecutes tourist overcharging. Most stall owners will drop the disputed charge the moment they hear this phrase.

### 5. Market and souvenir overcharge pushback (Jonker Street, Central Market, Kuching Main Bazaar)

`"Terlalu mahal"` (Too expensive) — the opener to any negotiation. Said flatly, without a counteroffer. The stall owner names a lower price.

`"Berapa harga terakhir?"` (What's the final price?) — the closer. Use it after two rounds of back-and-forth. Signals that the negotiation is ending and commits the seller to a number you can accept or walk away from.

`"Saya lihat di tempat lain"` (I'll check elsewhere) — the walk-away phrase. Nine times out of ten the seller calls you back with a better price within three steps.

`"Tak payah tawar, saya beredar"` (No need to negotiate, I'm leaving) — the final exit when the seller refuses to come down. Said without hostility; in Malaysian market culture, walking away is an accepted close to a failed negotiation and no face is lost on either side.

### 6. MDAC and airport-tax scam refusal

`"Saya dah bayar MDAC di laman web rasmi"` (I already paid MDAC on the official site) — the defense against anyone at KLIA, KLIA2, or a hotel who tries to charge you a "processing fee" for the Malaysia Digital Arrival Card. The MDAC is free.

`"MDAC adalah PERCUMA di imigresen-online.imi.gov.my"` (MDAC is FREE at the official site) — the explicit rebuttal. The only legitimate MDAC portal is *imigresen-online.imi.gov.my*; any third-party site, booth, or hotel concierge charging for it is running a scam.

`"Saya ada resit rasmi"` (I have the official receipt) — the closer. Show the PDF confirmation email on your phone and walk away. No follow-up needed.

### 7. Emergency and help

`"Tolong!"` (Help!) — shout it, not say it. Malaysian bystanders and shopkeepers respond to this word.

`"Panggil 999"` (Call 999) — when you need someone else to dial for you — inside a mall, a restaurant, or a hotel lobby. 999 reaches police, ambulance, and fire, with English-speaking tourist-police officers available on request.

`"Saya perlu doktor"` (I need a doctor) — for any medical situation. Most urban Malaysian *klinik* signs are in English, but this phrase works with taxi drivers and security guards who may not be.

`"Saya dirompak"` (I was robbed) — the specific word that triggers immediate response from police, hotel staff, and bystanders. More precise than "scammed" (*ditipu*) when force or theft was involved.

`"Saya hendak hubungi konsulat [negara]"` (I want to contact the [country] consulate) — replace *[negara]* with your country: *Amerika Syarikat* (United States), *Australia, United Kingdom, Kanada, Jerman* (Germany), *Perancis* (France), *Jepun* (Japan), *Singapura.*

## Pronunciation tips

Bahasa Malaysia is one of the more phonetic languages a traveler will encounter — spelling mostly matches sound. A few rules carry most of the weight: **"c"** is always pronounced "ch" (*cuba* = "chooba," *cari* = "charee"); **"sy"** is "sh" (*syarikat* = "sharikat"); final consonants are pronounced softly, almost swallowed, especially final "k" (*tak* sounds closer to "ta" with a glottal stop than a crisp "tack"); stress sits on the penultimate syllable in most words (*te-RI-ma*, *ko-re-LA-si*). Vowels are plain — a/e/i/o/u each have one sound and do not change. If a phrase in this card does not land the first time, slow down, hit the penultimate syllable a little harder, and try again.

## When to fight, and when to walk

Malaysia is a country where walking away almost always beats pushing through. The tell is that most Malaysian scams end the moment the scammer realizes you are not the easy mark; walking into the nearest shopping mall, hotel lobby, *balai polis*, or 24-hour *mamak* restaurant is nearly always the correct next step, and doing so is never considered rude. Physical confrontation, on the other hand, is never right — Malaysia has a conservative legal culture and tourists who throw the first punch rarely come out ahead, even when they were being scammed. 999 reaches tourist police who speak English and who routinely handle scam-report walk-ins; the Grab app's "share trip" feature is a quietly powerful defensive tool that lets a trusted contact watch your ride in real time. Above all: carry a photocopy of your passport for any situation where an ID is requested, and never surrender the original — not to a police officer on the street, not to a hotel clerk who wants to "hold it," not to a tour operator who wants to "process" it. The photocopy is always enough, and the original stays with you.
