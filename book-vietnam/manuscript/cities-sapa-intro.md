Sapa sits in the Hmong highlands of northwest Vietnam — terraced rice-field country, Fansipan at 3,143 meters, and a trekking-and-homestay economy built on kickbacks that intercept travelers the moment they step off the overnight train at Lao Cai. The *Lao Cai station / Sapa bus transfer fraud* starts at the platform: men waving "Sapa bus, 200,000 VND" when the posted official green-minivan shuttle fare is 50,000, and unofficial "local driver" solicitors who quote 800,000 VND for a "comfortable private ride" that is in fact the same old van as the shared shuttle with a different sticker on the window. The pattern repeats at the Hanoi side through "tourist office" shuttle-bus sellers on the Old Quarter who sell overpriced round-trips — the defense is to book at the official Hanoi bus station or on *vexere.com* rather than through a walk-in office that takes commission.

Inside Sapa town and on the trekking routes, four further scams dominate. The *Hmong trekking guide kickback* works by running a "300,000-VND village trek" that detours through family-home handicraft shops in Cat Cat, Lao Chai, and Ta Van where guests are pressured — "our family need you buy" — into 500,000–2,000,000-VND scarf purchases; the polite defense is to say "I will buy one scarf I like, thank you for the trek" and to buy only what you actually want. The *hotel / homestay arrival "upgrade" overcharge* runs guests who booked on Booking.com or Agoda — the host on arrival insists the booked room is "closed" and demands 500,000–800,000 VND per night to "upgrade," with the defense being to show the booking confirmation on your phone (hosts back down when the receipt appears). The *Fansipan cable car closure / weather-dependent upsell* works off Sapa-town touts claiming the cable car is shut when it is running — they offer a "private viewpoint" drive at 1,500,000 VND; a quick check of the Sun World Fansipan website or a 200-meter walk to the real station resolves the ruse. The *Sapa electric-cart "local driver" overcharge* quotes 500,000 VND for rides that posted boards price at 50,000. Finally the *Hmong, Dao, and Giay village-shop pressure sale* — "you touch, you must buy" doorway guilt — plays on tourists who do not know that Vietnamese shopping convention allows you to browse, thank the vendor, and walk out. The defense is the same single sentence, politely repeated: "*Tôi chỉ xem, cảm ơn*" (I am just looking, thank you).
