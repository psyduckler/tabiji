# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are organized by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — a tailor's shop, a bar with a suddenly inflated bill, a jet-ski operator demanding a deposit, a Grab-look-alike car that has detoured to a "partner" showroom — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. A signed credit-card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, dial **113** — the national police emergency number. For tourist-specific help dial **1800 1552**, the Vietnam National Administration of Tourism (VNAT) tourist-assistance hotline, which is staffed in Vietnamese and English and is toll-free from any Vietnamese phone. For medical emergencies, **115** reaches national ambulance dispatch; **114** reaches fire and rescue.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection (Google Maps dropped-pin is ideal), the business license (usually displayed on the wall with a red Ministry of Planning and Investment seal), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward. If a pickpocket team is still in the area, walk 100 meters in any direction before you pull out your phone to screenshot.

If your bag or wallet was just stolen in the street, shout — shopkeepers, xe-om riders, and passing motorbikes in Vietnam will often help, and a visible commotion is the fastest way to draw attention. In HCMC's District 1, the MRT / metro station supervisor's office is the closest authority; station staff can call police and hold security-camera footage for you. At Hanoi's Old Quarter, the nearest ward police station (*công an phường*) is always within five blocks.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest *tourist-district police station* if one is within ten minutes; otherwise, the nearest ward police station (*công an phường*). Tourist-facing police posts exist in Hanoi's Old Quarter, HCMC's District 1 near Ben Thanh, on Ha Long Bay's Bai Chay waterfront, and in Hoi An's Ancient Town. Say *"I want to file a report"* or use the Vietnamese phrase *"tôi muốn trình báo"* (toy mwon chin bow — "I want to file a report"). You will walk out with a *biên bản* (official police record) printed and stamped with a red seal. This is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

There is no nationwide online pre-filing portal like France's *pré-plainte en ligne*. In Vietnam, the *biên bản* must be filed in person, and almost always on the same day as the incident — delayed reports raise suspicion and slow the paperwork. If you are on a tight schedule (a flight or a cruise transfer the same day), go to the police *first*, then to the airport or pier; dispatchers and travel insurers will ask why you waited if you do not.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from Vietnam and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call. If the charge was a restaurant overcharge, a tailor "deposit," or a jet-ski damage fee, also call your bank's fraud line directly — the number on the back of your card — because fraud desks sometimes freeze faster than the Visa or Mastercard emergency lines. If the card is Vietnamese-issued, the major banks (Vietcombank, BIDV, VietinBank, Techcombank, ACB, VPBank) all run 24/7 card-loss lines.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the *biên bản* reference number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Visa and Mastercard both offer strong chargeback rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend credit over debit for every Vietnam trip.

Call **1800 1552** — the Vietnam National Administration of Tourism (VNAT) *Tourist Assistance* hotline — for advice on next steps if you are not sure how to escalate. VNAT 1800 1552 is not itself a fraud-report line and cannot initiate an investigation, but the dispatchers are trained on the common tourist scams (fake-Grab, off-platform booking fraud, dual-menu overcharging, rental-damage claims, unlicensed tour operators) and will direct you to the correct city or ward police station and tell you what additional evidence to preserve. The line is free from Vietnamese mobile and landline numbers.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. US citizens should contact the US Embassy in Hanoi or the US Consulate General in Ho Chi Minh City (Le Duan Boulevard). UK citizens can reach the British Embassy in Hanoi or the Consulate in HCMC. Canadian, Australian, and Irish citizens have embassies in Hanoi and Consulates-General in HCMC.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the *biên bản* reference number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position. The major dispatchers serving the Asia-Pacific market — Allianz Global Assistance, AXA Partners, World Nomads, Chubb, Zurich — all operate 24/7 English-language lines for travelers and have local medical-provider networks throughout Vietnam. Vietnamese international hospitals will typically bill your insurer directly once a *Guarantee of Payment* is in place; public hospitals usually require you to pay up front and reclaim later.

If the scam involved an off-platform rental or a wire-fraud hotel booking, open the dispute on the booking platform (Booking.com, Agoda, Airbnb, Expedia) as well as with your card issuer. Platforms can sometimes recover deposits the card company cannot, and Agoda in particular has a dedicated Vietnam fraud team given how many bookings on the platform involve Vietnamese hotels.

## Within the first week

File a formal consumer-protection complaint with the **Vietnam Competition and Consumer Authority (VCCA)** — *Cục Cạnh tranh và Bảo vệ người tiêu dùng* — part of the Ministry of Industry and Trade. Report through *vcca.gov.vn* or the national consumer-protection hotline **1800 6838** (free). VCCA is the Vietnamese analog of the UK's Trading Standards or France's DGCCRF. It does not recover individual losses, but its regulatory actions against repeat-offender tailors, tour operators, and unlicensed rental shops are one of the few effective pressures on the scam economy. Tourist-facing businesses know which complaints reach VCCA; a filed complaint sometimes produces a restitution offer that the shop refused to make on the day of the incident.

For tour-operator disputes, file a parallel complaint with the **Vietnam National Administration of Tourism (VNAT)** via *vietnamtourism.gov.vn* or the tourist hotline **1800 1552**. VNAT keeps the license records of every registered tour operator in the country; with the license number or company name (always photograph a tour-office license before booking), VNAT can flag the operator, order compensation, and — for repeat offenders — revoke the license. For overcharging by licensed taxis, the Ministry of Transport (MoT) complaint channel is *gov.vn* with a vehicle-plate reference.

For banking and payment disputes — a charge your Vietnamese bank refuses to reverse, an ATM skim you consider the bank should cover, a disputed online-transfer fraud — escalate to the **State Bank of Vietnam (SBV) Consumer Protection Division** at *sbv.gov.vn*. The State Bank is the financial-sector ombudsman and publishes a standard complaint form online. For securities and investment-related fraud, the regulator is the **State Securities Commission of Vietnam (SSC)**.

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business's name, the address, the exact transaction details, the *biên bản* reference number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name and location only. Vietnamese defamation law (both civil and criminal) can be strict — stick to verifiable facts and receipts.

## If your phone or SIM was stolen

Call your carrier immediately to suspend the line. The three major Vietnamese carriers — **Viettel** (dial **198**), **Vinaphone** (dial **18001091**), and **MobiFone** (dial **18001090**) — all operate 24/7 stolen-device lines. Suspending the line prevents the thief from using your number to receive SMS two-factor-authentication codes and from running up international-roaming charges. If you are on a tourist eSIM (Airalo, Saily, etc.), the app will let you suspend the line in a few taps; do that before the *biên bản*.

File a *biên bản* at the nearest ward police station and include your phone's **IMEI** number (the fifteen-digit serial; find it on the original box, in your Apple or Google account, or by dialing \*#06# *before* the theft). The IMEI lets police flag the device and is required for any insurance claim. If your SIM was the target — for example, a SIM-swap scam to intercept your bank's one-time codes — contact your bank's fraud desk before anything else; SIM-swap fraud moves faster than theft recovery, and the first hour is decisive.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking, drug-testing, or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of 24/7 hospitals with English-speaking staff in each city is in Appendix C. At the hospital, ask for a toxicology screen; Vietnamese international hospitals such as Vinmec, FV Hospital (HCMC), Family Medical Practice (nationwide), and Hanoi French Hospital run these routinely and will print a report you can use as evidence.

For drug-testing or drug-planting scams — where a stranger, a bar staffer, or occasionally someone in a vest demands a roadside test or "finds" a substance in your possession — stay calm, do not resist, and call **113** or **1800 1552** before any money changes hands. Real narcotics cases in Vietnam go through official processes with written documentation, photographed evidence, and a police report you sign. Demands for cash to "make it go away" are the signature of a scam, not of legitimate enforcement.

In Vietnam, the medical-emergency number is **115** (national ambulance dispatch). For fire, dial **114**. For any incident involving a foreign tourist, **1800 1552** (VNAT) is the most reliable fallback with English-speaking dispatchers; **113** is the nationwide police line but English may be limited. If you are inside a venue and drink-spiking is suspected, have a friend call for an ambulance *before* you leave, not after — sometimes the aggressive bill pressure escalates once the scammer realizes you are leaving.

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate a replacement. Consular registration — STEP for US citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, LOCATE for UK, ROCA for Canadians, and the Irish equivalent — is worth doing before you travel, specifically for situations like this.
