# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The sixty-six scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: A stranger who approaches you first is almost certainly running a script

This is the single most important habit you can take to Vietnam. The instant a smiling local steps into your path uninvited at an airport arrivals hall, a cruise pier, a famous pagoda gate, or a market entrance, the interaction that follows is a rehearsed script. You are not being greeted. You are being sorted.

Vietnam's scam ecosystem is unusually verbal, and the openers are almost always warm. At Hanoi's Noi Bai Airport, a man in a polo shirt flashes a phone screen that looks like Grab and says "I am your driver!" — the real Grab driver is fifty meters away in the designated pickup zone. On Bui Vien walking street in Ho Chi Minh City, a young woman takes your arm and walks you toward a bar with no posted prices. On Ha Long Bay's Tuan Chau pier, a "cruise" representative offers a "special last-minute upgrade" to an operator that is not on the Ministry of Culture licensed list. In Hoi An, a friendly "fruit lady" loads the bamboo-pole baskets onto your shoulders for a "free photo" and then demands 500,000 VND per shutter click. In Da Nang, a man at the My Khe beachfront asks where you are from, invites you for "local tea," and hands you a bill for 6 million VND afterwards.

None of these interactions start with you asking for help. That is the tell. Vietnamese people are famously warm, but they do not generally accost foreign tourists on the street to volunteer Grab rides, tea invitations, or "last-minute" cruise upgrades.

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: when any stranger starts a conversation with you at an airport, a pier, or the entrance to a famous site, reply "*không, cảm ơn*" (no, thank you — pronounced *khome kaam un*) and keep walking. Do not break stride. Do not engage. If the person follows you, repeat the phrase once, louder. Vietnamese politeness conventions do not require you to listen to a sales pitch, and walking away is not rude.

*You will see this pattern in: the fake-Grab airport approach (Hanoi, Da Nang, HCMC, Nha Trang, Dalat, Phu Quoc), the Bui Vien hostess-bar gambit (HCMC), the Tuan Chau pier cruise solicitation (Ha Long Bay), the Hoi An fruit-lady photo trap, the My Khe "tea" invitation (Da Nang), the Sapa Hmong-village pressure sale, and almost every tout scene in the 66 scam entries that follow.*

## Pattern 2: "Grab is broken" or "Grab doesn't work here" means you are being overcharged

The second pattern is Grab and taxis. Vietnam has Grab and Be nationwide, and they are cheap, metered, and transparent — you see the price before you book, and you pay through the app. But a meaningful share of tourist rides begin with a driver refusing Grab and quoting a flat price — always 3× to 5× what the app would charge.

From Hanoi's Noi Bai Airport, the real Grab fare to the Old Quarter is 230,000–300,000 VND. The flat-rate airport-hall quote is 700,000–1,200,000. From HCMC's Tan Son Nhat Airport to District 1 is 180,000–250,000 on Grab; the street-taxi flat quote is 700,000. In Nha Trang, the Cam Ranh Airport flat quote runs to 1,200,000 VND when the Grab price is under 400,000. On Phu Quoc, drivers will even claim Grab "doesn't work here" — it does, the designated pickup zone is 50 meters from the arrivals exit. In Hue, the Phu Bai airport flat quote is 700,000 VND when Grab shows 250,000.

The "Grab is broken / Grab doesn't work here" phrase is the specific tell. Grab always works in Vietnam's eleven major cities. A refusal to open the app is a refusal to charge you the market price.

The defense is short and universal. Open Grab (or Be, or Xanh SM for electric cars) and book the ride on the app; the price is shown up front, paid digitally or in cash against the app fare, and there is no meter-is-broken conversation. If you must take a street taxi, look for **Mai Linh** (green) or **Vinasun** (white with red stripes) — the two licensed national operators — and insist on the meter. If the meter runs unusually fast, get out, pay the fair amount for the distance covered, and re-book on Grab. Watch out for copycat taxis — "Vinosun" with an extra letter, or "Mai Linnh" painted slightly differently — these are deliberate knockoffs.

*You will see this pattern in: the fake-Grab airport scam at every Vietnamese airport, the Vinasun copycat scam (HCMC), the Hue cyclo mid-ride extortion, the Tuan Chau pier taxi overcharge (Ha Long), the Can Tho street-taxi overcharge, and the Lao Cai-Sapa shuttle fraud.*

## Pattern 3: Prices that aren't posted will surprise you later

The third pattern is the unposted price. Vietnamese restaurants, street-food carts, jet-ski operators, boat captains, photo stands, and market vendors will sometimes — not always — quote you a price that is deliberately vague. "Special price for you." "Up to you." "Market rate." Each of these phrases hides a later surprise.

This pattern accounts for a meaningful share of the overcharging scams in this book. At Hanoi's Old Quarter shoe-shine booths, a boy starts polishing before you agree to anything and then demands 500,000 VND for a "repair" you did not ask for. On Bui Vien and in Nha Trang hostess bars, drinks are served without a visible menu and the tab arrives at 4–8 million VND. On Hoi An lantern boats, fake monks in robes press blessing bracelets on your wrist and then demand 500,000 VND. On Phu Quoc beaches, jet-ski operators quote "1,500,000 VND per hour" but quietly omit a deposit structure that they will later claim a 50-million-VND damage against. In Nha Trang and HCMC tourist restaurants, the English-language menu and the Vietnamese menu show different prices — sometimes 3× different — for the same pho.

The defense is a single habit, repeated before every transaction: ask for the price in writing, or point to a posted menu, *before* anything happens. For fish, prawns, or crab by weight, ask to see the scale and get the total in dong confirmed before it is cooked. For boat tours, confirm whether the price is *per person* or *per boat*, and whether cave fees and park entries are included. For jet skis, confirm the per-hour rate and the deposit, and record a two-minute phone video of the entire watercraft from every angle before riding off — date and time visible on the phone's clock. Photos and videos are your contract. If a menu exists only in English, ask for the Vietnamese menu as well — locals will sometimes slide it over without fuss, and the difference in price tells you whether you are at a legitimate restaurant or a dual-menu tourist trap.

*You will see this pattern in: the Hanoi shoe-shine upcharge, the Bui Vien and Nha Trang bar extortion, the Hoi An fake-monk bracelet, the Phu Quoc jet-ski deposit scam, the Nha Trang dual-menu tourist restaurant, the Cai Rang floating-market boat overcharge, the Hue Perfume River dragon-boat pressure, and the Dalat night-market shrinking-portion gambit.*

## Pattern 4: The "broken camera," the "scratch on the bike," the "damaged jet ski"

The fourth pattern is the damage claim. You rent something — a motorbike, a jet ski, a self-drive car, a bicycle, a set of snorkeling gear — and when you return it, the operator points at an imaginary or pre-existing scratch and demands millions of dong for "damage."

Hoi An, Hue, Da Nang, and Phu Quoc are the epicenters of the motorbike-scratch variant. The operator takes your passport as a deposit, points at a hairline scratch near the exhaust that was already there, and tells you it will cost 2,000,000 VND to fix. The jet-ski variant on Phu Quoc and Nha Trang scales up: 50,000,000 VND for a scratch that the next tourist will also be blamed for. The Da Nang self-drive rental car variant reaches 40,000,000 VND for "invisible" damage. Hoi An bicycle rentals run a miniature version: the chain "broke" on your watch, and the 2,000,000-VND claim appears.

The defense is photographic, and it is mandatory. Before you accept the rental, take six to ten date-stamped photos of the item from every angle: front, back, left, right, both sides of the seat, the specific areas where scratches typically appear, the odometer, and the fuel gauge. For cars, shoot a slow thirty-second video walkaround. Hand over an easily-replaceable paper photocopy of your passport identification page rather than the real passport; keep the real passport at the hotel safe, and offer a deposit in cash instead if pressed. If the operator refuses to accept a photocopy and insists on the real passport, walk to the next rental shop — in Hoi An, Hue, and Da Nang there are dozens. If a damage claim is made at return, produce the photos on the spot. If the operator still refuses to return the passport or deposit, call **113** immediately from the location and wait for police to arrive. Also note: the tourist-assistance hotline **1800 1552** (toll-free) can escalate serious rental disputes in English.

*You will see this pattern in: the Hue motorbike-scratch scam, the Hoi An bicycle-chain damage claim, the Da Nang self-drive rental damage, the Phu Quoc jet-ski deposit-damage, the Nha Trang beach-rental scam.*

## Pattern 5: An "official" is an official only at an official office

The fifth pattern is the stranger in authority. Real Vietnamese police — the People's Public Security in green uniforms, tourist-assistance officers often in plain clothes but with formal ID, traffic police in mustard-yellow vests — operate only from official stations and posted checkpoints. They do not conduct on-the-spot "fines" against foreigners for visa or clothing infractions on the sidewalk. They do not ask to search your wallet at a bar. They do not demand cash payable on the spot.

The *faux policier* pattern runs across Vietnam in two forms. The first is the HCMC and Hanoi Old Quarter "ticket checker" — a man with a lanyard and an official-looking vest will tell you your Hoi An Ancient Town or Hue Imperial City ticket is "expired" or "wrong" and demand a 500,000-VND fine on the spot. The real tickets are valid for the day or multi-day, and no fine is payable to a street figure. The second is the Dalat airport "regulation officer" — a man in a vest quotes a "government fixed taxi rate" of 1,500,000 VND that does not exist; Grab at the same airport is 300,000.

The defense is simple and universal. If anyone on the street identifies themselves as an official and wants to examine your wallet, your passport, your ticket, or your phone, refuse and walk into the nearest well-lit shop or hotel lobby. Say calmly: "*Tôi sẽ đi đến đồn cảnh sát*" (I will go to the police station — pronounced *toy say dee den don kang shaht*). Call **113** from your phone and put the call on speaker if the person has not already left. The operator may not speak English, but simply placing the call will end 95% of street encounters. For tourist-specific help, **1800 1552** is toll-free and staffed in English. A real officer will have no problem with this. A fake one will leave immediately.

*You will see this pattern in: the Hoi An fake-ticket-checker scam, the Hue Imperial City "re-entry fine" trap, the Dalat airport "official" taxi overcharge, and the general fake-official pattern at every major tourist pagoda, cruise pier, and airport.*

## Pattern 6: If you pay before you have the thing, you probably won't get the thing

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch by reading the fine print after you have committed — so this pattern is really about what to pin down before you click *Confirm* or hand over cash.

You book a "Ha Long Bay luxury cruise" online, pay full price by bank transfer, and the "operator" turns out to be a Facebook page with stolen photos of a licensed ship. You book a Dalat "resort" via an unfamiliar platform and arrive to find the address is a vacant lot. You hand 5 million VND to a pearl-farm pitch on Phu Quoc that is really a 45-minute sales room. You agree to an "all-inclusive" Cai Rang floating-market tour from HCMC that excluded the mandatory restaurant stop, the "coconut candy factory" kickback, and the "honey bee farm" commission stop once you arrived. You pay a deposit on a homestay in Sapa that was pulled from Booking.com after your transfer cleared, because the "discount" came with a demand to pay outside the platform.

All of these work because you committed before you knew the terms — and then the terms, the vendor, the venue, or the platform were not what you thought they were.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction you make in Vietnam. Book accommodation only through platforms that offer pay-through-platform guarantees and dispute resolution (Booking.com, Agoda, Airbnb, Expedia) — never by Facebook, Zalo, or direct wire transfer to a private account. When a host messages "pay by bank transfer for a 20% discount," recognize it as the off-platform fraud signal and refuse. Verify any property on Google Street View before you depart — a legitimate Vietnamese hotel has visible signage, and it appears on at least one established booking platform. Pay every rental, excursion, or hotel by credit card (Visa, Mastercard, American Express) rather than debit, cash, Wise, or bank transfer — only a credit card gives you a chargeback right, and you can reverse a fraudulent charge within thirty to sixty days. For cruises, verify the operator on the Vietnam National Administration of Tourism (VNAT) licensed-operator list at *vietnamtourism.gov.vn*. For ferries, buy at the actual pier office rather than from a street-agent storefront. For buses and trains, use *vexere.com*, *12go.asia*, or the Vietnam Railways website (*dsvn.vn*).

### One more: the "commission stop" pattern

Vietnam has its own set of tour-adjacent traps worth knowing. Any guided tour that includes an unscheduled stop at a "coconut candy factory," "honey bee farm," "pearl farm educational demonstration," or "silk weaving village" is almost certainly routing you to a commission-paying shop. This is not an emergency — you can simply decline to buy — but budget twenty extra minutes into your tour for it, and never feel obligated to purchase.

*You will see this pattern in: the Ha Long Bay fake-cruise booking scam, the Dalat fake-resort listing, the Phu Quoc pearl-farm hard sell, the Cai Rang coconut-candy kickback, the off-platform Booking.com / Agoda fraud (ubiquitous), and the Sapa homestay arrival-upgrade trap.*
