Hoi An is the most photogenic scam city in Vietnam. The lantern-lit evening stroll from the Japanese Covered Bridge down Nguyen Thai Hoc to the river is also the beat where pressure sales, fake monks, and invented "fines" work their nightly rounds. The *Hoi An tailor scam* is the flagship — there are more than three hundred tailor shops in the Ancient Town, and the pattern at the worst of them is "exclusive fabric" markup: a tout approaches with "free tea and design consultation," the tailor quotes 3,000,000 VND for a silk shirt that the same silk sells for 800,000 at two shops down, and hotels take commission for steering guests to their "partner." The defense is to cross-reference three shops before you commit, to ask to see the fabric bolt and ask the weight/percent composition (natural silk in Hoi An runs roughly 400,000–900,000 VND per meter depending on grade), to get a fitting schedule in writing, and to pay by credit card so a chargeback is possible if the garment isn't what was promised.

The *lantern-boat ride* at the Thu Bon waterfront comes with pushy vendors and a *fake-monk* routine — a man in robes drifts past, presses a blessing bracelet into your hand, and then demands 500,000 VND; real Buddhist monks in Vietnam do not press objects on tourists or demand donations, and handing the bracelet back with a firm "*không, cảm ơn*" ends the scene. The *Ancient Town ticket fake-checker* works the main-street entries — a man with a lanyard tells tourists their ticket is "expired" or "not for this zone" and demands a 500,000-VND fine; the real Ancient Town ticket (120,000 VND, purchased at the official booth) is valid for multiple entries over multiple days. The *"fruit ladies" photo trap* works Nguyen Thai Hoc and Tran Phu by loading a tourist's shoulders with the bamboo-pole baskets for "one photo" and then charging per shutter click — 100,000–500,000 VND per snap — with the defense being to agree one price for one photo or to simply decline. The *cooking-class booking kickback* via hotel concierges routes guests to overpriced classes at 3× the Google-rated price; cross-referencing on Google Maps and booking directly fixes it. Finally the *beach bicycle and motorbike damage claim* at An Bang runs the same photographic-defense logic as Hue: six to ten pre-rental photos, passport photocopy only, and a firm refusal with the photos shown on the spot.
