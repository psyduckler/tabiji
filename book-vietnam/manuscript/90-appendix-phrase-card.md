# Appendix A: Vietnamese Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Vietnam. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, followed by the **Vietnamese** (in Vietnamese Latin script with full diacritical tone marks), and a simple pronunciation guide in italics. Because Vietnamese is tonal, the pronunciation guide is approximate — speak clearly and calmly, and the key words will carry the meaning.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. Say the first three or four out loud a few times. You will feel much less awkward using them when the moment comes.

## A note on pronunciation

Vietnamese is a **tonal language** with six tones (northern dialect) or five tones (southern dialect). The same syllable spoken with different pitch contours means different words — *ma* can mean "ghost," "mother," "but," "rice seedling," "horse," or "tomb" depending on tone. Carrying every tone correctly is genuinely hard for adult learners. The good news: for defensive phrases, you do not need to. Vietnamese people are used to foreigners approximating the tones, and the key words of a polite refusal carry their meaning from context alone. If you say "*không, cảm ơn*" with any tone, any competent vendor will understand "no, thank you."

A few pointers that will get you 80 percent of the way:

- Vietnamese vowels have diacritics that signal quality and tone — *a* is "ah" (as in "father"), *â* is a short "uh" (as in "but"), *ô* is "oh," *ơ* is "uh" (as in "sir"), *e* is "eh," *ê* is "ay" (as in "hay"), *u* is "oo," *ư* is a flat central vowel that doesn't occur in English (try "oo" with unrounded lips).
- The letter *đ* (with a crossbar) is a *d* sound as in English "dog." The letter *d* without the crossbar is a *z* sound in the north, a *y* sound in the south.
- The letter *nh* at the end of a syllable is a palatal *ny* sound, as in "canyon."
- Final consonants are often unreleased — *bạc* ends in an unreleased *k*, not a released "k" sound.
- Northern Vietnamese pronounces "r" like "z"; southern Vietnamese pronounces it like the English "r." Either works.

## A note on politeness

Vietnamese does not use gendered politeness particles the way Thai does, but it does have a strong **personal-pronoun system** based on age and relationship. For a foreign traveler, the safe defaults are:

- **Tôi** (*toy*) — "I" (neutral, slightly formal; works for any age).
- **Bạn** (*bahn*) — "you" (same-age peer; safe with anyone roughly your age).
- **Anh** / **chị** (*ahn* / *chee*) — "you" to a man / woman slightly older than you.
- **Em** (*em*) — "you" to a man / woman clearly younger than you.
- **Cảm ơn** (*kaam un*) — "thank you." Always add this to defensive phrases. A sentence without *cảm ơn* sounds curt; a sentence with it sounds firm but polite.

Throughout this appendix, we use **tôi** for "I" and **bạn** or **anh/chị** for "you" as appropriate, with the pronunciation guide reflecting both northern and southern norms where they diverge.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**  
Bao nhiêu tiền?  
*Bow nyew tien?*

**Please use the meter.**  
Làm ơn bật đồng hồ.  
*Laam un bat dong ho.*

Say this the instant you get into a Vinasun or Mai Linh street taxi. If the driver refuses, get out. The phrase uses *đồng hồ* (meter/clock) — the part that makes it a request is *làm ơn bật* ("please turn on"). Alternatively, simply open Grab or Be on your phone and book the ride — Vietnamese drivers universally understand what opening the app means.

**Can I see the menu, please?**  
Cho tôi xem thực đơn.  
*Cho toy sem thuuk dun.*

Use this before you sit down at any restaurant. The word that matters is *thực đơn* (menu). If the waiter brings a menu in English only, ask:

**Can I see the Vietnamese menu?**  
Cho tôi xem thực đơn tiếng Việt.  
*Cho toy sem thuuk dun tyeng viet.*

This is the single most effective defense against the dual-menu price-discrimination scam in Nha Trang, HCMC, and Hoi An. Nearly every restaurant has a Vietnamese menu, and the prices on it are usually 40–70% of the English menu.

**What is the price per kilogram? (for seafood, fruit, or items priced by weight)**  
Giá bao nhiêu một kg?  
*Zah / Yah bow nyew moht key-low?*

Fish, prawns, crab, and other items priced by weight should be confirmed in writing or on a phone calculator before cooking. A legitimate restaurant will weigh the whole item on a visible scale, quote a total in dong, and write it down. Demand this — the per-gram surprise bill is one of the most common scams in this book.

**Do you accept credit cards?**  
Bạn có nhận thẻ tín dụng không?  
*Ban ko nyan tay tin zung khome?*

If the answer is "*có*" ("yes, we accept") but the card reader later turns out to be "broken," you are being scammed. See "Scam in progress" below.

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**  
Không, cảm ơn.  
*Khome, kaam un.*

Said firmly and without slowing your pace. This is the single most useful phrase in this entire appendix. Vietnamese people understand it as a complete refusal; you do not need to explain why.

**I'm not interested.**  
Tôi không quan tâm.  
*Toy khome kwan tam.*

Stronger than *không, cảm ơn*. Use this when a tout, a tailor-shop recruiter, or a cyclo driver keeps talking after the first refusal.

**Please leave me alone.**  
Làm ơn để tôi yên.  
*Laam un day toy yen.*

Literally "please leave me in peace." Gentle but clear. Use this for persistent touts.

**Don't touch me.**  
Đừng chạm vào tôi.  
*Dung chaam vow toy.*

Say this clearly and step back. The word *đừng* ("don't") is emphatic. Most touts will disengage immediately rather than escalate.

**Excuse me, please let me through.**  
Xin lỗi, cho tôi qua.  
*Sin loy, cho toy kwa.*

The *xin lỗi* ("excuse me") softens it so the person blocking your path hears a polite request. Say it clearly and keep moving forward.

**I'm just looking, thank you.**  
Tôi chỉ xem thôi, cảm ơn.  
*Toy chee sem thoy, kaam un.*

This is the key phrase for Sapa village-shop doorway pressure sales. It communicates "I am browsing only; I do not intend to buy." Use it firmly when a vendor says "you touch, you must buy."

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I'm calling the police.**  
Tôi sẽ gọi cảnh sát.  
*Toy say goy kang shaht.*

This is the firmest card in the deck. The police number is **113** and the tourist-assistance hotline is **1800 1552** (free, English-speaking). Say the phrase, hold up your phone, and start dialing. Most scam operators back down the moment they hear *cảnh sát*.

**I'm calling the tourist hotline.**  
Tôi sẽ gọi đường dây trợ giúp du lịch.  
*Toy say goy duhng zay chuh zup zoo lik.*

*1800 1552* is staffed in Vietnamese and English; dialing it in front of a scammer is often more effective than 113 because tourist-facing dispatchers know how to de-escalate the specific scripts.

**I'll call my bank.**  
Tôi sẽ gọi ngân hàng.  
*Toy say goy ngan hang.*

Say this if a restaurant, taxi driver, or jet-ski operator disputes a credit-card transaction. Calling your card issuer from the table signals that the charge is going to be disputed.

**I was scammed. Please help.**  
Tôi bị lừa. Xin giúp đỡ.  
*Toy bee luah. Sin zup duh.*

The phrase *bị lừa* is the specific Vietnamese expression for "was scammed / was tricked" — it communicates what happened with more urgency than any English translation. Use this when speaking to a tourist-police officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, a fellow traveler, or any bystander you want to enlist.

**Please give me an itemized receipt.**  
Cho tôi biên lai chi tiết.  
*Cho toy bee-en lie chee tyet.*

In Vietnam, most legitimate businesses will provide an itemized receipt on request. Asking for *biên lai chi tiết* (literally "detailed receipt") forces the establishment to account for every item, and it is the most polite version of "I do not accept this charge." A legitimate place will hand it to you without complaint. A tourist trap will stall.

**I didn't order this.**  
Tôi không gọi món này.  
*Toy khome goy mon nye.*

Say this calmly about any unrequested dish, bottle, or "service" that appears on your bill. In Vietnam you are not legally required to pay for food or drinks you did not order, though you will usually need to say so explicitly.

**This is not what we agreed.**  
Đây không phải là điều chúng ta đã thỏa thuận.  
*Day khome fye la dyew chung ta da thwa thwun.*

A short, firm phrase for taxi fares that exceed a quoted price, jet-ski "damage" claims, and tour-operator price-creep. A simpler alternative: "*Chúng ta đã thỏa thuận giá khác*" (*Chung ta da thwa thwun zah khak* — "we agreed a different price").

## Getting help

**Where's the nearest police station?**  
Đồn cảnh sát gần nhất ở đâu?  
*Don kang shaht gun nyet uh dow?*

Alternatively, dial **1800 1552** — the dispatcher will give you the nearest station address in English.

**I want an official taxi.**  
Tôi muốn taxi chính thức.  
*Toy mwon taxi chin thuuk.*

Or simpler: open Grab, Be, or Xanh SM on your phone. These are metered, tracked, and generally free of the "broken meter" scam.

**Please call 1800 1552.**  
Làm ơn gọi một tám không không một năm năm hai.  
*Laam un goy moht tam khome khome moht nam nam hai.*

Vietnamese numbers: *một* (1), *hai* (2), *năm* (5), *tám* (8), *không* (0). Every Vietnamese adult recognizes the tourist hotline number when you write it down.

**Help! Thief!**  
Cứu tôi! Ăn cắp!  
*Kyoo toy! An kop!*

Shouted in a crowded market or street, this is understood by every bystander as "someone stole from me, help me stop them." The word *ăn cắp* (thief) carries legal weight in Vietnam.

**Does anyone speak English?**  
Ai biết nói tiếng Anh không?  
*Eye byet noy tyeng ang khome?*

**Please help me.**  
Làm ơn giúp tôi.  
*Laam un zup toy.*

## Reading Vietnamese dong amounts

Vietnamese dong prices are usually written with the dong symbol **₫** or with "VND." Because the dong has low per-unit value, restaurants often drop three zeros on price boards — *50k* or *50.000* means 50,000 VND. Decimal conventions use the comma as thousands separator and the period or comma as decimal, sometimes inconsistently. A few helpful markers:

- Paper notes: 1,000 (gray-green), 2,000 (brown), 5,000 (blue), 10,000 (orange/yellow plastic polymer), 20,000 (blue polymer), 50,000 (pink polymer), 100,000 (green polymer), 200,000 (reddish-brown polymer), 500,000 (blue polymer). All notes feature a portrait of Ho Chi Minh.
- Coins are rarely used in circulation anymore.
- One US dollar is roughly 25,000 dong as of late 2025 — verify on xe.com before travel since the rate drifts.
- A common sanity check: 100,000 VND ≈ $4 USD; 1,000,000 VND ≈ $40 USD.

| Amount | Written | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 VND | một nghìn | *moht ngin* |
| 10,000 VND | mười nghìn | *muh-ee ngin* |
| 20,000 VND | hai mươi nghìn | *hai muh-ee ngin* |
| 50,000 VND | năm mươi nghìn | *nam muh-ee ngin* |
| 100,000 VND | một trăm nghìn | *moht chahm ngin* |
| 500,000 VND | năm trăm nghìn | *nam chahm ngin* |
| 1,000,000 VND | một triệu | *moht chyew* |

If a vendor quotes a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it out:

**Please write the number down.**  
Làm ơn viết số ra.  
*Laam un vyet suh rah.*

Almost every vendor will oblige, often on the back of a receipt or a paper napkin. Pointing at the calculator app on your phone also works.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Vietnam, polite firmness works better than raised voices — raising your voice is read as a loss of composure and rarely helps your case. A calm *không, cảm ơn* is already a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *làm ơn để tôi yên* ("please leave me alone") and *đừng chạm vào tôi* ("don't touch me") are both firm without being rude, and *tôi sẽ gọi cảnh sát* ("I'm calling the police") is the firmest card in the deck.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar — and a calm *không, cảm ơn* with a slight smile is, in itself, proof you know enough Vietnamese that the interaction is going to be harder than they expected.
