# Appendix C: Emergency Contacts {-}

Every phone number in this appendix was verified current at the time of publication, but phone numbers do change, consulates move, and hospitals occasionally renumber their switchboards. If a number below fails to connect, search the organization’s name plus *téléphone* and you will almost always find the current line on the first page of results.

**Before you travel**, we strongly recommend you double-check your own embassy or consulate’s current number directly on its official website — the US State Department’s travel.state.gov, the UK’s gov.uk, Canada’s travel.gc.ca, Ireland’s dfa.ie, Australia’s smartraveller.gov.au. Save the main switchboard and the after-hours duty-officer line on your phone before departure, and screenshot this appendix so you can read it on the Paris Métro where your signal cuts out. Even a verified number can become a wrong number between editions.

## The numbers that work everywhere in France

These are the lines to dial from any phone — mobile or landline, French SIM or roaming — anywhere in the country. All of them are free.

- **Pan-European emergency number (police, ambulance, fire):** **112**. This is the number to call if you do not speak French. Operators in every major French city have English-capable routing and will bring a translator onto the line within seconds. Say *“English, please”* clearly, then state your location as a landmark or square rather than a street address that is hard to pronounce — *“near the Eiffel Tower”* or *“Place Masséna, Nice”* is more useful than a postal address you are sight-reading.
- **Police nationale (direct):** **17**. The French national police. Use this for any crime in progress, any suspicious person following you, any pickpocket you have just caught. Operators may or may not speak English; dial 112 instead if you need English routing.
- **Pompiers (firefighters, also medical rescue and traffic accidents):** **18**. French firefighters handle far more than fires — they are the first responders for road accidents, cardiac events on the street, drownings at coastal resorts, and almost any mountain or water rescue. If you are unsure whether to call 18 or 15, call 18; they will triage and route.
- **SAMU (medical emergency, paramedics):** **15**. The national medical dispatch service. Call 15 when someone needs an ambulance and a doctor, not just first aid. If you are not sure what is happening medically, 18 or 112 will also get help moving; do not waste seconds deciding.
- **SMS and fax emergency line (for deaf, hard-of-hearing, or discreet use):** **114**. Text the nature of the emergency and your location. Useful if you cannot safely speak — for example, if you are in a taxi that is not driving where you asked, or hiding from someone. Also the correct number for deaf or hard-of-hearing travelers.
- **European missing-children hotline:** **116 000**. Free, 24/7, operates in multiple languages. If a child is separated from you in a crowded station or market, this is the line to call alongside 17.
- **SNCF rail-safety hotline:** **3117** (from any phone in France) or the **3117 app**. Report harassment, theft, or threatening behavior on trains and at stations. The app lets you send your location and a short message silently from your seat.
- **Info Escroqueries (national anti-fraud information line, free):** **0 805 805 817**. Not an emergency line — this is the consumer-facing hotline for victims of fraud, phishing, card skimming, and confidence scams. French only, but useful for general guidance and to find the right reporting channel for your specific loss.
- **SignalConso (online consumer-fraud reporting):** **signal.conso.gouv.fr**. The DGCCRF (French consumer-protection authority) portal. Report tourist-menu overcharging, taxi fraud, counterfeit-goods shops, and non-refund disputes. English interface available.

## Filing a *plainte* online before you visit the station

- **pre-plainte-en-ligne.gouv.fr** — file the pre-paperwork for a *plainte* (formal criminal complaint) online before going to the *commissariat* in person. You still must appear to sign — this is not a substitute for the station visit — but it cuts the intake time dramatically and is the difference between a two-hour wait and a twenty-minute signature. English-friendly interface.

Once you have filed, you will be asked to pick a station and an appointment window. Choose the one closest to where the crime occurred if possible; *commissariats* have jurisdiction over their arrondissement or *quartier*, and a station in a different city can still take the report but will route it back. Bring your passport, the email confirmation from the pre-complaint portal, and any photos or receipts. You will leave with a stamped *récépissé de plainte* — keep it. Your insurance, your embassy, and your card issuer may all ask to see it.

## Embassies and consulates in France for major passport nationalities

France hosts most countries’ embassies in Paris only, with consulates general in a handful of larger cities for the United States and United Kingdom. Numbers below are main switchboards; every embassy also operates a separate after-hours duty-officer line for citizens in distress, published on its own website.

### United States

The US maintains an embassy in Paris and consulates general in Marseille and Strasbourg, plus smaller consular agencies in Bordeaux, Lyon, Rennes, Toulouse, and Nice. The consular agencies are not full consulates — they handle notarials, passport intake, and emergency liaison, but most citizen services route back to Paris, Marseille, or Strasbourg.

- **US Consular Agencies:** Bordeaux, Lyon, Rennes, Toulouse, Nice. Current addresses and hours are published on fr.usembassy.gov — they change more often than the consulates general, so verify before a visit.

## City-by-city emergency contacts

For every city in this book, the *commissariat de police* is the default destination for a *plainte*. In smaller towns, the equivalent is the *brigade de gendarmerie* — the Gendarmerie and the Police nationale split France geographically (cities to the Police, rural and small-town areas to the Gendarmerie), but both accept theft reports from tourists and both can issue a *récépissé*. When in doubt, dial 17 and the dispatcher will tell you which station to go to.

Where we are not certain of a specific street address, we tell you so rather than guess. The current address of any *commissariat* or *gendarmerie* can be verified in seconds at service-public.fr or on Google Maps, and your hotel’s front desk will know the closest station by heart.

### Paris

- **Police report intake:** every arrondissement has its own *commissariat central*. The main *Préfecture de Police* is at **1 bis rue de Lutèce, 75004 Paris**, on Île de la Cité. In tourist areas, the nearest *commissariat* is rarely more than a ten-minute walk; hotel concierges keep the list.
- **Lost and found (*Service des objets trouvés*):** **36 rue des Morillons, 75015 Paris**. Central for the whole city. Items turned in on the Métro, at a museum, in a taxi, or to a police officer eventually arrive here.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospitals:** Hôtel-Dieu (central Paris, Île de la Cité) is the traditional central-Paris emergency department and is walking distance from the Louvre and Notre-Dame area. Other large emergency departments are at Pitié-Salpêtrière (13th arrondissement), Georges-Pompidou (15th), and Saint-Louis (10th). All have at least some English-speaking staff on rotation.
- **English-speaking medical care:** the **Hôpital Américain de Paris** in Neuilly-sur-Seine (+33 1 46 41 25 25) is the long-established English-speaking private hospital for the region — not a public A&E, but the default for expats and insured travelers. **SOS Médecins Paris** (3624) dispatches English-speaking GPs to your hotel room 24/7 for non-emergency care.

### Nice

- **Police report intake:** the *Commissariat central de Nice* serves the city; the tourist-heavy Promenade des Anglais and Vieux Nice area is also covered by visible *Police municipale* patrols.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **Hôpital Pasteur 2**, in the northeast of the city, is the main emergency department for Nice and the surrounding Alpes-Maritimes.
- **English-speaking medical care:** Nice’s large expat and tourist population means most private clinics on the Côte d’Azur have some English-speaking staff. Your hotel or travel insurance assistance line will route you to the closest English-speaking GP.

### Cannes

- **Police report intake:** the *Commissariat de police de Cannes* handles reports for the city. During the festival and during the yachting weeks, *Police municipale* maintain visible posts on La Croisette.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **Centre Hospitalier de Cannes (Hôpital des Broussailles)** is the main public emergency department.
- **English-speaking medical care:** private clinics along the Côte d’Azur between Cannes and Antibes commonly have English-speaking doctors; ask the hotel concierge or call your travel-insurance assistance line.

### Saint-Tropez

- **Police report intake:** the *Brigade de gendarmerie* serves Saint-Tropez (the town is small enough that gendarmerie, not Police nationale, holds jurisdiction). The station is near the port. For any emergency dial 17 — the dispatcher will direct you.
- **Major emergency hospital:** **Hôpital de Saint-Tropez (Hôpital Général de Gassin)**, just outside the village in Gassin, is the closest 24/7 emergency department.
- **English-speaking medical care:** private clinics in Saint-Tropez and neighboring Sainte-Maxime serve a heavily international clientele in season; any hotel will have a short list.

### Marseille

- **Police report intake:** the *Hôtel de Police* (Marseille’s main *commissariat*) is located in the 2nd arrondissement near the Joliette area. Tourist-heavy zones (Vieux-Port, Notre-Dame-de-la-Garde, Le Panier) fall under surrounding district stations.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **Hôpital de la Timone** is the main public emergency department for Marseille and one of the largest in southern France.
- **US Consulate General:** +33 4 91 54 92 00.
- **UK Consulate General:** +33 4 91 15 72 10.
- **Non-emergency medical information:** dial **3237** for pharmacy on-call information.

### Avignon

- **Police report intake:** the *Commissariat de police d’Avignon* serves the walled city and surrounding districts. The station address can be confirmed at service-public.fr.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **Centre Hospitalier d’Avignon (Hôpital Henri Duffaut)** is the main public emergency department.
- **English-speaking medical care:** smaller cities have fewer dedicated English-speaking clinics — fall back to your travel-insurance assistance line or ask at the tourist office on Place de l’Horloge.

### Montpellier

- **Police report intake:** the *Hôtel de Police de Montpellier* serves the city; the Écusson (historic center) is covered by surrounding district stations.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **CHU de Montpellier (Hôpital Lapeyronie)** is the main public emergency department.
- **English-speaking medical care:** Montpellier’s medical school and international student population mean several private clinics have English-speaking staff.

### Toulouse

- **Police report intake:** the *Hôtel de Police de Toulouse* serves the city. A non-emergency switchboard for information is published as **+33 5 61 12 81 97**.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **CHU de Toulouse (Hôpital Purpan)** is the main public emergency department; **Hôpital Rangueil** handles adult emergencies on the south side of the city.
- **English-speaking medical care:** Toulouse’s aerospace-industry expat population means several private GPs advertise English-speaking service.

### Lyon

- **Police report intake:** the *Hôtel de Police de Lyon* serves the city. Every *arrondissement* also has a district *commissariat*.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospitals:** **Hôpital Édouard Herriot** (central Lyon, 3rd arrondissement) and **Hospices Civils de Lyon – Hôpital de la Croix-Rousse** cover the main emergency flows.
- **English-speaking medical care:** **SOS Médecins Lyon** dispatches English-speaking GPs across the metro area. Lyon also has several private clinics that serve the international business community.

### Chamonix

- **Police report intake:** the *Brigade de gendarmerie de Chamonix* serves the valley.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **Hôpital de Chamonix** handles frontline emergency care; severe cases route to **CHU Annecy** or to the regional CHU in Grenoble.
- **Mountain rescue (*Secours en montagne*):** **04 50 53 16 89** (PGHM Chamonix — the high-mountain gendarmerie unit). For any on-mountain emergency also dial **112**; the dispatcher coordinates with the PGHM directly.

### Annecy

- **Police report intake:** the *Commissariat de police d’Annecy* serves the city.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **Centre Hospitalier Annecy Genevois (CHANGE)** is the main public emergency department.
- **English-speaking medical care:** Annecy’s proximity to Geneva means many locals speak English; private clinics around the lake are accustomed to international patients.

### Bordeaux

- **Police report intake:** the *Hôtel de Police de Bordeaux* serves the city. The US Consular Agency Bordeaux can liaise in citizen emergencies — current address on fr.usembassy.gov.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **CHU de Bordeaux (Hôpital Pellegrin)** is the main public emergency department.
- **English-speaking medical care:** Bordeaux’s wine-industry expat community has created a short list of English-speaking GPs, usually accessible via your hotel concierge or travel-insurance line.

### Biarritz

- **Police report intake:** the *Commissariat de police de Biarritz* serves the town. In summer, *Police municipale* maintain visible posts along the Grande Plage.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **Centre Hospitalier de la Côte Basque** in neighboring Bayonne is the main public emergency department for the Biarritz–Bayonne–Anglet area.
- **English-speaking medical care:** the Basque Coast’s surfing and international tourism mean several local GPs speak English; your hotel or the tourist office on Square d’Ixelles will have a list.

### Strasbourg

- **Police report intake:** the *Hôtel de Police de Strasbourg* non-emergency switchboard is published as **+33 3 90 23 17 17**.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **Hôpitaux Universitaires de Strasbourg (Nouvel Hôpital Civil)** is the main public emergency department.
- **US Consulate General:** located in Strasbourg; current address and phone on fr.usembassy.gov.
- **English-speaking medical care:** Strasbourg’s position as a seat of European institutions means English-speaking medical care is widely available at the university hospital and at private clinics serving the Council of Europe community.

### Colmar

- **Police report intake:** the *Commissariat de police de Colmar* serves the town; outlying Alsace wine-route villages fall under *gendarmerie* jurisdiction. For any emergency dial 17 or 112.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** **Hôpitaux Civils de Colmar (Hôpital Pasteur)** is the main public emergency department.
- **English-speaking medical care:** smaller-city Alsace has fewer dedicated English-speaking clinics; fall back to the university hospital in Strasbourg for complex care, or to your travel-insurance assistance line for referrals.

### Mont-Saint-Michel

- **Police report intake:** the *Brigade de gendarmerie* covering Mont-Saint-Michel is in nearby **Pontorson**. The Mont itself is a small island village with no on-site *commissariat*; for anything serious, dial 17 or 112 and a unit will be dispatched from the mainland.
- **Major 24/7 emergency hospital:** the closest hospitals are in **Avranches** (to the southeast) and **Pontorson**. Severe cases route to the regional CHU in Caen or Rennes.
- **Coast Guard (sea emergencies, critical for the bay and quicksand rescues):** **196** (CROSS — the French maritime rescue coordination center).
- **English-speaking medical care:** Mont-Saint-Michel is a day-trip village with minimal permanent medical infrastructure. If you need non-emergency care, plan for the drive to Avranches or Saint-Malo.

## If you lose your passport

1. **Report it first** — go to the nearest *commissariat de police* (or *brigade de gendarmerie* outside cities) and file a *plainte pour perte ou vol*. You will leave with a *récépissé*. This is the document your embassy wants to see before issuing an emergency replacement.
2. **Call your embassy’s main switchboard** — or its after-hours line if it is a weekend — and request an emergency passport appointment. US, UK, Canadian, Irish, and Australian embassies all issue same-day or next-day emergency travel documents; the fee varies.
3. **Bring** the *récépissé*, any photo ID you still have (a driver’s license photo on your phone counts), a passport photo (photo booths are common in French train stations), and the payment method your embassy specifies.
4. **For onward travel**, airlines will accept the emergency document issued by your embassy. Border police at Charles de Gaulle, Orly, Nice, and Marseille airports see these documents every day.

## If your phone is stolen

1. **Freeze the SIM** by calling your carrier’s customer-service line from any other phone. French carriers: **Orange** 3900 (from France) or +33 9 69 39 00 00 (from abroad); **SFR** 1023 or +33 6 10 00 10 23; **Bouygues Telecom** 614 or +33 6 60 61 46 14; **Free Mobile** 3244 or +33 1 78 56 95 60. If you use a foreign SIM, call your home carrier’s international line — most have a 24/7 theft-reporting number that forwards from France.
2. **File a *plainte*** at the nearest *commissariat* with your device’s IMEI (if you recorded it) and its serial number. Insurance claims require the signed *récépissé*.
3. **Lock and wipe remotely** via Apple’s Find My or Google’s Find My Device from any browser. Do this before filing the *plainte* — a wiped phone cannot leak your email and banking apps while you wait in line.
4. **Change passwords immediately** for any account whose app was installed on the phone: email, banking, social media. Start with email; everything else resets off it.

## If your credit card is stolen or skimmed

1. **Freeze the card** by calling the number on the back (or use the issuer’s app’s “lock card” feature, which works in seconds). This matters more than filing a police report first — every minute you wait is a minute the thief can use the card.
2. **Report to police** with a *plainte*. Insurance reimbursement and the card issuer’s fraud-dispute process both want a *récépissé*.
3. **Dispute the charges** with your issuer. Most European and US card issuers reverse fraudulent charges fully; the *récépissé* speeds the process.
4. **Watch for follow-on phishing** — a skimmed card often leads to email and SMS phishing pretending to be your bank. Never click a link in one of these messages; go to your bank’s app or website directly.

## A closing note

This appendix is meant to be read once on the plane, screenshot for offline use, and forgotten about for the rest of your trip. Phone numbers change. Consulates move. Hospitals renumber their switchboards. If any number in this list fails, the pan-European 112 will still work, and your embassy’s website will still have the current line for your country.

Travel safely, travel calmly, and if the worst happens, remember: a stolen phone is an inconvenience, not a crisis. Filing a *plainte*, freezing a card, and calling an embassy are ordinary procedures — officers and consular staff handle these cases every day, and most travelers resolve everything within a few hours and return to their trip. France is not a dangerous country; it is a practiced one. Help is close, and English is closer than you think.
