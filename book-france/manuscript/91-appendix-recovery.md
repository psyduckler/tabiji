# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are ordered by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed, do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. A signed credit-card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, this is a crime under French law (*séquestration*, unlawful confinement), and the correct response is to dial **112** and stay on the line. French emergency operators in tourist areas are trained to respond in English, and **114** (originally the SMS line for deaf and hard-of-hearing callers) is a useful backup if you need to summon help silently from inside a venue.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection, the business registration notice (almost always displayed on the wall behind the counter as the *extrait K-bis* or *SIRET*), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward. If a pickpocket team is still in the area, walk 100 meters in any direction before you pull out your phone to screenshot.

If your bag or wallet was just stolen, shout *“Au voleur !”* (*stop thief*) — this is the French hue-and-cry and bystanders are more likely to help than you might expect. French bystander-intervention law (*flagrant délit*) specifically protects anyone who helps restrain a thief caught in the act, and Article 73 of the *Code de procédure pénale* authorizes any citizen to detain a suspect caught red-handed until police arrive.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest *commissariat de police* (urban police station) or *brigade de gendarmerie* (the gendarmerie covers rural areas, suburbs, and most small towns). Most tourist districts have one within a ten-minute walk, and French stations in major cities almost always have an English-speaking officer on shift. Say *“J’ai été victime d’une arnaque”* (*I was scammed*) or *“On m’a volé”* (*I was robbed*). Ask to file a *plainte* — also called a *dépôt de plainte* — the formal written complaint that opens a criminal case. You will need the *plainte* reference number for every subsequent step: the card chargeback, the travel-insurance claim, the embassy replacement passport.

Know the difference between a *plainte* and a *main courante*. A *main courante* is a register entry — the police log your statement but do not open an investigation. It is lighter, faster, and appropriate for lost-item reports or low-stakes incidents where you just want something on record. For a scam, a theft, or any charge you intend to dispute with your card issuer or insurer, you need a full *plainte*, not a *main courante*. Officers occasionally offer the *main courante* because it is less paperwork; politely insist on the *plainte*.

If you are short on time — for example, you need to catch a train the same day — use **pré-plainte-en-ligne.gouv.fr** to start the process online. The *pré-plainte en ligne* is a pre-filing system: you enter your details online, receive an appointment at the commissariat of your choice, and when you arrive the officer already has your file on screen. It cuts the in-person time significantly. The online form is in French, but the fields are simple and translation tools handle it well.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from France and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call. If the scam was a restaurant overcharge or a taxi rip-off, also call your bank’s fraud line directly — the number on the back of your card — because fraud desks sometimes freeze faster than the Visa or Mastercard emergency lines. French-issued cards can additionally be blocked via the national opposition line **0 892 705 705**.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the *plainte* reference number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Under the European Payment Services Directive (PSD2), you have strong chargeback rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend credit over debit cards for every French trip.

Call **0 805 805 817** — *Info Escroqueries* — a free national line operated by the *Police nationale* specifically for consumer fraud. Operators are trained on the common tourist scams (bogus wifi-café charges, three-card monte, distraction pickpocketing near ATMs, duplicate-terminal card skims) and can direct you to the appropriate brigade and tell you what additional evidence to preserve. The line is staffed weekdays; outside those hours, the *plainte* at the commissariat is your primary channel.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. US citizens should contact the US Embassy in Paris; the consulates general in Marseille and Strasbourg handle regional passport services, while the *agences consulaires* in Bordeaux, Lyon, Nice, Rennes, and Toulouse offer limited services and will refer you to Paris, Marseille, or Strasbourg for emergency passport issuance.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the *plainte* reference number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position. The common French-market dispatchers — Allianz, Axa, Europ Assistance, Mondial Assistance — all operate 24/7 English-language lines for travelers. French hospitals bill the state directly if you present a valid *carte Vitale* or, for EU residents, an EHIC / GHIC; US travelers and other non-EU visitors will pay up front and reclaim via their travel insurer.

If the scam happened to be an off-platform rental or a wire-fraud hotel booking, open the dispute on the booking platform (Airbnb, Booking.com, Hotels.com) as well as with your card issuer. Platforms can sometimes recover deposits the card company cannot.

## Within the first week

File a formal complaint with the **DGCCRF** — *Direction générale de la Concurrence, de la Consommation et de la Répression des fraudes*, France’s consumer-protection agency — through its online portal **signal.conso.gouv.fr** (*SignalConso*). SignalConso forwards your complaint directly to the business, gives them a chance to respond, and feeds the DGCCRF’s regulatory-action pipeline. The portal has an English interface and is the French analog of Italy’s Cittadinanzattiva or the UK’s Trading Standards. DGCCRF does not recover individual losses, but their sanctions against repeat-offender venues (restaurants with unposted prices, unlicensed taxis, tourist-menu abuse) are the single most effective pressure on the scam economy.

For restaurant overcharging and tourist-menu traps, file a parallel complaint with the local *mairie* (town hall) consumer-affairs service. Paris, Nice, Marseille, Lyon, Strasbourg, and Bordeaux all have *Service consommation* or *Médiateur* portals with intake forms for tourist disputes, and every *mairie* is required by law to forward confirmed complaints to the DGCCRF regional directorate.

For banking and payment disputes — a charge your bank refuses to reverse, a blocked-card fee you consider abusive, a disputed online-transfer fraud — escalate to the **médiateur bancaire**, the free banking ombudsman. Every French bank has one, and the *Fédération Bancaire Française* (FBF) publishes the directory at **lesclesdelabanque.com**. For investment-related fraud, the regulator is the **AMF** (*Autorité des marchés financiers*); for bank-sector fraud more broadly, the **ACPR** (*Autorité de contrôle prudentiel et de résolution*).

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business’s name, the address, the exact transaction details, the *plainte* reference number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name and location only.

## If your phone or SIM was stolen

Call your carrier immediately to suspend the line. Orange, SFR, Bouygues Telecom, and Free all operate 24/7 stolen-device lines — the numbers are on each carrier’s website and on the back of any welcome letter you received with the SIM. Suspending the line prevents the thief from using your number to receive SMS two-factor-authentication codes and from running up international-roaming charges.

File a *plainte* and include your phone’s **IMEI** number (the fifteen-digit serial; find it on the original box, in your Apple or Google account, or by dialing \*#06# before the theft). The IMEI lets police flag the device across French carriers and is required for any insurance claim. If your SIM was the target — for example, a SIM-swap scam to intercept your bank’s one-time codes — contact your bank’s fraud desk before anything else; SIM-swap fraud moves faster than theft recovery, and the first hour is decisive.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of English-speaking hospitals in each French city is in Appendix C. At the hospital, ask specifically for a *“dépistage toxicologique”* (drug screen or toxicology panel). Keep the hospital report; it is evidence.

In France, the medical-emergency number is **15** (*SAMU*, the dispatch service for ambulances and mobile ICU units). For fire and non-SAMU medical rescue, **18** (*Pompiers*) also dispatches ambulances and is often faster in dense urban areas. **112** works for everything and is the safest default if you are unsure which service you need; **17** is the direct line to the *Police nationale* for non-medical emergencies. If you are inside a venue and drink-spiking is suspected, have a friend call for an ambulance *before* you leave, not after — sometimes the aggressive bill pressure escalates once the scammer realizes you are leaving.

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate a replacement. Consular registration (STEP for US citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, and equivalents for other nationalities) is worth doing before you travel, specifically for situations like this.
