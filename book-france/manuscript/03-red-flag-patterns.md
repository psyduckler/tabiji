# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The one hundred and ninety-one scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: In a tourist zone, physical contact is a pickpocket tell

This is the single most important habit you can take to France: if a stranger comes inside your arm’s length in a famous square, a station concourse, or on a packed Métro train, your bag is being checked, not greeted.

French pickpocket teams are unusually physical. Outside the Sacré-Cœur in Paris, a smiling woman steps in and, while her hand is near your shoulder, has already tied a woven friendship bracelet on your wrist — her partner’s hand is in your bag the same second. The same two-person trick runs at the base of the Eiffel Tower, on the steps of the Trocadéro, and on the Croisette in Cannes. On Line 1 of the Paris Métro between Concorde and Hôtel de Ville, a group of teenagers will board at the last second, cluster three-deep around the doors as they close, and have a phone and a wallet off a seated tourist before the next station. Paris’s RATP has run multi-week campaigns warning riders in five languages to keep bags in front on Lines 1, 4, 6, and 14.

On the Vieux Port in Marseille, the moped *scippo* variant — a passenger on the back of a scooter slicing a bag strap on a brush-past — has returned in waves every summer. In Nice, the Promenade des Anglais and the flower market on Cours Saleya run a swim-and-steal variant: one person asks you to watch their phone while they step to the water, and by the time you realize the phone is a decoy, your own belongings are gone. In Annecy, the lakeside walk from the Pont des Amours toward the old town has documented pickpocket reports every tourist season in recent years.

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: when any stranger enters your personal space in a crowd, step one full pace back and put a hand on your bag. Walk on the wall side of a street rather than the open side. Keep your crossbody bag on the wall side of your body, zipper facing in. Never carry a wallet in a back pocket inside any central arrondissement. And on a packed Métro or bus at tourist hotspots — Paris’s Line 1, Line 4, Line 6, Line 14, the RER B to Charles de Gaulle, Marseille Métro Line 1 to the Vieux Port, Lyon Line A to Bellecour — your bag goes in front, both hands on it, until you get off. If someone tries to tie something on you, drop it on the ground and walk away. They will not follow.

*You will see this pattern in: the Sacré-Cœur and Trocadéro friendship-bracelet rings (Paris), the Eiffel Tower approach teams, the Métro Line 1 and Line 4 pickpocket crews, the RER B airport-train thefts, the Vieux Port moped scippo (Marseille), the Promenade des Anglais swim-and-steal (Nice), the Cours Saleya market pickpocketing, the Croisette bracelet ambush (Cannes), the lakeside pickpocket pattern (Annecy), the Place Bellecour pickpocket cluster (Lyon), and almost every Métro and tram line flagged in a city chapter.*

## Pattern 2: “Excusez-moi…” from a stranger is a scam opener

The next pattern is harder to spot because it begins as a small kindness or a small question.

You are walking beside the Seine near the Pont des Arts. A woman ahead of you bends down, picks something up, and with a surprised look shows you a heavy gold ring. “I don’t need this — you keep it,” she says, pressing it into your palm and then, a beat later, asking whether you might have “a little something” for a coffee to say thank you. This is the *arnaque à la bague* — the gold ring scam — and it has been documented on the Rick Steves Travel Forum, r/ParisTravelGuide, and *Le Parisien* for more than a decade. The ring is brass. It is worth nothing. The “finder” works the Quai by the Louvre, the Champ de Mars, the Tuileries, and the Pont Neuf on a rotating schedule with a partner who is usually pretending to be a lost tourist.

Other openers: a clipboard-holder at the base of the Eiffel Tower asks you to sign a petition for deaf children; while you read, her two partners lift your phone and your wallet. A man points at the ground at the Trocadéro and tells you your shoelace is untied; his partner is already behind you. A woman on Place Kléber at the Strasbourg Christmas Market presses a “souvenir card” into your hand and asks for a small signature of thanks — while her partner behind you is in your coat pocket. A “lost” tourist on the Gare de Colmar platform asks you to help lift her heavy suitcase onto the TER Alsace train; while both your hands are busy with her bag, her confederate is in yours.

None of these interactions are casual. *Le Parisien* has run repeated features on the *bande de mineures* (teenage-girl petition gangs) arrested at the Eiffel Tower, the Louvre, and the Trocadéro — groups of five to ten, brought from the suburbs, rotated across sites every few hours. The Colmar train-station luggage-helper scam has appeared multiple times in *Dernières Nouvelles d’Alsace* — particularly during the Christmas Market season when arrivals and departures concentrate.

French people do not generally approach foreign tourists on the street for a “found” ring, for a petition, for a photo, or for help lifting a bag. When it happens in a tourist zone, something else is going on.

The defense is short and not rude. Reply to any “excusez-moi, vous parlez anglais?” approach with a firm “non merci” and do not stop walking. Do not reach for a “found” ring, sign a clipboard, or hand over your phone for a photo at a famous site. If you want a photo of yourself, ask another tourist with their own camera; scammers carry nothing. If someone is trying to put something in your hand, drop it on the ground and walk on. They will not chase you.

*You will see this pattern in: the gold ring trick along the Seine (Paris), the Eiffel Tower and Trocadéro petition rings, the Louvre clipboard scam, the Strasbourg Christmas Market petition variant, the Colmar train-station luggage-helper theft, the friendship-bracelet gangs outside the Sacré-Cœur (Paris), the Croisette bracelet ambush (Cannes), the bracelet teams on the Promenade des Anglais (Nice), and the string/bracelet friendship scam around the Capitole (Toulouse).*

## Pattern 3: If prices are not posted outside, you do not sit down

The third pattern is the most common and, across a full meal, among the most expensive. French law requires *affichage obligatoire* — restaurants must post a menu with prices visible from outside — and the DGCCRF enforces it with fines. If no menu is posted, if the posted menu omits per-item prices for fish or meat, or if a *rabatteur* (tout) at the door is calling to you in English and Italian, you are being sorted into a tourist trap.

This single pattern accounts for a meaningful share of the overcharging scams in this book. On the Mont-Saint-Michel causeway, La Mère Poulard — the famous omelet restaurant — is the best-documented example of an “iconic” restaurant where the tourist price of a single soufflé omelet (€40–80) is not posted anywhere a visitor sees before sitting down; *Ouest-France* has run multiple letters-to-the-editor on this over the years. In Saint-Tropez, the Port and Place des Lices restaurants work a *minimum de consommation* model — an unposted €80–120 per-person minimum on weekends during season — that has been the subject of repeated *Nice-Matin* consumer features. On the Cannes Croisette, an unposted “seaside terrace supplement” can double a bill. In Avignon, inside the Palais des Papes area, a variant charges a “cover” (*couvert*) at €4–7 per person for bread and water that arrived unordered. On Promenade des Anglais in Nice, the Cours Saleya daytime restaurants and the Vieux Nice *socca* stalls run *al peso* seafood priced by the 100 grams that can surprise a table that ordered what it thought was a €26 bouillabaisse.

The defense is a single habit, repeated before every sit-down meal. Before taking a chair, photograph the full menu from outside the restaurant, including any *couvert* or *service* line. If the *couvert* is above €3 per head, walk — French law permits a cover for bread and water but any reasonable restaurant keeps it under €3, and anything in the €4–7 range is a tourist-trap tell. If no menu is posted, walk. If a *rabatteur* is at the door calling to tourists, walk — that is the clearest tell of a tourist trap, one that French locals on r/france and r/ParisTravelGuide consistently name. When a waiter offers seafood “priced by the 100 grams,” ask to be shown the whole fish on the scale before it is cooked, and get a per-item euro figure confirmed out loud. Three minutes of vigilance at the door saves a bill in the hundreds.

*You will see this pattern in: the La Mère Poulard omelet markup (Mont-Saint-Michel), Saint-Tropez Port restaurant minimums, the Cannes Croisette “terrace supplement,” the Avignon Palais-area unposted *couvert*, the Nice Cours Saleya seafood-per-gram, the Vieux Port bouillabaisse tourist menu (Marseille), the Place du Capitole tourist menus (Toulouse), the Place Kléber Christmas Market food-stall markup (Strasbourg), and most of the restaurants flagged along the Riviera and Provence chapters.*

## Pattern 4: Airport and station transport starts at the official queue — never from a driver who approached you

The fourth pattern starts at the airport.

You have just finished a long flight, you are tired, and somewhere between baggage claim and the official taxi queue a well-dressed man steps into your path. He is not in uniform. He says, in good English, “taxi to Paris?” and quotes a flat fare that sounds reasonable. An hour later the bill is double what he quoted, the “broken” card reader has forced you to an ATM where he watched your PIN, and there is no receipt.

France’s airport and station transport scams fall into three families. The most common is the *taxi clandestin* — an unlicensed driver with no meter, no legal obligation to honor a quoted price, and no easy way to complain to the *préfecture* if things go wrong. They work every major French airport, and they specifically target tourists who do not know the posted fixed fares. The official fare from Paris Charles de Gaulle to the Right Bank is €56 flat and to the Left Bank €65 flat (current Paris *tarif forfaitaire*). From Paris Orly, €36 flat to the Left Bank and €44 flat to the Right Bank. From Nice Côte d’Azur to the city center it is a flat €32 (current *tarif forfaitaire*); any driver who quotes €80+ is scamming. From Marseille Provence it is a flat €50 by day and €60 at night to the Vieux Port. Any driver who quotes substantially above these rates, or who “won’t use the meter,” is a scammer. In Chamonix, *taxi clandestin* variants run the Geneva Airport–Chamonix transfer route every winter weekend; the going rate for the licensed shuttle is €35 per seat, and any SUV pickup quoting €200+ for a private car with no receipt is unlicensed.

The second family is the “broken meter” or “broken card reader” scam inside a licensed taxi. The driver claims his reader does not work, and steers you to an ATM to withdraw cash while he watches your PIN. This has been documented from Paris to Marseille to Nice to Bordeaux. In any French taxi, if the card reader is “broken,” get out, pay cash for the metered total only, take a photograph of the *plaque d’immatriculation* (license plate) on the rear, and file a *plainte* — either at a *commissariat* or online at pre-plainte-en-ligne.gouv.fr. You do not need to go to an ATM.

The third family is the RER and regional-train pickpocket — not a driver scam, but a transport scam that catches airport-to-destination travelers. The RER B between Charles de Gaulle and central Paris is notorious enough that Île-de-France Mobilités posts multilingual warnings at every station; Nice’s Tram T2 (Aéroport ↔ Port Lympia) and the Lignes d’Azur 12 and 230 buses between the airport and Gare de Nice-Ville run similar patterns. The defensive move is to sit with luggage wedged between feet and back, phone in front pocket only, and headphones off at stops.

The defense, across all three families, is to never take a ride from a driver who approached you first. Walk past anyone who says “taxi?” in an airport arrival hall and go directly to the posted official taxi queue, the Uber pickup zone, or the RoissyBus / Les Cars Air France queue. Use train and tram transfers where they exist: the RER B from CDG to central Paris is €11.80; the Orlyval and Orlybus are €9.50–11.50; the Nice tram from the airport to the city is €1.70; the Marseille Provence shuttle bus is €9–10. These skip the scam entirely. When you do take a taxi, agree on the posted *tarif forfaitaire* out loud (say the number and have the driver confirm) before you close the door, and if the meter deviates or the app route changes, speak up immediately. The fixed fares and transit prices cited in this pattern are set annually by *arrêté préfectoral* and by the regional transport authority; verify the current figures at the official airport or *préfecture* website before you rely on them.

*You will see this pattern in: the CDG and Orly taxi clandestin scams (Paris), the RER B luggage pickpocket, the Nice airport tram overcharge, the Marseille Provence airport and Gare Saint-Charles approach teams, the Bordeaux-Mérignac taxi-meter variants, the Toulouse-Blagnac flat-fare switch, the Lyon-Saint-Exupéry TGV-to-taxi handoff, the Geneva–Chamonix clandestin shuttle (Chamonix), and the “broken card reader” ATM-walk across the country.*

## Pattern 5: An official is an official in a station, not on a sidewalk

The fifth pattern is the stranger in authority. Real French police — *Police nationale* in dark navy uniforms, *Gendarmerie nationale* in dark blue with red piping, *Police municipale* in city-specific uniforms, and the *BAC* (Brigade anti-criminalité) plainclothes teams — will always take you to a station for any document or wallet check. They will never examine your cash on the street. They will never photograph your wallet. They will never tell you to transfer money to a “safe account” by phone. If any of those things is happening to you, you are being scammed.

The *faux policier* (fake plainclothes police) scam runs across France. Two men in ordinary clothes flash a laminated badge and tell you there have been reports of counterfeit euros in the area — would you show them your wallet so they can check? They riffle through your bills, palm a wad of cash, and hand the wallet back. You notice the missing bills only once they have turned the corner. The Paris variant concentrates in Montmartre below the funicular, the Trocadéro, and the Châtelet–Les Halles transit hub. The Nice variant runs the same script on the Promenade and on the road up to Le Château. *Le Parisien* and *Nice-Matin* have published multiple arrest follow-ups with named suspects and recovered cash.

Two related patterns extend this into other kinds of authority. The *faux neveu* or *arnaque aux grands-parents* (fake-relative / grandparent phone scam) has reached every major French city — an impostor calls an elderly resident claiming to be a grandchild in trouble, needing a bank transfer for bail, hospital costs, or an accident. The DGCCRF and Info Escroqueries (0 805 805 817) document hundreds of attempts a month. A second variant is the *arnaque au faux conseiller bancaire* — a caller claims to be from your bank’s fraud department and asks you to validate a transaction or read out a one-time code. Your bank will never ask this.

The defense is simple and universal. If anyone on the street identifies themselves as an official and wants to examine your wallet, your cash, your documents, or your phone, refuse and walk into the nearest shop or hotel lobby. Say calmly: “Allons au commissariat” (let’s go to the police station). A real officer will have no problem with this. A fake one will leave. If they produce a badge, ask to see it close up and note the number; a real *carte professionnelle* has a tricolor band and a badge number that can be verified at the *préfecture*. For phone versions of the scam — a cold call from someone claiming to be the police, the bank, or a utility — hang up and call back using the number from the organization’s published website or the back of your bank card, never the number the caller gave you.

*You will see this pattern in: the Montmartre and Trocadéro faux policier ring (Paris), the Châtelet–Les Halles plainclothes variant, the Promenade des Anglais wallet-check (Nice), the Monaco-border variant in Menton and Roquebrune (Nice), the Vieux Port approach (Marseille), the arnaque aux grands-parents phone scam everywhere, and the faux conseiller bancaire banking-fraud call.*

## Pattern 6: Money off the platform is money gone

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch by reading the fine print after you have committed — so this pattern is really about what to pin down before you click *Confirmer*.

You book a Chamonix chalet online, pay in full by bank transfer, and arrive to find a locked gate and an unreachable host. You book a Cannes villa on Instagram from what looks like a legitimate luxury-rental account; the property is real, but it was booked by the owner directly a week ago, and the “broker” who took your wire is a fraud. You pick up a rental car at Nice Airport and on return the attendant points at a scratch under the bumper and charges €300 to your deposit before you can argue. You book a Riviera speedboat tour in Saint-Tropez; the sea is too rough and the tour is “canceled” but the operator keeps the full fare and refuses a refund. You buy what you believe is the Eurostar ticket from a reseller at Gare du Nord for €250 when the official price direct from SNCF is €120. You cross the line of the Paris *ZFE-m* (Zone à Faibles Émissions mobilité) in a rental car without a valid Crit’Air sticker and a €68 fine arrives at your home address three months later.

All of these work because you committed before you knew the terms — and then the terms, the rules, the venue, or the platform were not what you thought they were.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction you make while abroad. Book accommodation only through platforms that offer pay-through-platform guarantees and dispute resolution (Airbnb, Booking.com, Hotels.com, Vrbo); never wire a deposit and never pay cash off-platform on arrival. Verify any property on Google Street View before you depart — a legitimate *gîte* or B&B has visible signage or an intercom label at the claimed address; a scam property does not exist. Pay every rental, excursion, or hotel by credit card (Visa, Mastercard, American Express) rather than debit, cash, PayPal Friends & Family, or bank transfer — only a credit card gives you a chargeback right under the EU consumer-protection framework, and you can usually reverse a fraudulent charge within thirty days. Never agree to a “small surcharge to pay by card”; EU PSD2 rules bar merchant surcharges on most consumer card payments, and a legitimate operator will not ask. Photograph rental cars from every angle before you drive off; film a slow 360-degree video with the date visible. Before buying any ticket — to the Eiffel Tower, the Louvre, the Palace of Versailles, the Palais des Papes, the Mont-Saint-Michel Abbey, or any museum — buy from the operator’s official website or app, never from a reseller on the street, inside a “Tourist Office”-branded storefront, or on a Facebook ad. Know the real price so you can spot the markup.

### One more: the Crit’Air / ZFE trap

France’s *ZFE-m* (Zone à Faibles Émissions mobilité) is the French analog of Italy’s ZTL — a low-emission zone that restricts older vehicles inside central Paris, Lyon, Marseille, Strasbourg, Toulouse, Montpellier, and most large-city centers. Every vehicle entering must display a valid *Crit’Air* sticker (€3.67 direct from certificat-air.gouv.fr). Rental cars usually have the sticker, but a missing or invalid sticker triggers a €68 fine by mail — passed through by the rental company with an administrative surcharge that often drives the total past €150 per entry. If you are driving into a French city center, verify the Crit’Air sticker is on the windshield before you leave the rental lot, and know the city’s ZFE rules before you cross the ring road. When in doubt, park outside and take public transport in.

*You will see this pattern in: off-platform chalet-rental fraud (Chamonix), Instagram villa-broker scams (Cannes, Saint-Tropez), the Nice Airport phantom-damage rental pattern, the Gare du Nord Eurostar reseller markup, the Riviera speedboat no-refund weather trap (Saint-Tropez), the Mont-Saint-Michel ferry and shuttle reseller markup, the ZFE-m Crit’Air trap (Paris, Lyon, Marseille, Strasbourg, Toulouse), and the “broken card reader” ATM-walk across the country.*
