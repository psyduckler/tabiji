# Appendix A: French Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in France. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, followed by the **French**, and a simple pronunciation guide in italics.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. If you can, say the first three or four out loud a few times. You will feel much less awkward using them when the moment comes.

A note on pronunciation: French vowels are clean and held briefly; every vowel is always pronounced the same way — *a* is “ah,” *i* is “ee,” *o* is “oh,” *u* is a tight rounded “oo” (say “ee” with pursed lips), and *e* at the end of a word is usually silent. French has four famous nasal vowels — *an/en* (“ahn” through the nose, no final consonant), *on* (“ohn” nasal), *in/un* (“ahn” lighter, nasal) — and you do not need to master them, but knowing they exist helps you not over-pronounce the final *n*. The letter *r* is a soft sound in the back of the throat, not a rolled one; if you can’t manage it, an English “r” is fine. The letter *h* is always silent. Final consonants are usually silent (*vous* sounds like “voo,” not “vooss”) with a handful of common exceptions. Stress falls evenly, with a slight emphasis on the final syllable of a phrase. None of this has to be perfect — French people will forgive a strong accent as long as the key words are clear.

A note on formality: all the phrases below use the *vous* (formal) form, which is what you should use with any stranger. When you see *monsieur* or *madame*, that is the default polite address. The informal *tu* is for friends, family, and children — never for strangers, waiters, or police, no matter what age they are. Using *vous* automatically registers you as polite, which is the correct register for anything defensive.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**
Combien ça coûte?
*Kohm-BYAN sah KOOT?*

Or, more casual but equally understood:
C’est combien?
*Seh kohm-BYAN?*

**Please show me the menu with prices.**
Montrez-moi la carte avec les prix, s’il vous plaît.
*Mohn-TRAY mwah lah KART ah-VEK lay PREE, seel voo PLAY.*

Use this before you sit down at any restaurant. French law requires an *affichage obligatoire* — a visible price menu posted outside every restaurant. Asking to see it before you sit is the single most reliable way to avoid a Saint-Tropez, Mont-Saint-Michel, or Riviera-terrace overcharging surprise.

**Is there a cover charge? And is service included?**
Y a-t-il un couvert? Et le service est-il compris?
*Yah-TEEL un koo-VEHR? Eh luh sehr-VEES eh-TEEL kohm-PREE?*

The two words that matter are *couvert* (a per-head cover charge for bread and water, legal but usually under €3 at fair places and €4–7 in tourist traps) and *service* (service charge, which is legally included in all French menu prices — the phrase *service compris* is required by law). Asking both questions before you sit is how you head off an unposted cover or a surprise “service supplement.”

**What is the price per 100 grams?**
Quel est le prix au 100 grammes?
*Kell eh luh PREE oh sahn GRAHM?*

Seafood and other items priced *au kilo* or *au 100 grammes* should be weighed in front of you before cooking. A legitimate restaurant will show you the whole fish on a scale and quote a total in euros. Demand this — the surprise by-weight bill is one of the most common scams in this book.

**Do you accept credit cards?**
Acceptez-vous la carte bancaire?
*Ahk-sehp-TAY voo lah KART bahn-KAIR?*

If the answer is “oui” but the card reader later turns out to be “broken,” you are being scammed. See “Scam in progress” below.

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**
Non, merci.
*Nohn, mair-SEE.*

Said firmly and without slowing your pace. No elaboration is needed. French people understand *non merci* as a complete refusal; repeating it louder or softer does not strengthen it.

**I’m not interested.**
Ça ne m’intéresse pas.
*Sah nuh man-tay-RESS pah.*

Stronger than *non merci*. Use this when a *rabatteur* (tout), a clipboard-holder, or a bracelet-seller keeps talking after the first refusal.

**I’m leaving.**
Je m’en vais.
*Zhuh mahn VAY.*

**I don’t want this.**
Je n’en veux pas.
*Zhuh nahn vuh PAH.*

**Leave me alone.**
Laissez-moi tranquille.
*Lay-SAY mwah trahn-KEEL.*

This is the correct firm-but-polite way to say “back off” to someone who is in your personal space. The verb *laisser* means “to leave” in the sense of “let be,” and you are telling them to leave you in peace. It carries weight without sounding aggressive.

**Don’t touch me.**
Ne me touchez pas.
*Nuh muh too-SHAY pah.*

The phrase that goes with Pattern 1 in the red-flag chapter. Say this clearly and step back. Most pickpocket pairs will disengage immediately rather than escalate.

**Excuse me, please let me through.**
Excusez-moi, laissez-moi passer.
*Ex-koo-ZAY mwah, lay-SAY mwah pah-SAY.*

The *excusez-moi* softens it so that the person blocking your path hears a polite request rather than a command. If they do not move, repeat it once and keep walking.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I’m calling the police.**
J’appelle la police.
*Zhah-PELL lah poh-LEES.*

Or, in rural areas where the *Gendarmerie* is the police service:
J’appelle la gendarmerie.
*Zhah-PELL lah zhahn-dar-muh-REE.*

This is the single most useful phrase in this entire appendix. Both phrases work — *la police* (Police nationale) works everywhere, and *la gendarmerie* is correct outside major cities. Say it clearly. The word *police* or *gendarmerie* changes the atmosphere in a room immediately, and most scam operators back down the moment they hear it.

**I’ll call my bank.**
J’appelle ma banque.
*Zhah-PELL mah BAHNK.*

Say this if a restaurant or taxi driver disputes a credit-card transaction. Calling your card issuer from the table is a legitimate move and signals that the charge is going to be disputed.

**I was scammed. Please help.**
Men say: J’ai été escroqué. Aidez-moi, s’il vous plaît.
*Zhay eh-tay ess-kroh-KAY. Ay-DAY mwah, seel voo PLAY.*

Women say: J’ai été escroquée. Aidez-moi, s’il vous plaît.
*Zhay eh-tay ess-kroh-KAY. Ay-DAY mwah, seel voo PLAY.*

French past participles agree with the subject’s gender in writing (*-é* for men, *-ée* for women), but the pronunciation is identical. An alternative phrasing that locals use just as often:

On m’a arnaqué / arnaquée.
*Ohn mah ar-nah-KAY.*

(“Someone scammed me.”) The word *arnaque* is the specific everyday word for “scam” and communicates what happened with more urgency than the formal *escroquerie*. For pickpocketing specifically: men say *on m’a volé* and women say *on m’a volée* — “I’ve been robbed.”

**Please give me an itemized receipt.**
Je voudrais une facture détaillée, s’il vous plaît.
*Zhuh voo-DRAY oon fahk-TOOR day-tah-YAY, seel voo PLAY.*

In France, restaurant bills are required by law to itemize every dish with a price; a single-total bill with just a *couvert* line and nothing else is illegal and should be refused. Asking for the *facture détaillée* (itemized invoice) forces the establishment to account for every item, and it is the most polite version of “I do not accept this charge.” A legitimate place will hand it to you without complaint. A tourist trap will stall.

**I didn’t order this.**
Je n’ai pas commandé ça.
*Zhuh nay pah koh-mahn-DAY sah.*

Say this calmly about any unrequested *pain*, *eau*, *amuse-bouche*, or *apéritif* that appears on your bill. In France you are not legally required to pay for food you did not order, though you will usually need to say so explicitly.

**This is not what we agreed.**
Ce n’est pas ce que nous avions convenu.
*Suh neh pah suh kuh noo-zah-vyohn kohn-vuh-NOO.*

A short, firm phrase that accuses nobody but puts the burden of explanation on the other party. Use this for taxi fares that exceed a quoted price, hotel charges that differ from the booking, and tour-operator price-creep.

**I’ll file a complaint.**
Je vais déposer une plainte.
*Zhuh vay day-poh-ZAY oon PLAHNT.*

*Déposer une plainte* is the formal criminal-complaint filing — stronger than the lighter *main courante* (just a register entry). A tourist saying this credibly — especially combined with *j’appelle la police* — is taken seriously.

## Getting help

**Where’s the nearest police station?**
Où est le commissariat le plus proche?
*Oo eh luh koh-mee-sar-YAH luh ploo PROSH?*

Or for the *Gendarmerie* (rural areas and some Paris suburbs):
Où est la brigade de gendarmerie la plus proche?
*Oo eh lah bree-GAHD duh zhahn-dar-muh-REE lah ploo PROSH?*

**I want an official taxi.**
Je voudrais un taxi officiel.
*Zhuh voo-DRAY un tahk-SEE oh-fee-SYELL.*

Use this at airports and stations where unlicensed drivers approach you. “Official” in France means a white car (or any color with a white roof sign labeled TAXI), a visible meter, a posted license number on the dashboard and rear door, and a lit rooftop TAXI sign. Anything else is a *taxi clandestin*.

**Please call 112.**
Appelez le 112, s’il vous plaît.
*Ah-puh-LAY luh sahn DOOZ, seel voo PLAY.*

Or just shout:
Au secours! Appelez le 112!
*Oh suh-KOOR! Ah-puh-LAY luh sahn DOOZ!*

112 is the pan-European emergency number and routes you to the appropriate service (Police nationale, Gendarmerie, ambulance, fire) depending on what you say. *Sahn DOOZ* (the French way to say “112”) is easy to shout under stress, and every French adult recognizes it immediately. The related direct lines are 17 (Police nationale), 15 (SAMU medical), and 18 (Pompiers — firefighters and medical rescue).

**Help! Thief!**
Au secours! Au voleur!
*Oh suh-KOOR! Oh voh-LUHR!*

Shouted in a crowded street, this is understood by every bystander as “someone stole from me, help me stop them.” The words *au voleur* are the classic French hue-and-cry and carry legal weight — a bystander who helps you restrain a thief is protected under French law (*flagrant délit*).

**Does anyone speak English?**
Est-ce que quelqu’un parle anglais?
*Ess-kuh kell-KUN parl ahn-GLAY?*

**Please help me.**
Aidez-moi, s’il vous plaît.
*Ay-DAY mwah, seel voo PLAY.*

## Reading euro amounts on French bills

French receipts and price tags use the comma as a decimal and the space (or period) as a thousands separator — the reverse of American convention. This book writes prices in the English style (€1.50, €1,500), but in France, the bill you’re handed will use the French style — watch for the reversal and don’t panic when you see it.

- On a French bill, **€1,50** means one euro fifty cents — not one thousand five hundred.
- On a French bill, **€1 500** or **€1.500** means one thousand five hundred euros — not one euro fifty.
- **€1 500,00** is the fully formatted “one thousand five hundred euros exactly” — that’s the style you’ll see on a restaurant *addition*.

| Amount | Written | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| €1 | un euro | *un UH-roh* |
| €5 | cinq euros | *SAHNK UH-roh* |
| €10 | dix euros | *DEES UH-roh* |
| €20 | vingt euros | *VAN-tuh-roh* |
| €50 | cinquante euros | *SAHN-kahnt UH-roh* |
| €100 | cent euros | *SAHN UH-roh* |
| €500 | cinq cents euros | *SAHNK sahn-ZUH-roh* |
| €1,000 | mille euros | *MEEL UH-roh* |

If a waiter or cashier tells you a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it out:

**Please write the number down.**
Écrivez le chiffre, s’il vous plaît.
*Ay-kree-VAY luh SHEEF-ruh, seel voo PLAY.*

They will almost always oblige.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In France, polite firmness works better than raised voices — though unlike in some other European countries, a raised voice is viewed as a loss of composure and rarely helps your case. A calm *non merci* is already a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *laissez-moi tranquille* and *ne me touchez pas* are both firm without being rude, and *j’appelle la police* is the firmest card in the deck.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar. Calm and firm beats loud and uncertain every time.
