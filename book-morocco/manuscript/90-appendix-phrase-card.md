# Appendix A: Darija Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Morocco. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, the **Darija** text in Latin transliteration (the form most travelers can actually read aloud), the Arabic script, and a simple pronunciation guide in italics. Where French is the more practical language for the same phrase — and in Morocco it often is — the French equivalent is given as well.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. The Arabic script is also useful — copy a phrase into a note, and you can hand the screen to anyone whose dialect you cannot follow.

## A note on pronunciation

Darija — the spoken Moroccan vernacular — is different enough from Modern Standard Arabic and from Egyptian or Gulf Arabic that even fluent Arabic speakers from elsewhere often struggle. It draws heavily on Berber, French, and Spanish vocabulary, drops most short vowels, and uses several consonants that English speakers find unfamiliar. Three are worth knowing:

- *gh* — a soft throat-click, similar to the French *r*. Practice on the word *Maghreb* (the local name for North Africa). It is the same sound.
- *kh* — a harder back-of-throat sound, like the *ch* in Scottish *loch* or German *Bach*.
- *q* — a deeper *k* made at the back of the throat. *Marrakech* is properly *Mar-ra-kesh*; the local pronunciation is *Mar-ra-kesh* with a brief throat-stop on the *q*.

You do not have to get any of these perfect. A Moroccan listener will accommodate a foreign accent, the same way an American shopkeeper would accommodate a French speaker asking *"how much?"*

## A note on politeness

Moroccans use the word *aafak* (please) constantly, and adding it to any phrase softens it without weakening it. Refusing in Darija is socially acceptable; the local conflict-resolution norm is firm but warm, and walking away mid-conversation after a clear *"la, shukran"* is not rude. Raised voices are not common in everyday Moroccan exchanges and tend to escalate, not de-escalate, a situation.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**  
*Bsh-hal?* / *Bshhal hada?*  
بشحال؟ / بشحال هادا؟  
*B-sh-HAL — b-sh-HAL HA-da?*  
French: *C'est combien?*

**Please turn on the meter.**  
*Aafak khalli l-compteur.*  
عافاك خلي الكونتور  
*AH-fak KHA-li al-COMP-ter*  
French: *Le compteur, s'il vous plaît.*

Say this the instant you get into any petit taxi. If the driver refuses, get out — there is always a next taxi.

**One individual seat — seventy dirhams.**  
*Place individuelle — sebʿīn dirham.*  
*PLAS in-di-vid-WELL — sib-EEN dir-HAM*  
French: *Une place individuelle, soixante-dix dirhams.*

The grand-taxi mantra. Say it loud enough for the driver and the other passengers to hear. The other passengers are your allies — none of them is paying tourist rates.

**Can I see the menu, please?**  
*Aafak, ana bghit la carte.*  
عافاك، أنا بغيت الكارت  
*AH-fak — A-na BGHIT la CART*  
French: *Je voudrais la carte, s'il vous plaît.*

Say this *before* you sit down on a Moroccan terrace. Photograph the menu prices on your phone. Confirm aloud what you are ordering matches the photo.

**Is there a service fee?**  
*Wash kayn frais de service?*  
واش كاين فري دو سيرفيس؟  
*WASH kayn fray duh sair-VEES?*  
French: *Y a-t-il un frais de service?*

The "you ordered bread you didn't ask for, here's a 30 MAD charge" play. Asking up-front kills it.

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**  
*La, shukran.*  
لا، شكرا  
*LAH, SHU-kran*  
French: *Non, merci.*

The single most useful phrase in this appendix. Firm, polite, understood everywhere in Morocco. Say it and keep walking. Walking away is not rude.

**I'm not interested.**  
*Ma bghit-sh.*  
ما بغيتش  
*ma BGHIT-sh*  
French: *Ça ne m'intéresse pas.*

**Please leave me alone.**  
*Aafak, salli-ni.* / *Khalli-ni, aafak.*  
عافاك سالني / خاليني عافاك  
*AH-fak — KHA-li-ni*  
French: *Laissez-moi tranquille, s'il vous plaît.*

**Don't touch me.**  
*Ma t-qiss-nish.*  
ما تقيسنيش  
*ma t-KISS-nish*  
French: *Ne me touchez pas.*

The henna-ambush phrase. Said the moment a hand reaches for your wrist on Djemaa el-Fna or Plaza Uta el-Hammam.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I'm calling the police.**  
*Ana ghadi n-aaiyiṭ ʿla l-bulīs.*  
أنا غادي نعيط على البوليس  
*A-na GHA-di n-aa-YIT ʿla al-bu-LEES*  
French: *J'appelle la police.*

The firmest card in the deck. **19** is the Sûreté Nationale's urban-police line; **177** reaches the Gendarmerie Royale on highways and rural roads. Say the phrase, hold up your phone, and dial. Real Moroccan police stations are walking distance from any tourist corridor.

**I'll call my bank.**  
*Ana ghadi n-aaiyiṭ ʿla l-banka.*  
أنا غادي نعيط على البانكا  
*A-na GHA-di n-aa-YIT ʿla l-BANK-a*

**I was scammed. Please help.**  
*Ghashshu-ni. ʿawn-uni, aafak.*  
غشوني. عاونوني عافاك  
*GHASH-shu-ni. AAW-nu-ni, AH-fak*  
French: *On m'a arnaqué. Aidez-moi, s'il vous plaît.*

The verb *ghashsh* (to deceive, to cheat) is the Darija term for being scammed and communicates the situation with more urgency than the English translation suggests.

**Please give me an itemized receipt.**  
*Aafak, ʿṭi-ni ticket-bordereau.*  
عافاك، عطيني تيكي  
*AH-fak, AA-ti-ni ti-KAY*  
French: *Donnez-moi un reçu détaillé, s'il vous plaît.*

A Moroccan business issuing a printed receipt with line-items is harder to pad than one writing a number on a napkin.

**I didn't order this.**  
*Ma ṭlabt-sh hada.*  
ما طلبتش هادا  
*ma TLABT-sh HA-da*  
French: *Je n'ai pas commandé cela.*

The bread-basket-on-the-table response.

**This is not what we agreed.**  
*Hada mashi lli ttafaq-na ʿli-h.*  
هادا ماشي اللي اتفقنا عليه  
*HA-da MA-shi LLEE tta-FAQ-na ʿLEE-h*  
French: *Ce n'est pas ce dont nous étions convenus.*

The desert-tour bait-and-switch phrase, the camel-trek time-upcharge phrase, and the riad-room-changed-on-arrival phrase. Said calmly, with the booking confirmation on your phone screen.

## Getting help

**Where is the nearest police station?**  
*Fīn aqrab commissariat?*  
فين أقرب كوميساريا؟  
*FEEN AQ-rab ko-mi-sa-ree-AT?*  
French: *Où est le commissariat de police le plus proche?*

Every taxi driver in Morocco will know the answer.

**I want to file a report.**  
*Bghit n-dīr shikāya.*  
بغيت ندير شكاية  
*BGHIT n-DEER shi-KA-ya*  
French: *Je veux porter plainte.*

The Moroccan legal-report noun is *shikaya* (Arabic) or *plainte* (French). You will leave the station with a printed report bearing a reference number — keep this for your insurance and credit-card disputes.

**Does anyone speak English?**  
*Wash kayn shi-wahed kay-tkellem ngliziya?*  
واش كاين شي واحد كيتكلم نغليزية؟  
*WASH kayn SHEE wa-HID kay-t-KEL-lem n-gli-ZEE-ya?*  
French: *Quelqu'un parle anglais?*

In any urban Moroccan police station, ferry-port office, or major hospital, someone usually does — at minimum, French, often English.

**Help! Thief!**  
*ʿAwn-uni! Sarrak!*  
عاونوني! سراق!  
*AAW-nu-ni — sa-RRAK!*

Shout *sarrak* if your bag is being lifted in the street. Moroccan crowd-response on this word is reliable; the bystander will usually move toward the thief.

## Reading dirham amounts

The Moroccan dirham is abbreviated **MAD** in international banking and **DH** or **dh** locally on receipts and signage. Decimal separator is the comma (35,50 DH = 35 dirhams 50 centimes); thousands separator is the space (1 800 DH). Coins run 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10 dirhams; banknotes run 20, 50, 100, 200 dirhams. ATMs typically dispense 100s and 200s.

| Amount | Darija | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 dirham | *waḥed dirham* | *WA-hid dir-HAM* |
| 5 dirhams | *khamsa dirham* | *KHAM-sa dir-HAM* |
| 10 dirhams | *ʿashra dirham* | *AASH-ra dir-HAM* |
| 50 dirhams | *khamsīn dirham* | *kham-SEEN dir-HAM* |
| 100 dirhams | *miyya dirham* | *MEE-ya dir-HAM* |
| 500 dirhams | *khams miyya dirham* | *KHAMS MEE-ya dir-HAM* |
| 1,000 dirhams | *alf dirham* | *ALF dir-HAM* |

If a vendor quotes a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it:

**Please write the number down.**  
*Aafak, ktib l-ʿadad.*  
عافاك، كتيب العدد  
*AH-fak, k-TIB l-ʿA-dad*  
French: *Pouvez-vous écrire le chiffre, s'il vous plaît?*

A separate trap to know about: prices quoted in **euros** at souk shops in Marrakech, Fez, and Essaouira are almost always 30–50% above the dirham equivalent. Always ask for the dirham price and pay in dirhams.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Morocco, polite firmness works better than raised voices. Moroccans value warmth in transactions and tend to read raised volume as panic — which the scammer can use. The escalation ladder runs in three steps: the first refusal (*la, shukran*) walks past most attempts; if it doesn't, the stronger refusal (*ma bghit-sh*, *salli-ni*) closes most others; and the final card (*ana ghadi n-aaiyiṭ ʿla l-bulīs*, with phone in hand) closes the rest.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no.
