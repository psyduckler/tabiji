# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The 61 scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: A friendly stranger who approaches you first is running a script

This is the single most important habit you can take to Morocco. The default setting in the Marrakech medina, in Fez al-Bali, in the blue alleys of Chefchaouen, on Plaza Uta el-Hammam, around the Place Moulay Hassan in Essaouira, on Bab Boujloud in Fez — the default is that a Moroccan who genuinely wants to help you with directions will tell you the answer in two sentences and walk on. The default is *not* that they walk with you for ten minutes and bring you to their cousin's shop.

If a stranger speaking decent English or French steps out of a side alley and opens with *"my friend, where are you from? Where are you going?"* — you are five seconds into a script. The most common version is the *"that way is closed"* play that runs in every Moroccan medina. The man is friendly, the script is fluent, the route detours through three or four turns into a *"family" cooperative shop* — carpets in Fez and Marrakech, argan oil and spice in Essaouira, Berber jewelry and tagine pottery in Chefchaouen — and the *"guide"* expects 100–200 MAD when you reach the destination, which was usually open the whole time. This pattern is documented relentlessly on r/Morocco; one widely-cited TripAdvisor review captured it as *"every day was relentless scamming of road closed or we are from your hotel."*

A specific variant deserves its own callout. The *"I work at your riad"* opener is run by men who sit near the riad-cluster entrances at Bab Doukkala in Marrakech or at the Bab Boujloud and Tala'a Kebira ends of the Fez medina. They identify a tourist with a daypack pulling out a phone, ask which riad, and announce that *they* are from that riad (or that the manager sent them). They then walk you past your actual riad to a "better one" or to a shop. Real riad staff do not look for arriving guests on the street. If your riad has agreed an airport-pickup or a *grand-taxi* meet at Bab Doukkala, your name will be on a small written sign held by a person waiting at the agreed spot — never spoken to you by a stranger who appears to know you.

A second variant is the henna-tattoo wrist-grab on Djemaa el-Fna in Marrakech, on Plaza Uta el-Hammam in Chefchaouen, and at the Bab al-Ain medina entrance. A woman in traditional dress steps off the curb, says *"a gift, no money — for luck"* and a syringe-style applicator is on the back of your hand before your brain catches up. The price is then 200–300 MAD, and two male relatives appear on cue.

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: keep your phone out of sight, do not break stride for anyone who opens with a question, say *"la, shukran"* clearly, and keep walking. If you genuinely need directions, ask a shopkeeper already at work in their store — never a man who approaches you.

*You will see this pattern in: Marrakech "That Way's Closed" Fake Guide, Marrakech Henna Tattoo Ambush, Fez Medina Faux-Guide, Chefchaouen Medina Guide Maze Trap, Chefchaouen Henna Tattoo Ambush, Tangier Petit Socco Faux-Guide, Rabat Kasbah des Oudayas Fake Guide, Essaouira Place Moulay Hassan Gnawa Music Donation, Spanish Mosque Sunset Tip Demand.*

## Pattern 2: "The meter is broken" — or the transport-overcharge play

Morocco has two parallel taxi systems that scam differently. **Petit taxis** are the small red, blue, or yellow city taxis (color depends on the city — red in Casablanca, blue in Rabat, yellow in Fez, ochre in Marrakech) and they are metered by law. **Grands taxis** are the older Mercedes sedans that run shared inter-city routes at fixed per-seat fares. Both are running scams; the scripts are different.

The petit-taxi scam is the meter refusal. You arrive at Mohammed V (CMN) in Casablanca, Marrakech-Menara (RAK), Tangier-Ibn Battuta (TNG), or Agadir-Al Massira (AGA). The driver looks at your luggage and quotes a flat rate — 250–400 MAD into Casablanca, 100–250 MAD into Marrakech, 250–500 MAD into Tangier, 200–400 MAD into Agadir — when the metered fare on the same trip would be 60–80% lower. The driver's framing is almost always *"the meter is broken,"* *"there's a night tariff supplement,"* or *"airport rate, fixed."* All three are scripts. The defense is single-line: *"avec le compteur, s'il vous plaît"* (with the meter, please) said before getting in. If the driver refuses, walk to the next taxi. There is always a next taxi.

The grand-taxi scam is the seat overcharge. The Tangier-to-Chefchaouen run is officially 70 MAD per *place individuelle* (individual seat) in a 6-seat shared sedan, or 420 MAD for a *grand taxi privé* (private hire of all six seats). Drivers at Tangier's bus station and at the Chefchaouen Bab al-Ain rank routinely quote 700 MAD per person to tourists who don't know the official rate. The Marrakech-to-Essaouira and the Ouarzazate-to-Marrakech routes run the same play. The defense is to know the per-seat rate before you arrive at the rank, and to say *"place individuelle, soixante-dix dirhams"* (individual seat, seventy dirhams) clearly enough that the driver and the other passengers all hear it. If the driver refuses, the next driver will accept.

A subset variant is the ride-hail cancel-and-cash play. Inwi's CarHail and Heetch (the locally-licensed apps) both work in Casablanca and Rabat. A driver accepts the booking, calls you, and says *"the app isn't working, I can't see the map — pay cash and I'll cancel"* — then quotes a higher rate than the app would have charged. The defense is to cancel the booking yourself and re-book; never pay a ride-hail driver in cash off-app.

The defense is short and universal: use the meter or the app, know the per-seat grand-taxi rate before you arrive at the rank, and never get in a vehicle whose price has not already been agreed.

*You will see this pattern in: Casablanca CMN Petit Taxi Refusal, Marrakech Petit Taxi Meter Scam, Tangier Petit Taxi Refusal, Agadir Petit Taxi Refusal, Rabat-Salé Airport Petit Taxi Refusal, Tangier–Chefchaouen Grand Taxi Overcharge, Marrakech–Essaouira Grand Taxi Overcharge, Ouarzazate Grand Taxi Per-Seat Scam.*

## Pattern 3: Prices that aren't posted will surprise you later

This pattern hits every Moroccan tourist corridor with two signature variants: the terrace-cafe overcharge and the souk-pricing pitch.

The terrace-cafe overcharge runs hardest on Plaza Uta el-Hammam in Chefchaouen, on Place Moulay Hassan in Essaouira, around Djemaa el-Fna in Marrakech, on Place du Petit Socco in Tangier, and inside the Habous Quarter in Casablanca. The mechanic is the same: tourists are seated, no menu appears, food is brought, and the bill at the end is 2× to 4× the price the same dish runs uphill or one street back. A tagine that should be 60–80 MAD bills at 200; a salade marocaine at 30 MAD bills at 80; a "service" charge of 30 MAD for a basket of bread you didn't order appears; a bottle of water comes at 30 MAD instead of 8. The Brigade Touristique has flagged this in *Le Matin* coverage on multiple occasions, but enforcement is uneven and the script keeps running. The defense is a single firm habit: ask for the menu *before* you sit down, take a phone photo of the prices on the menu, and confirm aloud that what you are ordering matches what you photographed.

The souk-pricing variant is the "we are a women's cooperative" or "this is the original *spice souk*" pitch with no posted prices. Marrakech's Souk Semmarine and Souk el-Attarine, Fez's spice section near the Qarawiyyin, and Essaouira's argan-oil belt all run versions. The price quoted is in euros, the conversion to dirhams is bad, and the product is often diluted. The defense is to buy argan oil only from places your riad has recommended, look for AMONT certification on cooperative bottles, and assume that any quoted euro price is already 30–50% above the dirham equivalent.

The defense is a single habit, repeated before every transaction: ask for the price in writing, or point to a posted menu. If neither exists, walk.

*You will see this pattern in: Plaza Uta el-Hammam Cafe Overcharge, Marrakech Djemaa el-Fna Restaurant Overcharge, Fake Argan Oil and Spice Shop, Essaouira Fake Argan Oil Cooperative, Tangier Petit Socco Cafe Overcharge, Casablanca Habous Quarter Two-Price, Place Moulay Hassan Cafe Overcharge.*

## Pattern 4: The deposit-and-damage equipment claim

Morocco's signature version of this universal pattern runs at three rental venues: the quad-bike rental booths at the base of the Erg Chebbi dunes outside Merzouga, the jet-ski stalls on Agadir's central beach in summer, and the camel-trek stations at Merzouga and (occasionally) at Plage de Diabat outside Essaouira.

The script is the same in each case. You rent the equipment, pay a deposit (usually 500–2,000 MAD or your passport — never give the passport), use the equipment, return it. The operator inspects it, finds a scratch or a dent or a "broken steering," and announces a damage fee of 1,000–3,000 MAD. The fee did not exist when you rented; the damage was either pre-existing or invented. If you protest, the operator becomes loud and brings two friends over to "explain." The defense is photographic and mandatory: before you ride away, take an eight-angle smartphone video walk-around of the equipment with timestamps visible, and refuse to leave any deposit larger than 500 MAD or any document larger than a photocopy of your passport's photo page (never the original).

A close variant is the camel-trek photo upcharge. You agree on a 2-hour camel ride at sunset for an agreed price (200–400 MAD per person is standard). Halfway through, the guide stops the camels, says *"now we take photo, with you on top, special price 100 dirhams each photo,"* and the price multiplies. The defense is to agree the per-person all-in price before mounting and put it in writing — even a photo of a piece of paper with the figure works.

The defense is photographic, and it is mandatory. Eight angles. Timestamp visible. Deposit capped. Passport stays at the riad.

*You will see this pattern in: Merzouga Quad Bike Damage Surcharge, Agadir Jet-Ski Deposit Hold, Merzouga Camel Trek Time-and-Photo Upcharge, Essaouira Jet-Ski Damage Claim.*

## Pattern 5: A "police officer" or "guide" is legitimate only from an official badge

Real Moroccan police carry a numbered metal badge (the Sûreté Nationale) or a green-and-white *Gendarmerie Royale* uniform on rural roads. Real licensed tour guides carry a national ONMT (Office National Marocain du Tourisme) ID badge and have been trained at the Institut Supérieur International du Tourisme in Tangier. Anything else is theater.

The flagship fake-officer scam is the Chefchaouen hash-tout shakedown. A young man on a medina alley offers cannabis (real, often pre-rolled, priced in the 50–200 MAD range — and remember, possession is illegal in Morocco regardless of the Rif's growing reputation). Within two to ten minutes, a man in a short jacket steps out and identifies himself as a police officer. He may flash a real-looking ID or wave a wallet. He says cannabis is illegal — which it is — and that you are going to the station unless you settle here. The opening number is 2,000 MAD; the closing number depends on your wallet and how rattled you look. r/Morocco threads document this scam running in Chefchaouen continuously since at least 2018, and the US State Department warns about it directly.

A milder variant runs at the Aït Benhaddou approach, where a man at the parking area collects a "20 MAD entry fee" from arriving visitors. Aït Benhaddou has no entry fee — the river crossing is free and the village is open. The defense is to walk past anyone collecting fees outside an official ONMT-marked ticket booth.

The Faux-Guide variant is the licensed-guide impersonator at the entrance to the Hassan II Mosque in Casablanca, the Bahia Palace in Marrakech, and the Mausoleum of Mohammed V in Rabat. They wear a shirt that vaguely suggests a uniform, claim they are an official guide, and demand 100–200 MAD for a "tour" that follows a public path. Real licensed guides carry a laminated ONMT card with a photo and a number; ask to see it before agreeing to anything.

The defense is simple: ask for the card. If it does not appear, the person is not real. If you are stopped on the street and the situation feels coercive, say *"je veux aller au commissariat de police"* (I want to go to the police station) and start walking toward an open shop, hotel lobby, or chain café. Real officers will accept being told you'd rather walk to the station; fakes melt away the moment the destination is named.

*You will see this pattern in: Chefchaouen Hash Tout Police Shakedown, Aït Benhaddou Fake Entry Fee, Hassan II Mosque Faux-Guide, Bahia Palace Faux-Guide, Mausoleum Faux-Guide, Marrakech Drug-Sting Variant.*

## Pattern 6: If you pay before you have the thing, you probably won't get the thing

Morocco's pre-payment risk surface is concentrated in three places: the Marrakech-to-Merzouga 3-day desert tour, hotel and riad bookings made through unverified WhatsApp/Instagram channels, and the longer-haul Marrakech-Sahara-Fez 8-to-10-day private tour offered through unlicensed operators.

The Marrakech-to-Merzouga shared-tour bait-and-switch is the canonical case. An unverified operator (the booking page is a clone of a real Tripadvisor-listed tour, or a brand-new Instagram profile) sells a 3-day tour for €280–€350 with photographs of luxury Berber camp tents, en-suite bathrooms, and a private 4×4. What arrives is a basic-tent camp shared with twenty others, a 2017 minivan that breaks down on the Tizi n'Tichka pass, and a guide who pivots the itinerary to four carpet-cooperative stops where the operator earns commission. By the time you can dispute, the operator's number has gone dark.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction you make in Morocco:

- **Book accommodation** only through platforms with chargeback guarantees (Booking.com, Agoda, Airbnb, Expedia) — never via WhatsApp, Instagram DM, or direct bank transfer to a Moroccan account
- **Book Sahara tours** only through verified operators with a published Marrakech office and visible Tripadvisor verification (Plan-It Morocco, Sahara Magic Tours, Morocco Sahara Adventures, and Get Your Guide's local-listed partners are examples that meet the bar) — expect to pay €600–€900 for a 3-day private 4×4 with a real desert camp rather than €300 for the bait-and-switch
- **Pay by credit card** (Visa, Mastercard, American Express) wherever possible — never by debit card, cash deposit, or Western Union
- **Verify any Sahara-tour deposit request** by asking for the operator's *patente* (Moroccan business-license) number; legitimate tour operators provide it without complaint

### One more: Drug possession is real exposure, regardless of the Rif's growing reputation

Cannabis is grown openly in the Rif and sold openly on Chefchaouen alleys, and it is also illegal under Moroccan law. The Sûreté Nationale and the Gendarmerie Royale both make periodic enforcement arrests, especially on the Tetouan-Chefchaouen and Tangier-Chefchaouen roads. Tourist possession is what makes the fake-cop shakedown threat work — because the threat is genuinely credible. The defense is the same as for any pre-payment scam: do not start the transaction. Decline every street offer with *"la, shukran"* and walk on.

*You will see this pattern in: Marrakech-to-Merzouga Tour Bait-and-Switch, Ouarzazate Desert Tour Bait-and-Switch, Marrakech WhatsApp Riad Booking Fraud, Tangier Hostel-Booking Wire-Transfer Scam, Chefchaouen Hash Tout Police Shakedown.*
