# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are organized by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — a tourist-trap restaurant with a padded bill, a Whistler parking lot where you just scanned a suspicious QR code, an Uber car that's demanding cash, a Gastown sidewalk confrontation with a fake monk — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. A signed credit-card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, dial **911** — the universal Canadian emergency number that reaches police, fire, and ambulance everywhere in the country. For any non-active-emergency scam (hotel-guest impersonation call after the fact, a suspicious rental-listing withdrawal, a taxi card-swap you noticed hours later), the **Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre** at **1-888-495-8501** is the nationwide reporting line; the CAFC does not investigate individual cases but aggregates reports for police action and reports to the banks.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection (Google Maps dropped-pin is ideal), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. For a Toronto card-swap fraud, the screenshot you want is the card the driver handed back — take a photo before you leave the vehicle, showing the last four digits. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward.

If your bag, phone, or wallet was just stolen in the street, shout — shopkeepers, bystanders, and passing drivers in Canadian cities are genuinely responsive, and a visible commotion draws police attention within minutes. In Toronto, Union Station's TTC supervisor office is the closest authority during a subway-related theft; in Montreal, métro station supervisors have the same role; in Vancouver, the Canada Line and Expo Line SkyTrain attendant offices the same. At all Canadian international airports, the airport's police detachment (usually marked "Airport Law Enforcement" or "Paid Duty Officer") is the closest authority.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest **police station** (for an active-day incident) or call the city's non-emergency police line (for a discovered-later incident). The defense against any Canadian scam requires a **police report number** — your credit card issuer and travel insurer will ask for it. Toronto Police non-emergency: **416-808-2222**. Montreal SPVM: **514-393-1133**. Vancouver VPD: **604-717-3321**. RCMP (for Banff, Jasper, Whistler, and most outside-major-city locations): **911** for active, or the local detachment's posted non-emergency line.

Tell the officer: *"I want to file a police report for a scam / fraud."* You will leave with a **case number** (sometimes called an "Occurrence Number" in Ontario, or a *Numéro de dossier* in Quebec). This is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

There is a nationwide online pre-filing option: the **Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre's online reporting tool** at *antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca*. This is the right starting point for any scam that is not active (i.e., the scammer is no longer present), and you can file from your hotel room. The CAFC generates a report number and forwards the case to the relevant local police service for investigation. For active incidents (you are currently in the scam), walk to or call the local police first.

Freeze the card that was used. International and Canadian emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. Have your card number (or the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call. If the charge was a restaurant overcharge, a taxi card-swap, or a rental damage-deposit hold, also call your bank's fraud line directly — the number on the back of your card — because fraud desks sometimes freeze faster than the Visa or Mastercard international lines. If the card is Canadian-issued, the major banks (BMO, Royal Bank of Canada, TD Canada Trust, Scotiabank, CIBC, National Bank, Desjardins) all run 24/7 card-loss lines.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the police report number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Visa and Mastercard both offer strong chargeback rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend credit over debit for every Canada trip. **Never pay by Interac e-Transfer for a tourist transaction**, because Interac e-Transfers are irreversible in Canadian banking — the moment the transfer clears, the money is gone and no chargeback exists.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. US citizens should contact the US Embassy in Ottawa or one of the US Consulates General (Toronto, Montreal, Quebec City, Halifax, Calgary, Vancouver). UK citizens can reach the British High Commission in Ottawa or consular offices in Toronto, Montreal, Calgary, and Vancouver. Australians, Irish, New Zealanders, and most EU citizens are served through their respective Ottawa embassies and some Toronto or Vancouver consulates.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the police report number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position. The major dispatchers serving the North American market — Allianz Global Assistance, AXA Partners, World Nomads, Chubb, Manulife, TD Insurance — all operate 24/7 English (and many French) lines for travelers and have direct-billing networks with major Canadian hospitals.

If the scam involved an off-platform rental or a wire-fraud Airbnb booking, open the dispute on the booking platform (Booking.com, Airbnb, VRBO, Expedia) as well as with your card issuer. Platforms can sometimes recover deposits the card company cannot.

## Within the first week

File a formal consumer-protection complaint with your province's consumer affairs office. **Ontario** consumers use the Ministry of Public and Business Service Delivery's Consumer Protection Ontario (*ontario.ca/consumer-protection*). **Quebec** consumers use the Office de la protection du consommateur (*opc.gouv.qc.ca*). **British Columbia** consumers use Consumer Protection BC (*consumerprotectionbc.ca*). **Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba** have Service Alberta / Consumer Protection division. **Atlantic provinces** share the Nova Scotia / New Brunswick / PEI / Newfoundland consumer affairs framework. These offices do not recover individual losses, but their regulatory actions against repeat-offender operators, rental-listing scam operators, and tour companies are one of the few effective pressures on the scam economy. Tourist-facing businesses know which complaints reach the provincial consumer protection office; a filed complaint sometimes produces a restitution offer that the operator refused to make on the day of the incident.

For bank and payment disputes — a charge your Canadian bank refuses to reverse, an ATM skim the bank should cover, a disputed Interac e-Transfer — escalate to the **Ombudsman for Banking Services and Investments (OBSI)** at *obsi.ca*. OBSI is the financial-sector independent dispute-resolution body and can compel banks to investigate cases that the bank's first-line customer service dismisses.

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business's name, the address, the exact transaction details, the police report number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name and location only. Canadian defamation law is civil-liability-based (not criminal the way Vietnamese or Turkish defamation law can be), so factual statements with supporting receipts are protected.

## If your phone or SIM was stolen

Call your carrier immediately to suspend the line. The three major Canadian carriers — **Bell** (dial **611** from a Bell phone), **Rogers** (dial **611**), and **Telus** (dial **611**) — all operate 24/7 stolen-device lines. Canadian flanker brands (Fido, Koodo, Virgin Plus) route through the same networks. If you are on a foreign SIM, call your home carrier's international line; tourist eSIMs (Airalo, Saily, Holafly) can be suspended in-app.

File a police report with the phone's **IMEI** number (the fifteen-digit serial; find it on the original box, in your Apple or Google account, or by dialing \*#06# *before* the theft). The IMEI lets police flag the device and is required for any insurance claim. If your SIM was the target — for example, a SIM-swap scam to intercept your bank's one-time codes — contact your bank's fraud desk before anything else; SIM-swap fraud moves faster than theft recovery, and the first hour is decisive.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking, drug-testing, or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of 24/7 hospitals with English-speaking staff in each city is in Appendix C. At the hospital, ask for a toxicology screen; Canadian hospitals run these routinely through their emergency departments and will print a report you can use as evidence.

In Canada, the medical-emergency number is **911** (universal for police, fire, and ambulance). For any incident involving a foreign tourist, **911** is the most reliable line. If you are inside a venue and drink-spiking is suspected, have a friend call for an ambulance *before* you leave, not after — sometimes the aggressive bill pressure escalates once the scammer realizes you are leaving.

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate a replacement. Consular registration — STEP for US citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, LOCATE for UK, ROCA for Canadians abroad, and the Irish equivalent — is worth doing before you travel, specifically for situations like this.
