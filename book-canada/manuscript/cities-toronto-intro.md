Toronto is Canada's largest city — six million people across the Greater Toronto Area, the Pearson (YYZ) gateway that handles more than 45 million passengers a year, and the single most-documented tourist-scam ecosystem in the country. The scams cluster at three venues: the Pearson arrivals curb, the downtown Union Station / Yonge-Dundas / Entertainment District taxi corridor, and the hotel-guest impersonation phone-scam channel. The *Pearson Uber cancel-and-cash scam* is the flagship: a driver accepts your Uber booking at the Terminal 1 or Terminal 3 arrivals curb, steps out, and tells you the app "glitched — just cancel and we'll settle cash, $100 to downtown" when the real Uber fare is 45–65 CAD. Uber's *Toronto Star* coverage (2024) has flagged the pattern; the defense is to never cancel the app yourself and to decline any cash-negotiation, letting the app process the ride to completion. If the driver insists, exit the vehicle at the next well-lit area and re-book.

Six further scams structure the Toronto visit (Toronto has seven total in this book — one more than most Canadian cities because of the infrastructure density). The *Toronto taxi card-swap fraud* is the urban signature: a driver runs your credit card, claims the reader "didn't work," and hands you back a demagnetized dummy of similar colour; by the time you check the last four digits on the card, the real card is being cloned elsewhere. The defense is to confirm the last four digits match every time and, if a reader "doesn't work," pay the $20 cash fare instead of handing over a card a second time. The *fake eTA visa websites* scam catches arrivals before they even board — visa-exempt travelers flying to Canada need an Electronic Travel Authorization at the real cost of 7 CAD through *canada.ca/eta*, and fraudulent sites charge 40–100 CAD for "expedited processing"; book only on the *canada.ca* domain. The *pickpocketing at Yonge-Dundas Square and Eaton Centre* works the crowded PATH underground, the subway exits, and the sidewalk performers' audience; cross-body bags worn in front, zippers toward the chest, phone in a zipped inner pocket. The *Airbnb permit-hijacking and fake rental listings* scam uses real Toronto short-term-rental permit numbers attached to listings whose photos are pulled from mid-range hotels; book only on-platform, refuse "20% discount for bank transfer" offers, and verify the property on Google Street View before departing. The *fake-police identity check* at Union Station asks to "inspect your wallet for counterfeit bills" — real Toronto Police do not conduct counterfeit-bill inspections on sidewalks; walk into the nearest lobby and dial 911 to verify. The *TTC transit-ticket and Presto-card reseller scam* at subway exits sells "already-loaded" Presto cards that have either been drained or are duplicates — buy Presto only at fare-vending machines or the TTC customer service centre at Bloor-Yonge. Finally the *hotel-guest impersonation phone scam* — a "front desk" call to your room asks to "re-verify" your credit card due to a "system glitch"; real front desks never call rooms to re-verify cards, and the card on file is already on record from check-in. Hang up and call the front desk directly through the hotel operator.
