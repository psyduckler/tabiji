Niagara Falls is Canada's most-visited natural attraction — the Horseshoe Falls, the Maid of the Mist boat ride, and Clifton Hill's carnival-style entertainment strip. The scam ecosystem is entirely tourist-trap and concentrates on the Clifton Hill / Fallsview corridor; there are no significant airport-transport scams here (most arrivals drive from Toronto's YYZ or Buffalo's BUF). The *hidden TIF (Tourism Improvement Fee)* is the signature issue: most Clifton Hill and Fallsview restaurants silently add 10% to the bill as a "TIF" charge without disclosing it on menus, on the assumption that tourists will not check. The *Toronto Star* and Ontario Ministry of Consumer Services have both flagged the practice. The defense is to scan the bottom of the printed bill before paying — the TIF line appears in fine print as "Tourism Improvement Fee" or "TIF" — and, if undisclosed on the menu, ask to have it removed. The fee is optional from the restaurant's perspective despite how it is presented.

Four further scams shape the Niagara visit. The *Clifton Hill attraction bait* uses high-saturation billboard signage and package-deal language to bundle unwanted Wax Museum, Ripley's, or mini-golf tickets into a "Niagara Falls experience" that tourists think they've already paid for on the Maid of the Mist — the defense is to buy individual attraction tickets directly at each real attraction entrance, and to treat any "combo pass" street-vendor quote with skepticism. The *overpriced Fallsview dining trap* pairs "included" entrees with "minimum $80-per-person window table" charges that aren't mentioned at the door — ask for the full menu and the window-seating charge in writing before being seated, and for any quoted "prix-fixe" menu, read the full list before committing. The *unofficial tour ticket hustler* on Clifton Hill and the Niagara Parkway sells fake Maid of the Mist, Journey Behind the Falls, and Niagara Parks attraction tickets at a 50–100% markup; buy only at Niagara Parks' own booth (at Table Rock Centre) or on *niagaraparks.com*. Finally the *camera drop shakedown* — a "tourist" asks you to take their photo with a cheap pre-positioned camera, drops it as if you fumbled it, and demands $200 for a "replacement" — is easily defeated by declining to handle the camera; "Sorry, I'm bad with cameras, maybe ask another tourist" ends the script instantly.
