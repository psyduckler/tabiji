# Appendix A: Canadian-Traveler Quick-Reference Card {-}

Canada is bilingual (English and French federally; French is the working language throughout Quebec and parts of New Brunswick and Ontario), so this appendix is structured slightly differently from the other volumes in this series. Most Canadian scams can be ended in plain English — Canadian politeness conventions genuinely do accept "No thank you" as a complete refusal — so we focus here on the **exact short scripts** that end specific scam types, rather than on language instruction.

For Montreal, Quebec City, and some Gatineau / Hull neighborhoods in Ottawa, French equivalents are given in bold italics.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any script you think you are most likely to need. The emergency phone numbers are the single most important lines on this card — memorize **911** (all emergencies) and **1-888-495-8501** (Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre).

## Exit phrases — English and French

### Declining and leaving (the most-used category)

**No thank you. / *Non merci.***  
The universal Canadian refusal. Said with a smile and continued walking pace, it ends 90% of street-tout approaches. Never explain why; the explanation is the opening the scammer is waiting for.

**I'm not interested. / *Ce n'est pas pour moi. Merci.***  
Stronger than "no thank you." Use after the first refusal if a tout persists. The French *"Ce n'est pas pour moi"* is softer than a direct *"non"* and is the Quebecois polite-firm register.

**Please leave me alone. / *Laissez-moi tranquille, s'il vous plaît.***  
Literally "leave me alone, please." Gentle but clear. Use for persistent street touts who have not disengaged after two refusals.

**Please don't touch me. / *S'il vous plaît, ne me touchez pas.***  
Say this clearly and step back. This is the phrase that ends the fake-monk bracelet scam and the gold/jewelry blessing scam in Vancouver, Toronto, and Montreal; the scammer will disengage immediately.

**Excuse me, please let me through. / *Excusez-moi, laissez-moi passer.***  
The *excusez-moi* softens the request while still being firm. Say it clearly and keep moving forward.

**I'm just looking, thank you. / *Je regarde seulement, merci.***  
Use this firmly in any Petit Champlain souvenir stall, Quebec City sugar-shack tourist-trap, or Toronto downtown jewelry kiosk where a vendor starts pressuring you to buy.

### Responding to transport scams (taxi / Uber)

**I'll wait for the driver in my app.**  
The single most effective phrase against the Uber cancel-and-cash scam at YYZ, YOW, YHZ, YQB, YYC, YVR, and YYJ. A driver who claims "the app glitched — just cancel and we'll settle cash" is running the script; this sentence ends it, and a real Uber will arrive within minutes.

**Please complete the fare on the reader, with a tap.**  
The defense against the Toronto and Montreal taxi card-swap fraud. Insist on a contactless tap (Interac Flash or Visa Tap) rather than a card insert, which gives the driver 5+ seconds of card possession to swap. If the driver insists the tap "doesn't work," pay the fare in cash and never hand a card over a second time.

**Please show me the last four digits you charged.**  
Say this *before* you leave the vehicle after paying by card. A legitimate driver will show you the receipt on the reader; a card-swap fraudster will get flustered. Also: visually check the last four digits of the card the driver hands back — if they don't match your card, that's the swap.

**No cash, I'll pay by card only.**  
The defense against any transport-scam insistence on cash. Canadian taxis and rideshares legally must accept credit cards; a driver insisting on cash-only is running a scam.

### Responding to "skip-the-line" touts

**Thanks, I'll book through the official website.**  
The universal defense against fake Parliament tour resellers (Ottawa), Moraine Lake shuttle resellers (Banff), Calgary Stampede ticket scalpers, Niagara Falls attraction touts, Halifax Maritime Museum skip-the-line sellers, and Whistler Blackcomb lift-ticket resellers. Say it politely and walk to the actual official ticket booth.

**Parliament tours are free — let me check the official schedule.**  
The Ottawa-specific defense against the fake-Parliament-tour reseller scam. Real Parliament of Canada tours are free and booked through *ourcommons.ca* and *parl.ca*; any person selling them on the sidewalk is a fraud.

**Parks Canada is the only source for Moraine Lake reservations.**  
The Banff-specific defense against shuttle reseller fraud. Reservations open on *reservation.pc.gc.ca* and sell only there.

### Responding to "official" demands (fake police, fake parking attendants)

**I'd like to call 911 to verify.**  
The universal defense against any "official" on the street asking for cash, your wallet, your passport, or to inspect documents. A real Canadian police officer will happily wait while you call; a fake will disengage immediately.

**May I see your badge number and business card?**  
The Canadian police-officer-identification phrase. Real officers carry both and present them without protest. Toronto Police, RCMP, OPP, and Sûreté du Québec officers all have shoulder badges with visible numbers.

**I pay parking through the official city app.**  
The defense against the Montreal and Vancouver "street-parking fake attendant" scam. Say this and walk away; any person demanding cash for a meter on the street where the real meter uses PayByPhone or a kiosk is running a fraud.

### Responding to restaurant overcharges

**May I see the menu with the service charge disclosure?**  
The defense against the Niagara "hidden TIF" scam, the Montreal forced-tipping POS scam, and the Whistler "subtle" bill-padding. Legitimate Canadian restaurants disclose "service included" or "auto-gratuity 18%" on the menu; if the bill has a charge that wasn't on the menu, ask to have it removed.

**The tip field is editable — I'll set it to 15%.**  
The Canadian customary tip is 15–18% on the pre-tax subtotal for good service; a Montreal or Quebec City POS terminal pre-filled at 20% after "service included" was already rolled into the total is running the forced-tipping scam. Adjust down.

### Filing a scam report

**I was just scammed. Please help. / *Je viens d'être escroqué(e). Pouvez-vous m'aider ?***  
Use this when speaking to a hotel front-desk clerk, a fellow traveler, or any bystander. The word "scammed" / *escroqué* is the specific phrase that triggers immediate recognition and help.

**I'd like to file a police report.**  
Say this at the nearest police station or by dialing the non-emergency police line of the city you're in (Appendix C lists each city's line). Canadian police stations routinely file tourist-scam reports and issue a case number that your credit card issuer and travel insurer will require.

## Numbers that work everywhere

| Number | Who answers | When to call |
|---|---|---|
| **911** | Police, fire, or ambulance | Any active emergency |
| **1-888-495-8501** | Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre | Non-urgent scam reports, cross-provincial fraud |
| **311** | City non-emergency | Municipal questions (not for tourist scams) |
| **1-800-267-1133** | Service Canada | Lost passport, visa, eTA questions |
| **#677** | Provincial police (non-emergency) | OPP Ontario, Sûreté du Québec, RCMP elsewhere |

## Reading Canadian money

Canadian dollars are written with `CA$` or `$` (in Canada, `$` defaults to CAD). Common paper notes: $5 (blue), $10 (purple), $20 (green), $50 (red), $100 (brown). Coins: $1 ("loonie," gold), $2 ("toonie," two-tone). One US dollar ≈ 1.36 CAD as of late 2025 — verify on xe.com before travel.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Canada, polite firmness works better than raised voices — raising your voice is read as a loss of composure, and Canadian service culture does not reward it. A calm *"No thank you"* is already a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *"Please leave me alone"* and *"I'd like to call 911 to verify"* are both firm without being rude.

One last thing worth saying out loud: Canadian politeness is genuine but not infinite. A scammer who has been told "no thank you" twice and keeps pressing is past the point where you owe them any further politeness. Walking away, dialing 911, or stepping into the nearest lobby are all completely reasonable responses that no Canadian will fault you for.
