# Appendix A: Australian Exit-Phrase Card {-}

Australia is an English-speaking country, so the "phrase card" for Australia is less about vocabulary than about register. The phrases below are short scripts you can say — word for word — at the moments when ordinary politeness would cost you money. They are organized by situation.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark the one or two sections you think you are most likely to need. If you can, say the first few lines out loud a few times so they feel natural in the moment.

A note on Australian register: Australians are genuinely informal, directness is welcomed, and "mate" is gender-neutral and polite. A firm "no thanks, mate" is more effective than an apologetic "oh, I'm sorry but..." — the apology gives the scammer room to escalate; the direct refusal closes the interaction. None of the phrases below are rude by Australian standards. Say them at a normal volume, without slowing down, and keep walking.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**

The basic opener. Use it before sitting down at any café or restaurant, before accepting any "helpful" transport offer, and before any booking you did not initiate.

**Is there a weekend surcharge, a holiday surcharge, or a service charge?**

In Australia these are legal if disclosed on the menu — but they are frequently stacked without warning in high-tourism zones (Byron Bay, Port Douglas, Sydney CBD, Southbank Melbourne). Ask this before you sit down. If the answer is yes and the combined premium is over 20 percent, walk to the café next door.

**Can I see a printed menu with all prices before we order?**

A legitimate Australian restaurant will produce one immediately. A tourist trap will tell you "it's on the board" and point vaguely at a sign that lacks itemized prices. The board-only setup is a known pattern for bill inflation on fish priced "by the 100 grams" or meat priced "market rate."

**Is this the metered fare?**

Say this to any Australian taxi driver before you close the door, and say it again after the first kilometer. A legitimate driver will confirm. A scammer will offer a "flat rate" above the meter and become uncomfortable when you decline. Australian licensed taxi operators are legally required to run the meter on demand — a refusal is a breach of their licence.

**Can you send me an Airbnb or Booking.com confirmation through the app?**

Say this before paying any short-stay rental deposit. A legitimate host will do this inside five minutes. A scammer will invent reasons why the app is broken, why their account is under review, why "platform fees" mean you should wire direct. The moment any of that happens, walk away.

**What's the ABN?**

For any tour, service, or operator quoting AUD $200 or more. Every legitimate Australian business has an Australian Business Number — eleven digits, searchable at abn.business.gov.au. A legitimate operator will give it to you immediately; a scammer will stall or refuse. Typing the ABN into the register takes twenty seconds and confirms the business is real.

## Declining and leaving

**No thanks, mate.**

Said at a normal volume, without slowing down, without smiling, without making eye contact longer than one second. This is the default refusal for every unsolicited approach in Australia — the Cavill Avenue nightclub promoter, the Pitt Street Mall clipboard charity, the Queen Street Mall petition, the Surfers Paradise timeshare recruiter, the Cairns Esplanade tour agent, the Darwin airport taxi tout.

**Not interested, thanks.**

One step firmer. Use this if the first "no thanks" did not land. Two uses of this phrase in a row are sufficient to end any Australian street approach. Past that, you are under no obligation to continue the conversation.

**I'll think about it and come back if I'm keen.**

The polite exit for any high-pressure sales situation — the Gold Coast theme-park-ticket timeshare pitch, the Great Barrier Reef cheap-tour booth on the Esplanade, any "limited-time" offer in a hotel lobby. "If I'm keen" is Australian English for "if I decide I want this." It commits you to nothing and gives you the off-ramp.

**Can you give me a minute? I need to check with my bank or my travel insurance.**

The polite exit specifically for when you feel pressured and need to buy time. A legitimate operator will wait. A scammer will escalate pressure — "the offer ends today," "the spots are going fast," "my manager already signed off" — which is itself the tell. Walk out once you have your phone in hand.

## Scam in progress

**Show me an itemized receipt, please.**

For any disputed bill — the Byron Bay 25 percent weekend surcharge, the Brisbane Valley nightclub tab, the Sydney airport taxi top-up, the Melbourne café "service charge." Legitimate operators itemize on demand. Scammers stall, refuse, or produce an already-signed card slip with no breakdown. If the receipt is missing items or contains items you did not order, you can legally dispute the charge — in person with the venue manager, and through your credit card's chargeback process the next business day.

**I'll pay the metered fare only.**

For any taxi where the driver is claiming a surcharge that is not on the meter. Say it calmly, pay with a card on the in-car terminal (which is legally required in every Australian licensed taxi), and photograph the licence plate and driver ID on your way out. Then file a complaint with the relevant state transport regulator — Transport NSW, Transport for Victoria, Transport and Main Roads Queensland.

**I'll take this to chargeback.**

The phrase that most aggressively ends an Australian overcharge dispute. Credit card chargebacks in Australia follow the Visa, Mastercard, and American Express global rules — and when documented properly they routinely succeed. Mentioning the word "chargeback" often produces an immediate refund offer. If it does not, follow through: call your card issuer within 48 hours, provide the transaction details, and photograph any supporting evidence (receipts, menu photos, booking confirmations).

**I'm calling the police.**

Use this only for genuine threats, aggressive restraint of your freedom of movement, or coercive payment demands. Dial 000. English operators are available 24/7 everywhere in Australia. For a non-emergency transport or consumer dispute, dial 131 444 (state police non-emergency line) or report online through your state's police portal.

**I was scammed. Can you help?**

Use this when speaking to a police officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, a tourism information officer, or any bystander you want to enlist. "Scammed" is the right word — more specific than "robbed" (which implies force) and more direct than "overcharged." Australians will understand immediately and most will help.

## Cultural notes

**"Thongs" means flip-flops, not underwear.** Australian English diverges from American and British English in ways that occasionally cause confusion. Other traps: "esky" is a cooler, "ute" is a pickup truck, "servo" is a gas station, "bottle-o" is a liquor store, "Maccas" is McDonald's. None of these will cost you money but knowing them makes you slightly less of a target for "helpful" strangers who spot you as newly arrived.

**"How ya going?" means "how are you?"** — it is not a question about your route. Reply with "good thanks, you?" or just "yeah, good." Getting this wrong is harmless but flags you as new.

**Tipping is not expected.** Australian hospitality workers are paid a full minimum wage of $25.41 per hour in 2026, plus weekend and public-holiday penalty rates. Card readers in high-tourism zones will prompt you for 10 percent or 15 percent tips; decline them without guilt. This is not a cultural offense — it is a deliberately designed pressure mechanism that costs tourists 10 to 15 percent on every transaction where they do not consciously decline. Tipping $1 to $2 for exceptional service on a large bill is appreciated but optional. Tipping a full percentage on every coffee or lunch is an American practice; Australians do not do it.

**"She'll be right"** means "it will work out fine." It is a uniquely Australian sign-off to reassure someone. Not every use is reassuring — some Australian scammers deploy it to defuse a tourist's legitimate concern about an overcharge ("don't worry mate, she'll be right"). If you hear it in an overcharge dispute, it means the speaker wants you to stop asking questions. Ask the questions.
