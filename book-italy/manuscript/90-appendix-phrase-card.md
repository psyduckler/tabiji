# Appendix A: Italian Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Italy. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, followed by the **Italian**, and a simple pronunciation guide in italics.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. If you can, say the first three or four out loud a few times. You will feel much less awkward using them when the moment comes.

A note on pronunciation: Italian vowels are short and crisp, and each is always pronounced the same way — *a* is "ah," *e* is "eh," *i* is "ee," *o* is "oh," *u* is "oo." The letter *c* is "ch" before *e* and *i* (so *centro* is "chentro"), and hard "k" everywhere else. Double consonants are held a touch longer (*anno*, the year, sounds different from *ano*). Stress usually lands on the second-to-last syllable (*car·ta*, *pro·ble·ma*) unless marked with an accent. An accent that should be there will be written in ( as it is on "città"). None of this has to be perfect — Italians will forgive a strong accent as long as the key words are clear.

A note on formality: all the phrases below use the *lei* (formal) verb form, which is what you should use with any stranger. When you see "mi faccia" or "chiami" or "mi mostri," that is the *lei* imperative. Italians will immediately clock you as polite, which is the correct register for anything defensive.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**
Quanto costa?
*Kwan-toh KOH-stah?*

**Please show me the price list.**
Mi mostri il listino prezzi, per favore.
*Mee MOH-stree eel lee-STEE-noh PRET-tsee, pehr fah-VOH-reh.*

**Is there a cover charge? And is service included?**
C'è il coperto? E il servizio è incluso?
*Cheh eel koh-PEHR-toh? Eh eel sehr-VEE-tsyoh eh een-KLOO-zoh?*

Use this before you sit down at any restaurant. The two words that matter are *coperto* (the mandatory per-head cover charge that Italian restaurants are allowed to apply, typically €1.50–€3 in fair places and up to €5 in tourist traps) and *servizio* (service charge, which should never be applied on top of coperto). Asking both questions before you sit is the single most reliable way to avoid a Quadrilatero or Piazza Navona overcharging surprise.

**What does the fish weigh?**
Quanto pesa il pesce?
*KWAN-toh PEH-sah eel PEH-sheh?*

Fish and other items priced *al etto* (per 100 grams) should be weighed in front of you before cooking. A legitimate restaurant will bring the whole fish to your table, weigh it on a visible scale, and quote a total. Demand this — the 400–600-gram "al etto" surprise is one of the most common scams in this book.

**Do you accept credit cards?**
Accettate la carta di credito?
*Ah-chet-TAH-teh lah KAR-tah dee KREH-dee-toh?*

If the answer is "sì" but the card reader later turns out to be "broken," you are being scammed. See "Scam in progress" below.

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**
No, grazie.
*Noh, GRAH-tsyeh.*

Said firmly, once, and without slowing your pace. No elaboration is needed. Italians understand "no grazie" as a complete refusal; repeating it louder or softer is unnecessary.

**I'm not interested.**
Non mi interessa.
*Non mee een-teh-RES-sah.*

Stronger than "no grazie." Use this when a tout, a clipboard-holder, or a bracelet-seller keeps talking after the first refusal.

**I'm leaving.**
Me ne vado.
*Meh neh VAH-doh.*

**I don't want this.**
Non lo voglio.
*Non loh VOH-lyoh.*

**Leave me alone.**
Mi lasci stare.
*Mee LAH-shee STAH-reh.*

This is the correct firm-but-polite way to say "back off" to someone who is in your personal space. The word *lasci* is the formal imperative of *lasciare* (to leave, to release) — you are telling them to release you. It carries weight without sounding aggressive.

**Don't touch me.**
Non mi tocchi.
*Non mee TOK-kee.*

The phrase that goes with Pattern 1 in the red-flag chapter. Say this clearly and step back. Most pickpocket pairs will disengage immediately rather than escalate.

**Excuse me, please let me through.**
Scusi, mi faccia passare.
*SKOO-zee, mee FAH-chah pah-SAH-reh.*

The *scusi* softens it so that the person blocking your path hears a polite request rather than a command. If they do not move, repeat it once and keep walking.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I'm calling the police.**
Chiamo i carabinieri.
*Kee-AH-moh ee kah-rah-bee-NYEH-ree.*

Or:
Chiamo la polizia.
*Kee-AH-moh lah poh-lee-TSEE-ah.*

This is the single most useful phrase in this entire appendix. Either version works — the *carabinieri* and the *polizia di stato* both respond to 112. Say it clearly. The word *carabinieri* or *polizia* changes the atmosphere in a room immediately, and most scam operators back down the moment they hear it.

**I'll call my bank.**
Chiamo la mia banca.
*Kee-AH-moh lah MEE-ah BAHN-kah.*

Say this if a restaurant or taxi driver disputes a credit-card transaction. Calling your card issuer from the table is a legitimate move and signals that the charge is going to be disputed.

**I was scammed. Please help.**
Sono stato truffato. Mi aiuti, per favore.
*SOH-noh STAH-toh troo-FAH-toh. Mee ah-YOO-tee, pehr fah-VOH-reh.*

**If you are a woman**, use the feminine endings:
Sono stata truffata.
*SOH-noh STAH-tah troo-FAH-tah.*

Use this when speaking to a Carabinieri officer, a Polizia di Stato officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, or any bystander you want to enlist. *Truffato/a* is the specific word for "scammed" and communicates what happened more precisely than any English translation. For pickpocketing specifically, *mi hanno borseggiato* (masculine) or *mi hanno borseggiata* (feminine) — "they pickpocketed me."

**Please show me an itemized receipt.**
Vorrei la ricevuta dettagliata, per favore.
*Vohr-REH-ee lah ree-cheh-VOO-tah det-tah-lee-AH-tah, pehr fah-VOH-reh.*

In Italy, restaurant bills sometimes arrive as a single total with a *coperto* line and little else. Asking for the *ricevuta dettagliata* (itemized receipt) forces the establishment to account for every item, and it is the most polite version of "I do not accept this charge." A legitimate place will hand it to you without complaint. A tourist trap will stall.

**I didn't order this.**
Non ho ordinato questo.
*Non oh ohr-dee-NAH-toh KWEH-stoh.*

Say this calmly about any unrequested *pane*, *acqua*, *antipasto*, or *limoncello* that appears on your bill. In Italy you are not legally required to pay for food you did not order, though you will usually need to say so explicitly.

**This is not what we agreed.**
Non è quello che avevamo concordato.
*Non eh KWEL-loh keh ah-veh-VAH-moh kon-kor-DAH-toh.*

A short, firm phrase that accuses nobody but puts the burden of explanation on the other party. Use this for taxi fares that exceed a quoted price, hotel charges that differ from the booking, and tour-operator price-creep.

**I'll file a complaint.**
Presenterò una denuncia.
*Preh-zen-teh-ROH OO-nah deh-NOON-chah.*

*Denuncia* is the formal consumer- or criminal-complaint filing. A tourist saying this credibly — especially combined with *chiamo i carabinieri* — is taken seriously.

## Getting help

**Where's the nearest police station?**
Dov'è il commissariato più vicino?
*Doh-VEH eel kom-mees-sah-RYAH-toh pyoo vee-CHEE-noh?*

Or for the Carabinieri:
Dov'è la caserma dei carabinieri più vicina?
*Doh-VEH lah kah-SEHR-mah day kah-rah-bee-NYEH-ree pyoo vee-CHEE-nah?*

**I want an official taxi.**
Vorrei un taxi ufficiale.
*Vohr-REH-ee oon TAHK-see oof-fee-CHAH-leh.*

Use this at airports and stations where unlicensed drivers approach you. "Official" in Italy means a white-painted car with a meter, a posted licence number on the rear door, and a rooftop TAXI sign. Anything else is an *NCC abusivo*.

**Please call 112.**
Chiami il 112, per favore.
*Kee-AH-mee eel OO-noh OO-noh DOO-eh, pehr fah-VOH-reh.*

Or just shout:
Aiuto! Chiami il 112!
*Ah-YOO-toh! Kee-AH-mee eel OO-noh OO-noh DOO-eh!*

112 is the pan-European emergency number and routes you to the appropriate service (Carabinieri, Polizia, ambulance, fire) depending on what you say. Easier to say under stress than the longer sentence, and every Italian adult knows what *uno-uno-due* means.

**Help! Thief!**
Aiuto! Al ladro!
*Ah-YOO-toh! Ahl LAH-droh!*

Shouted in a crowded street, this is understood by every bystander as "someone stole from me, help me stop them." The words *al ladro* are the classic Italian hue-and-cry and carry legal weight — a bystander who helps you restrain a thief is protected under Italian law (*arresto in flagranza di reato*).

**Is there someone who speaks English?**
C'è qualcuno che parla inglese?
*Cheh kwahl-KOO-noh keh PAR-lah een-GLEH-zeh?*

**Please help me.**
Mi aiuti, per favore.
*Mee ah-YOO-tee, pehr fah-VOH-reh.*

## Reading euro amounts on a bill

Italian numerals use the comma as a decimal and the period as a thousands separator — the reverse of American convention. This trips up almost every visitor, and it can turn a simple bill into a five-minute argument.

- **€1,50** means one euro fifty cents — not one thousand five hundred.
- **€1.500** means one thousand five hundred euros — not one euro fifty.
- **€1.500,00** is the fully-formatted "one thousand five hundred euros exactly."

| Amount | Written | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| €1 | un euro | *oon EH-oo-roh* |
| €5 | cinque euro | *CHEEN-kweh EH-oo-roh* |
| €10 | dieci euro | *dee-EH-chee EH-oo-roh* |
| €20 | venti euro | *VEN-tee EH-oo-roh* |
| €50 | cinquanta euro | *cheen-KWAN-tah EH-oo-roh* |
| €100 | cento euro | *CHEN-toh EH-oo-roh* |
| €500 | cinquecento euro | *cheen-kweh-CHEN-toh EH-oo-roh* |
| €1.000 | mille euro | *MEEL-leh EH-oo-roh* |

If a waiter or cashier tells you a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it out:

**Please write the number down.**
Mi scriva il numero, per favore.
*Mee SKREE-vah eel NOO-meh-roh, pehr fah-VOH-reh.*

They will almost always oblige.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Italy, polite firmness works better than raised voices — though unlike in Japan, raising your voice is socially acceptable and not always counter-productive. An *iie* equivalent ("No grazie") delivered calmly is a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *mi lasci stare* and *non mi tocchi* are both firm without being rude, and *chiamo i carabinieri* is the firmest card in the deck.

A last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar. Calm and firm beats loud and uncertain every time.
