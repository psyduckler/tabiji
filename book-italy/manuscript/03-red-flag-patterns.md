# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The one hundred and forty-nine scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. If you learn the patterns, you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: In a tourist zone, physical contact is a pickpocket tell

This is the single most important habit you can take to Italy: if a stranger comes inside your arm's length in a famous piazza, a station concourse, or on a packed metro train, your bag is being checked, not greeted.

Italian scam teams are unusually physical. In Bologna, a beggar at the Due Torri grabs a tourist's arm as she asks for change, and in the same motion empties his pocket — a 44-year-old Bosnian woman was arrested doing exactly this on a documented 6pm afternoon in Piazza di Porta Ravegnana. In Florence, a man walks up and says your shoes are untied; as you look down, a woven friendship bracelet is already on your wrist and his partner's hand is in your bag. In Milan and Rome, bracelet pairs do the same trick at the Duomo and around the Castello Sforzesco. On the Venice San Marco pickpocket beat, a Corriere del Veneto investigation named a ring that demanded a €2,500-per-day take from its members; more than two hundred pickpockets were detained in 2025 alone, most of them working the same ninety-metre stretch of calle between Piazza San Marco and Campo San Luca. In Naples, the physical version is the *scippo* — a moped slowing beside you just long enough to rip a bag strap — and in Palermo it's the parallel scooter brush past in Via Maqueda.

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: when any stranger enters your personal space in a crowd, step one full pace back and put a hand on your bag. Walk on the portico-wall side of a street rather than the open side. Keep your crossbody bag on the wall side of your body, zipper facing in. Never carry a wallet in a back pocket inside the historic centre. And on a packed bus or metro at tourist hotspots — Rome's Line B to the Colosseum, Milan's Red Line at Duomo, Florence's Bus 7 to Piazzale Michelangelo — your bag goes in front, both hands on it until you get off. If someone tries to tie something on you, drop it on the ground and walk away. They will not follow.

*You will see this pattern in: the Via dell'Indipendenza borseggiatrici (Bologna), the Due Torri arm-grab (Bologna), the friendship-bracelet rings (Florence, Rome, Milan, Pisa, Verona), the Rolex hug-and-lift (Naples), the moped scippatori (Naples, Palermo), the San Marco pickpocket network (Venice), the Milano Centrale and Roma Termini platform teams, the Duomo pickpocket pairs (Florence, Milan), and almost every bus/metro line listed in a city chapter.*

## Pattern 2: "Scusi..." from a stranger is a scam opener

The next pattern is harder to spot because it begins as a small kindness or small question.

Two well-dressed women walking down Via dell'Indipendenza in Bologna ask you for a cigarette. A man outside the Florence Duomo points to the ground and tells you your shoelace is untied. A woman with a clipboard near the Uffizi asks you to sign a petition for deaf-mute schoolchildren. A priest outside the Vatican asks you to bless a rose. A teenage girl at the Trevi Fountain is about to take your photo, for a tip. A street photographer in Piazza Navona beats you to it, takes the photo, and demands €20. At Porta Marina outside Pompeii, a man in a neat shirt points at a storefront labelled "Info Point" and tells you the official ticket office is inside.

None of these interactions are casual. The 2016 Il Resto del Carlino arrest report for Via dell'Indipendenza named two Roma women, ages 18 and 26, both with prior convictions, using the cigarette script on a Japanese tourist — an off-duty policeman witnessed the theft from his pocket and made the arrest on the spot. The "Info Point" storefronts outside Pompeii have been the subject of multiple TripAdvisor "TRUFFA!" warnings and at least one active Carabinieri enforcement thread, because they charge €150-per-person for guided access the tourist could buy for €22 at the real entrance. In Verona and at Lake Garda, the *finte sordomute* petition ring has been arrested in batches of four — L'Arena named four Romanian women picked up by Polizia Locale di Bardolino in a single sweep, with a police officer injured during the pickup.

Italians do not generally approach foreign tourists on the street for a cigarette, for directions, for a petition, or for a photo. When it happens in a tourist zone, something else is going on.

The defense is short and not rude. Reply to any "scusi, posso fare una domanda?" approach with a firm "no grazie" and do not stop walking. Do not reach for a cigarette, sign a clipboard, or hand over your phone for a photo at a famous site. If you want a photo of yourself, ask a tourist with their own camera; scammers carry nothing. If you step on a "painting" laid across a walkway, keep walking — it cost the artist €2 and they will not chase you far.

*You will see this pattern in: the Via Indipendenza cigarette pickpocket (Bologna), the Duomo bracelet duos (Florence, Milan, Rome), the finte sordomute clipboard ring (Verona, Lake Garda, Siena, Rome), the Uffizi painting-ambush (Florence), the Trevi Fountain photographer (Rome), the Pompei "Info Point" storefront scam, and the fake-priest donation scam near the Vatican and Basilica di Santa Croce.*

## Pattern 3: If prices are not posted outside, you do not sit down

The third pattern is the most common and, across a full meal, among the most expensive. Italian law requires restaurants to post a menu with prices visible from the street. If no menu is posted, or if the posted menu omits per-item prices for fish or meat, or if the barker at the door is calling to you in English and German, you are being sorted into a tourist trap.

This single pattern accounts for a meaningful chunk of the overcharging scams in this book. In Bologna's Quadrilatero, a 2024 New York Times opinion piece documented "Ancient cold cuts butcher" chain shops that had actually been "open for three months" — 95 percent of the alleys between Piazza Maggiore and Piazza della Mercanzia are tourist traps per r/bologna locals, with unmarked coperto up to €3.50 per head, automatic 15 percent *servizio* on foreign cards, and fish priced *al etto* (per 100 grams) that arrives as a 600-gram portion at five times the menu price. On Capri's Piazzetta, a July 2022 receipt published by RaiNews showed €78 for six cornetti and five coffees — legal, because a bar tariff is technically posted, but illegal-feeling because the outdoor *al tavolo* tariff is never quoted and never visible from the table. In Sirmione on Lake Garda, Corriere Brescia's June 2025 "salassi" (bloodletting) coverage documented €75 lunches and €8 single gelato scoops along the peninsula. In Porto Istana, Sardinia, La Nuova Sardegna reported a Milanese family billed €1,155 for cheese and sausage priced *al etto* at tourist rates.

The defense is a single habit, repeated before every sit-down meal: before taking a chair, photograph the full menu from outside the restaurant, including the *coperto* line. If the coperto is above €2.50 per head, walk. If no menu is posted, walk. If a barker is at the door calling to tourists, walk — that is the clearest tell of a tourist trap that Italian locals on r/bologna, r/italy, and r/ItalyTravel name consistently. When a waiter offers you fish "al etto," ask to be shown the whole fish on the scale before it is cooked, and get a per-item euro figure confirmed out loud. Three minutes of vigilance at the door saves a bill in the hundreds.

*You will see this pattern in: the Quadrilatero tourist-menu trap (Bologna), Piazza Navona and Campo de' Fiori restaurants (Rome), San Marco restaurant maxi-conto (Venice), the Piazzetta café bill shock (Capri), Sirmione lakefront restaurants (Lake Garda), Quattro Canti tourist menus (Palermo), Corso Umberto pesce al etto (Taormina), Via Rizzoli outdoor tables (Bologna), and most waterfront restaurants across the Amalfi Coast and Cinque Terre chapters.*

## Pattern 4: Airport and station transport starts at the official queue — never from a driver who approached you

The fourth pattern starts at the airport.

You have just finished a long flight, you are tired, and somewhere between baggage claim and the official taxi queue, a well-dressed man steps into your path. He is not in uniform. He says, in good English, "taxi?" and quotes a flat fare that sounds reasonable. An hour later, the bill is double what he quoted, the "broken" card reader has forced you to a bancomat where he watched your PIN, and there is no receipt.

Italy's airport and station transport scams fall into three families. The most common is the *abusivo* NCC driver — a privately-licensed car-with-driver service that has no taxi meter, no legal obligation to honour a quoted price, and no way to complain to the Comune if things go wrong. They work every Italian airport, and they specifically target tourists who do not know the posted fixed fares. The official fare from Bologna Airport to the city centre is €12.80 for up to 6 km; from Naples Capodichino it is €21 to Piazza Garibaldi and €24 to the ferry port; from Milan Malpensa it is €95 flat to the city centre (the 2025 Comune di Milano rate); from Catania Fontanarossa to Taormina it is roughly €70-€90 for the 55-kilometre trip. Any driver who quotes a number substantially above the posted rate is a scammer. In Venice, the same pattern runs on water — il Gazzettino's Marco Polo airport coverage pegged unlicensed water-taxi operators at a roughly two-to-one ratio over licensed operators, with flat rates to San Marco that can exceed €200 when the public Alilaguna does the same trip for €15.

The second family is the "broken meter" or "broken card reader" scam inside a licensed taxi. The driver claims his reader does not work, and steers you to a bancomat to withdraw cash while watching your PIN. This has been documented from Rome to Florence to Palermo to Bologna. In any Italian taxi, if the card reader is "broken," get out, pay cash for the metered total only, take a photograph of the licence number on the rear door, and file a complaint with the Comune. You do not need to go to an ATM.

The third family is the Circumvesuviana and regional-train pickpocket — not a driver scam, but a transport scam that catches airport-to-destination travellers. Naples to Sorrento to Pompeii on the Circumvesuviana is notorious enough that Il Mattino has called it "un'aggressione al giorno" (an attack a day). The defensive move is to sit with luggage wedged between feet and back, phone in front pocket only, and headphones off at stops.

The defense, across all three families, is to never take a ride from a driver who approached you first. Walk past anyone who says "taxi" in an airport arrival hall and go directly to the posted official taxi queue. Use train and metro transfers where they exist: the Marconi Express from Bologna Airport is €12.20; the Leonardo Express from Fiumicino to Roma Termini is €14; the Malpensa Express to Milano Centrale is €13; the Alilaguna to Venice is €15. These skip the scam entirely. When you do take a taxi, agree on the posted fixed fare (say the number out loud and have the driver confirm) before you close the door, and if the meter deviates or the app route does, speak up immediately.

*You will see this pattern in: the Fiumicino and Ciampino airport taxi scams (Rome), the Malpensa and Linate overcharge (Milan), the Capodichino to Sorrento / Naples circuit, the Marco Polo water-taxi abusivi (Venice), the Santa Maria Novella taxi strikes (Florence), the Punta Raisi and Catania airport lines (Palermo, Taormina), the Bologna Airport / Centrale "broken card reader" variant, and the Circumvesuviana pickpocket line (Naples, Sorrento, Pompeii).*

## Pattern 5: An official is an official in a station, not on a sidewalk

The fifth pattern is the stranger in authority. Real Italian police — Carabinieri in black uniforms, Polizia di Stato in blue, Guardia di Finanza in grey and yellow — will always take you to a station for any document or wallet check. They will never examine your cash on the street. They will never photograph your wallet. They will never tell you to transfer money to a "safe account" by phone. If any of those things is happening to you, you are being scammed.

The *finti poliziotti* (fake plainclothes police) scam runs across Italy. Two men in ordinary clothes flash a laminated badge and tell you there have been reports of counterfeit euros in the area — would you show them your wallet so they can check? They riffle through your bills, palm a wad of cash, and hand the wallet back. You notice the missing bills only once they have turned the corner. The Milan variant, documented in Corriere Milano and Il Giorno, concentrates in Piazza del Duomo, Via Torino, Via Montenapoleone, and the Stazione Centrale forecourt. The Rome variant works the same script at Termini and around the Forum. The same pattern has run on the A4 autostrada and in northern Italian hotels, with Iranian- and Turkish-origin rings arrested repeatedly across Veneto, Brescia, and Padua.

Two related patterns extend this into other kinds of authority. The *finti parenti* (fake-relative) phone scam has reached Bologna, with a Carabinieri investigation under way into an impostor who convinced an elderly resident to gather her jewellery and then pushed her to the ground when the ruse unravelled. A Repubblica Roma investigation in summer 2025 documented POS-pirata attacks — portable card readers used to tap contactless payments out of pockets on the metro — with a single attack on a Rome tourist netting €9,000 in one contactless transaction. A 36-year-old Peruvian woman was arrested in Sorrento carrying the device.

The defense is simple and universal. If anyone on the street identifies themselves as an official and wants to examine your wallet, your cash, your documents, or your phone, you refuse and walk into the nearest shop or hotel lobby. Say calmly: "Andiamo al commissariato" (let's go to the police station). A real officer will have no problem with this. A fake one will leave. If they produce a badge, ask to see it close up and note the number; the number on a real badge is issued and traceable, and a real officer will hand it over. For phone versions of the scam — a cold call from someone claiming to be the police, the bank, or a utility — hang up and call back using the number from the organisation's published website, never the number the caller gave you.

*You will see this pattern in: the Roma Termini and Forum finti poliziotti, the Milan Piazza del Duomo plainclothes-ID-check, the A4 autostrada and northern-hotel ring, the Venice San Marco variant, the Florence Santa Maria Novella variant, the POS-pirata contactless theft on Rome's metro, and the finti parenti phone scam (Bologna).*

## Pattern 6: Money off the platform is money gone

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch by reading the fine print after you have committed, so this pattern is really about what to pin down before you click Confirm.

You book a Bologna apartment online, pay in full through Booking.com, and arrive at the address to find a locked door and an unreachable host. You book a Positano hotel from what looks like its official website; the real hotel, with the same name, exists a block away, and the email address you paid via wire transfer belongs to a woman in Napoli who has now vanished. You pick up a rental car at Olbia Airport and at return the attendant points at a scratch under the bumper and charges €300 to your deposit before you can argue. You book a "Blue Grotto boat tour" on Capri; the sea is too rough and the cave is closed, and the operator charges the full boat-tour fare and refuses a refund. You buy what you believe is the Cinque Terre Card from a reseller at La Spezia station for €35 when the official price is €17.30. You cross the line of the Florence ZTL once in a rental car and an €80 fine arrives in your country three months later.

All of these work because you committed before you knew all the terms, and then the terms — or the rules, or the venues, or the platforms — were not what you thought they were.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every platform interaction during your trip. Book accommodation only through platforms that offer pay-through-platform guarantees and dispute resolution (Airbnb, Booking.com, Hotels.com); never wire a deposit and never pay cash off-platform at arrival. Verify any property on Google Street View before you depart — a legitimate B&B has visible signage or an intercom label at the claimed address; a scam property does not exist. Pay every rental, excursion, or hotel by credit card (Visa, Mastercard, American Express) rather than debit, cash, PayPal Friends-&-Family, or bank transfer — only credit cards give you a chargeback right under the EU consumer-protection framework and you can usually reverse a fraudulent charge within thirty days. Never agree to a "small surcharge to pay by card"; EU PSD2 rules bar merchant surcharges on most consumer card payments, and a legitimate operator will not ask. Photograph rental cars from every angle before you drive off; film a slow 360-degree video with the date visible. Before buying any ticket to the Cinque Terre, the Blue Grotto, the Uffizi, the Colosseum, the Vatican, the Teatro Antico in Taormina, Gardaland, or any museum, buy from the operator's official website or app — not from a reseller on the street, in a "Info Point," or on a Facebook ad. Know the real price so you can spot the markup.

One more: the Italian ZTL (*Zona a Traffico Limitato*) is a real trap for tourists driving in. Florence, Siena, Bologna, Milan, Rome, Lucca, Pisa and most historic centres have gate cameras that fine rental cars €80 to €335 per unauthorised entry. Rental companies pass the fines through with an administrative surcharge that often makes the total exceed €200 per entry. If your route into the city centre takes you past a ZTL gate, you are not allowed through it without a resident permit; park outside and walk or take public transport in.

*You will see this pattern in: off-platform rental scams (Bologna Fiera, Milan Fashion Week, Positano clone-site, Sardinia apartment rental), the Cinque Terre Card reseller markup, the Capri Blue Grotto no-refund weather trap, the Teatro Antico scaffolding refund dispute (Taormina), the Olbia rental-car phantom-damage pattern (Sardinia), the ZTL fine trap (Florence, Siena, Bologna, Rome), the Rome POS-pirata contactless tap, and the "broken card reader" ATM-walk across the country.*
