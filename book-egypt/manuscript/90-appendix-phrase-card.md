# Appendix A: Egyptian Arabic Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Egypt. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, followed by the **Egyptian Arabic** in Latin transliteration, and a simple pronunciation guide in italics. Egyptian script (right-to-left Arabic) is included after the transliteration where it helps; the transliteration is the primary form because it is what you will actually be reading off your phone in the moment.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. Egyptian Arabic — *Masri* (لهجة مصرية) — is the spoken register that taxi drivers, vendors, hotel clerks, and police actually use, and it is what every adult in Cairo, Luxor, Aswan, Alexandria, Hurghada, and Sharm El Sheikh will recognize first. The phrases below are deliberately written in Masri rather than the formal Modern Standard Arabic (*Fusha*) you might find in a textbook — Fusha sounds stilted on the street and slows down the moment you most need it to land.

A note on transliteration. Egyptian Arabic vowels are short and crisp — *a* is "ah," *e* is "eh," *i* is "ee," *o* is "oh," *u* is "oo." A capital letter inside a word marks emphasis (a "thicker" consonant — *Sa* sounds heavier than *sa*). The combination *kh* is the throaty "ch" sound in the German *Bach* or Scottish *loch*. You will also see two number-letters borrowed from Arabic-chat conventions: **3** stands for the letter ع (*ayn*), a deep-throat sound made by tightening the back of your throat and then releasing — closest to a strained "ah." **7** stands for the letter ح (*Ha*), a breathy "h" made deeper in the throat than English "h." Neither needs to be perfect. Egyptians will forgive a strong foreign accent as long as the key word is clear. *Inshallah* — "God willing" — closes about half of all conversational sentences in Egypt and is fine to use as a gentle softener; it is not religious in everyday speech.

A note on gender. Egyptian Arabic conjugates verbs by the speaker's gender — "I want," "I am leaving," "I was robbed" all change shape depending on whether a man or a woman is saying them. Where the speaker's gender matters, the phrases below give both forms, with the **feminine** listed first because most readers of this book are tourists rather than residents and most travelers reading defensive phrases like these are women. The shorthand `(M)` and `(F)` mark which form is which. When in doubt, use the form that matches you and you will be understood.

A note on register. Egyptian Arabic has a polite mode and a sharp mode. The polite mode uses *min faDlik* (please) and *law sama7t* (if you would allow) and is what you should default to. The sharp mode drops these and is what you escalate to only when polite has failed and someone is still in your space. Both registers are below; use the polite first, the sharp second.

## Asking before you commit

**How much?**
Bekam? [Arabic: بكام؟]
*Beh-KAHM?*

**How much is this?**
Da bekam? [Arabic: ده بكام؟]
*Dah beh-KAHM?*

The single most important question in Egypt. Ask it before you sit down, before luggage goes in the trunk, before a guide walks beside you for ten minutes, before the felucca leaves the dock. *Bekam* is short, clear, and impossible to mishear. The price you are quoted at this moment is the price you have agreed to — anything higher at the end of the transaction is a scam, not a misunderstanding.

**Please show me the price list.**
Wareeni qa'imet el-as3ar, min faDlik. [Arabic: وريني قائمة الأسعار، من فضلك]
*Wah-REE-nee KAH-ee-met el-ahs-3AR, min FAD-lik.*

Use this in restaurants, papyrus shops, perfume sellers, and anywhere a quoted price feels improvised. A legitimate establishment has a printed list; a tourist-trap operation will stall.

**Is this for the whole trip or per person?**
Da lel-re7la kollaha walla lel-far'? [Arabic: ده للرحلة كلها ولا للفرد؟]
*Dah lel-REH7-lah KOH-la-hah WAH-lah lel-FARD?*

The pivot inside about half the taxi, felucca, camel-ride, and tour-bus scams in this book. The driver quotes "fifty pounds" and at the destination claims it was fifty per person, not fifty for the whole car. Asking this before the door closes — and asking the driver to nod or repeat it back — closes that loophole.

**What's included?**
Eh elli da5el fel-se3r? [Arabic: إيه اللي داخل في السعر؟]
*Eh EL-lee DAH-khel fel-SE3-r?*

For tour packages, hotel transfers, and any "all-inclusive" boat or camel ride. Force them to itemize. *Da5el* (literally "entering") is the word for "included."

**Where is the official ticket office?**
Fein shibbak el-tazaker el-rasmi? [Arabic: فين شباك التذاكر الرسمي؟]
*Feyn SHEB-bak el-tah-ZAH-ker el-RAS-mee?*

Critical at temples, museums, and citadels where freelance "guides" intercept you before the gate. The word *rasmi* — official — distinguishes the government counter from the man with a clipboard.

## When you need to refuse politely

**No thank you.**
La' shokran. [Arabic: لأ شكراً]
*Lah SHOH-kran.*

Said firmly, kept walking, no elaboration. Egyptians understand *la' shokran* as a complete refusal. Repeating it louder does not strengthen it — repeating it the same way a second time does. The glottal *la'* (with a sharp stop, not "la") is the standard refusal across all of Egypt.

**I'm not interested.**
(F) Mish 3ayza. [Arabic: مش عايزة]
*Mish 3AH-yzah.*
(M) Mish 3ayez. [Arabic: مش عايز]
*Mish 3AH-yez.*

Stronger than *la' shokran*. Use this when a tout, a shopkeeper, or a calèche driver keeps talking after the first refusal. *Mish* is the standard Egyptian negation prefix — "not."

**I have a guide already.**
(F) 3andi morshid khalaS. [Arabic: عندي مرشد خلاص]
*3AN-dee MOR-shid kha-LAS.*
(M) 3andi morshid khalaS.
*Same as above — this phrase is gender-neutral.*

The single best refusal at temple gates and pyramid approaches, where unbadged "guides" attach themselves the moment you arrive. *KhalaS* literally means "finished" or "done" — the implication is "this conversation is closed."

**Please leave me alone.**
(F) Sibni li-7ali, min faDlik. [Arabic: سيبني لحالي، من فضلك]
*SEEB-nee lee-7ALL-ee, min FAD-lik.*
(M) Sibni li-7ali, min faDlak.
*SEEB-nee lee-7ALL-ee, min FAD-lak.*

Note: *min faDlik* is the form you use when speaking to a woman, *min faDlak* when speaking to a man — Egyptian Arabic conjugates "please" by the *listener's* gender. Practice the version aimed at the kind of person likely to be hassling you.

**Don't follow me.**
(F) Ma-tmshish warayya. [Arabic: ماتمشيش ورايا]
*Mah-tem-SHEESH wah-RAH-yah.*
(M) Ma-tmshish warayya.
*Same form, gender-neutral here.*

For unbadged "guides" who walk alongside you through a temple complex despite repeated refusals. Said firmly, with eye contact, this almost always works.

**I don't need anything.**
(F) Ana mish me7tagga 7aga. [Arabic: أنا مش محتاجة حاجة]
*AH-nah mish meh7-TAH-gah 7AH-gah.*
(M) Ana mish me7tag 7aga. [Arabic: أنا مش محتاج حاجة]
*AH-nah mish meh7-TAHG 7AH-gah.*

Use this against the "free papyrus" / "free perfume sample" / "let me show you my shop" pulls. The phrase shuts the door on the entire script.

## When something feels wrong

**I want a receipt.**
(F) 3ayza waSl. [Arabic: عايزة وصل]
*3AH-yzah WAHSL.*
(M) 3ayez waSl. [Arabic: عايز وصل]
*3AH-yez WAHSL.*

The single most useful escalation phrase in this entire appendix. Egyptian merchants and drivers know that a tourist asking for *waSl* is a tourist preparing to dispute the charge. A legitimate operator hands one over without complaint. A scam operator stalls — and that stall is your signal to walk away or call 126.

**This is not what we agreed.**
(F) Da mish elli ittafa'na 3aleh. [Arabic: ده مش اللي اتفقنا عليه]
*Dah mish EL-lee it-tah-FA2-nah 3ah-LEH.*
(M) Same.

For the taxi driver who "remembers" the price was per person, the felucca captain who adds an extra hour of "tea time," the shopkeeper who claims the marked price was wrong. Said calmly, in front of witnesses, this puts the burden of explanation on the other side.

**I'll wait for the police.**
(F) Hastanna el-bolees. [Arabic: هستنى البوليس]
*Hahs-TAN-nah el-boh-LEES.*
(M) Same form — gender-neutral.

The atmosphere-changer. Use this when a scam is in progress and the operator has not backed off. *Bolees* (police) and *shorTa* (police, the slightly more formal version) are both understood. Most scam operations dissolve the moment a tourist says this credibly and pulls out a phone.

**Do not touch me.**
(F) Ma-tilmesnish! [Arabic: ماتلمسنيش]
*Mah-til-MES-neesh!*
(M) Same form.

The pickpocket-disengagement phrase. Said sharp, with a step backward and a hand raised. Most pickpocket pairs disengage immediately rather than escalate, especially in the close press of Khan El Khalili, the metro, or a Luxor temple corridor.

**Where is the Tourist Police?**
Fein el-shorTa el-siya7eyya? [Arabic: فين الشرطة السياحية؟]
*Feyn el-SHOR-tah el-see-yah-7EYE-yah?*

The Tourist Police (*shorTa siya7eyya*) is the dedicated branch for incidents involving foreign visitors. Their kiosks sit at the major tourist gates — Giza Pyramids, Karnak, Valley of the Kings, the Egyptian Museum, Citadel of Saladin — and every hotel concierge can direct you to the nearest one. The phrase above gets you pointed in the right direction.

**I'm calling my embassy.**
(F) Ana hatkallem el-sefara bta3ti. [Arabic: أنا هتكلم السفارة بتاعتي]
*AH-nah hat-KAL-lem el-seh-FAH-rah b'TAH-3tee.*
(M) Ana hatkallem el-sefara bta3i. [Arabic: أنا هتكلم السفارة بتاعي]
*AH-nah hat-KAL-lem el-seh-FAH-rah b'TAH-3ee.*

For situations where a Tourist Police kiosk is too far or unresponsive — typically arrivals halls, customs counters, and ticket-booth disputes that escalate. The mention of *sefara* (embassy) raises the stakes for officials who are improvising and signals that there will be a formal record of what happens next.

## Calling for help

**Help!**
El7a'ooni! [Arabic: الحقوني]
*El-7AH-2OO-nee!*

The Egyptian Arabic shouted hue-and-cry. Translates literally to "catch up to me" or "come help me." Every Egyptian adult understands it as "I am in trouble, intervene" and bystanders will respond. Shouted in a Khan El Khalili alley or on a Cairo metro platform, *el7a'ooni* draws a crowd faster than English does.

**Thief!**
7arami! [Arabic: حرامي]
*7ah-RAH-mee!*

Shouted at a pickpocket fleeing the scene. Pair with *El7a'ooni!* for maximum effect. Egyptian crowds will physically chase a *7arami* — this is the standard street response to theft.

**Call the police.**
(F) Etkallemi el-shorTa. [Arabic: اتكلمي الشرطة]
*Et-KAL-leh-mee el-SHOR-tah.*
(M speaking to anyone) Kallem el-shorTa. [Arabic: كلم الشرطة]
*KAL-lem el-SHOR-tah.*

Said to a bystander, hotel clerk, or shop owner. The number to dial is **122** (general police) or **126** (Tourist Police). Both are free from any phone in Egypt. *Shorta* is recognized everywhere; *bolees* is colloquial-equivalent.

**Call an ambulance.**
(F) Etkallemi el-iss3af. [Arabic: اتكلمي الإسعاف]
*Et-KAL-leh-mee el-iss-3AHF.*
(M) Kallem el-iss3af.
*KAL-lem el-iss-3AHF.*

The ambulance number is **123**. *Iss3af* is the standard word for ambulance and emergency medical service.

**I need a doctor.**
(F) Me7tagga doktor. [Arabic: محتاجة دكتور]
*Meh7-TAH-gah dok-TOR.*
(M) Me7tag doktor. [Arabic: محتاج دكتور]
*Meh7-TAHG dok-TOR.*

For anything short of an emergency. Hotel front desks routinely help guests find an English-speaking doctor — the phrase above to a concierge produces a referral within minutes.

**I need to call my embassy.**
(F) Me7tagga atkallem ma3a el-sefara bta3ti. [Arabic: محتاجة أتكلم مع السفارة بتاعتي]
*Meh7-TAH-gah at-KAL-lem MA3-ah el-seh-FAH-rah b'TAH-3tee.*
(M) Me7tag atkallem ma3a el-sefara bta3i. [Arabic: محتاج أتكلم مع السفارة بتاعي]
*Meh7-TAHG at-KAL-lem MA3-ah el-seh-FAH-rah b'TAH-3ee.*

Use this with police, hospital admissions staff, and customs officials who are detaining or pressuring you. Embassies cannot intervene in routine matters but they do make calls that change the tone of a situation.

## Money and money-changing

**I want a receipt.**
(F) 3ayza waSl. [Arabic: عايزة وصل]
*3AH-yzah WAHSL.*
(M) 3ayez waSl.
*3AH-yez WAHSL.*

Repeated from the previous section because it is the single most important phrase at any money-changer, ATM, or visa-on-arrival counter. Egyptian banks issue printed receipts as a matter of routine. The absence of one — at a customs counter demanding $10, at a Bureau de Change with a "broken" printer, at a hotel front desk that "ran out of paper" — is a scam signal, not a coincidence.

**What's the rate?**
Eh el-se3r? [Arabic: إيه السعر؟]
*Eh el-SE3-r?*

For licensed money-changers (*Sarraf*) and bank counters. Compare to the rate displayed on Google or the XE app before you change anything — Cairo airport's posted rates can be 10–15% worse than the bank rate in the city.

**Count it again, please.**
3eddha tani, min faDlak. [Arabic: عدها تاني، من فضلك]
*3ED-dha TAH-nee, min FAD-lak.*

The defensive move when a money-changer hands you a stack and pockets the rest. Egyptian pounds come in similar-colored notes (50 and 200 are both pinkish; 100 and 1 are both green) and short-counts are a documented Cairo and Sharm El Sheikh pattern. Counting back in front of the cashier closes the gap.

**I won't pay in dollars.**
(F) Mish hadfa3 bel-dolar. [Arabic: مش هدفع بالدولار]
*Mish hahd-FA3 bel-doh-LAHR.*
(M) Same form — gender-neutral.

For customs officials and visa-counter staff who demand cash dollars instead of the official $25 visa-on-arrival fee, or who ask for $10 and $15 supplements that do not exist on any official tariff. The legitimate visa fee is paid at the bank counter inside the arrivals hall, in exact dollars, against a printed sticker. Anything else is improvisation.

## Taxi and transport

**Use the meter please.**
(F) Sha3'al el-3addad, min faDlak. [Arabic: شغل العداد، من فضلك]
*Shah-3AHL el-3ah-DAD, min FAD-lak.*
(M) Same form.

Cairo white-and-yellow taxis are required to run a meter (*el-3addad*); most refuse with foreign passengers. Used as a phrase, this is mostly diagnostic — if the driver refuses, close the door without entering and walk to an Uber or Careem instead.

**I'm using Uber / Careem / InDrive.**
(F) Ana hastakhdem Uber. [Arabic: أنا هستخدم أوبر]
*AH-nah hahs-TAKH-dem OO-ber.*
(M) Same form.

Replace *Uber* with *Careem* or *InDrive* as needed. All three apps work in Cairo, Alexandria, Hurghada, Sharm El Sheikh, and (with reduced coverage) Luxor and Aswan. Saying this phrase to the curb-side driver who has waved you over closes the conversation immediately — they cannot compete with the in-app price.

**Stop here, please.**
WaSSal hena, min faDlak. [Arabic: وصل هنا، من فضلك]
*WAS-sal HE-nah, min FAD-lak.*

For taxi rides going off-route or stretching the meter. Said calmly, this is the standard way to end a ride mid-trip. *WaSSal* is the imperative of "to stop / arrive at." If the driver refuses to stop in central Cairo, Alexandria, or Sharm, the situation has escalated past haggling and you should be calling 126.

**I'll pay when we arrive.**
(F) Hadfa3 lamma niwSal. [Arabic: هدفع لما نوصل]
*Hahd-FA3 LAH-mah niw-SAL.*
(M) Same form.

The defensive answer to drivers who demand payment up-front and then renegotiate during the ride. The agreed price is the agreed price, and it is paid once, at the destination. Apps remove this entire negotiation — use them.

**The price is too high.**
El-se3r 3ali awi. [Arabic: السعر عالي أوي]
*El-SE3-r 3AH-lee AH-wee.*

The polite haggling opener at souks, calèche stands, and felucca docks. *Awi* (very) softens the complaint. This is the phrase that opens a real negotiation rather than a refusal.

## Useful at temples and monuments

**I have my own ticket.**
(F) 3andi tazkara bta3ti. [Arabic: عندي تذكرة بتاعتي]
*3AN-dee taz-KAH-rah b'TAH-3tee.*
(M) 3andi tazkara bta3i. [Arabic: عندي تذكرة بتاعي]
*3AN-dee taz-KAH-rah b'TAH-3ee.*

For freelance "guides" who attach themselves between the entrance and the ticket office and offer to "help" you buy the ticket. The official window inside the gate sells the same ticket for the official price. *Bta3ti* / *bta3i* — "mine" — is a possessive that closes the door on any "help."

**I don't need a guide.**
(F) Mish me7tagga morshid. [Arabic: مش محتاجة مرشد]
*Mish meh7-TAH-gah MOR-shid.*
(M) Mish me7tag morshid. [Arabic: مش محتاج مرشد]
*Mish meh7-TAHG MOR-shid.*

Said firmly at every temple, museum, and pyramid approach. Most "guides" who intercept you at the gate are unbadged freelancers, not licensed Egypt Tourism Authority guides. If you want a guide, book one through your hotel concierge or the Egypt Tourism Authority website before you arrive — never at the gate.

**I won't pay extra.**
(F) Mish hadfa3 ziyada. [Arabic: مش هدفع زيادة]
*Mish hahd-FA3 zee-YAH-dah.*
(M) Same form.

For the "tip" demanded by a temple guard who unlocks a door or an "extra fee" demanded after a camel-ride is complete. *Ziyada* literally means "extra" or "increase." Said calmly, this draws a clear line.

**Where is the official ticket office?**
Fein shibbak el-tazaker el-rasmi? [Arabic: فين شباك التذاكر الرسمي؟]
*Feyn SHEB-bak el-tah-ZAH-ker el-RAS-mee?*

Repeated from "Asking before you commit" because it is the single most powerful question at the temple gates. *Rasmi* — official — is the keyword that distinguishes the government counter from the man with the laminated badge.

**I will report this to the Tourist Police.**
(F) Hablagh el-shorTa el-siya7eyya. [Arabic: هبلغ الشرطة السياحية]
*Hah-BLAH3' el-SHOR-tah el-see-yah-7EYE-yah.*
(M) Same form.

The escalation phrase at temple ticket booths and museum gates where the price suddenly differs from the printed sign. *Hablagh* — "I will report" — is a verb every Egyptian recognizes. The Tourist Police kiosk is usually visible from the gate; pointing at it while saying this phrase often resolves the matter on the spot.

## Reading Egyptian pound amounts

Egyptian pounds (EGP, *gineih maSri*, جنيه مصري) come in 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 notes plus a few rarely-used coins. The 200-pound note (purple-pink) and the 50-pound note (light pink) are similar enough in low light that short-counts pivot on the difference. The 100-pound note (green) and the 1-pound note (also green, smaller, almost out of circulation) likewise. Prices are written in Western numerals on tourist-area signs; on hand-written receipts you may see Eastern Arabic numerals (٠ ١ ٢ ٣ ٤ ٥ ٦ ٧ ٨ ٩) which read the same way left-to-right as Western digits.

| Amount | Written | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 EGP | gineih wa7ed | *gee-NAY WAH-hed* |
| 5 EGP | khamsa gineih | *KHAM-sah gee-NAY* |
| 10 EGP | 3ashara gineih | *3ah-SHAH-rah gee-NAY* |
| 20 EGP | 3eshreen gineih | *3esh-REEN gee-NAY* |
| 50 EGP | khamseen gineih | *kham-SEEN gee-NAY* |
| 100 EGP | meet gineih | *MEET gee-NAY* |
| 200 EGP | meeteen gineih | *mee-TEYN gee-NAY* |
| 500 EGP | khomsomeyya gineih | *khom-soh-MEY-yah gee-NAY* |
| 1,000 EGP | alf gineih | *ALF gee-NAY* |

If a vendor or driver tells you a figure you do not understand, ask them to write it out:

**Please write the number.**
Ektib el-raqam, min faDlak. [Arabic: اكتب الرقم، من فضلك]
*EK-tib el-RAH-kam, min FAD-lak.*

They will almost always oblige.

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. In Egypt, polite firmness almost always works better than raised voices — though Egyptians are themselves comfortable with volume and you do not need to apologize for getting loud once a scam is clearly in progress. A calm *la' shokran* is already a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *sibni li-7ali* and *ma-tilmesnish* are firm without being rude, and *hastanna el-bolees* is the firmest card in the deck short of actually dialing 126.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar. Calm and firm beats loud and uncertain every time — and an English-speaking tourist who knows even ten words of Egyptian Arabic is read as a tourist who is paying attention, which is the single most effective scam deterrent in this book.
