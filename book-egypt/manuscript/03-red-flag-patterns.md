# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The forty-three scams in this book may sound different from each other, but almost all of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: The Handler Hostage — when the asset itself restrains you

This is the single most distinctive Egyptian scam pattern, and the one that shocks visitors most: the moment the camel rises, the felucca pushes off the steps, or the horse-drawn caleche begins to clip down the Corniche, the price you agreed to at the curb stops being the price the handler expects to be paid. The asset becomes a hostage situation. The camel will not kneel "because the ground is bad." The felucca captain stops rowing and lets the current take you upstream. The caleche driver claims the agreed sum was per person, not per ride, or that "pounds" meant US dollars rather than Egyptian.

You will see this on the Giza plateau every morning at the Sphinx ticket gate, where the **Camel Ride Hostage at Giza** flips a 50-EGP photo into a 500-EGP demand once your kid is four meters up. You will see it on the Luxor Corniche outside the Winter Palace Hotel, where **The Caleche Bait-and-Switch** turns 20 pounds into 200 the moment the carriage stops. You will see it in Cairo's Garden City, where the **Felucca Boat Bait-and-Switch** drifts you upstream until you pay the captain tip, the assistant share, and the fictitious fuel cost on a sail-powered boat. You will see it on the Luxor Corniche in **The Felucca Wind Tax**, where the captain kills the sail and demands extra for the outboard motor that was bolted to the stern from the start. You will see it on the Aswan Corniche in **The Felucca Bait-and-Pressure**, on the Hurghada El Mamsha promenade in the **Camel and Horse Ride Ransom**, and on the Mashraba beach strip in Dahab as the **Camel Ride on the Promenade Ransom**.

The pattern breaks the same way every time. Before you mount any animal or board any boat, type the full agreement on your phone screen — total price, route, dismount or return point, and the words "no extras for fuel, tip, assistant, or wind" — and have the handler photograph it back to you. Pay nothing on the way up; pay only when the rider is back on the sand or the boat is back at the dock. If a child is going up first, you go up second so that an adult is on the ground to negotiate. And book camel and felucca rides through your hotel concierge whenever possible, where the operator's reputation is on the line and the price is already settled.

## Pattern 2: The Authority Costume

The second pattern is the stranger in a uniform that almost looks right.

Real Egyptian Tourist Police wear light blue uniforms with shoulder insignia, carry official photo ID, and reach you by being stationed at major sites — they do not approach you on a sidewalk to collect fees. Real Ministry of Tourism guides carry laminated photo IDs with traceable numbers. Real airport staff stand inside marked counters, not at side desks before the immigration line. Almost every Egyptian "official-looking" approach inside a tourist zone is a costume.

You will see this in Cairo as **Fake Tourist Police**, where a man in tan khaki and a print-shop lanyard intercepts you near the Solar Boat Museum and demands a $30 cash "registration fee" that does not exist. You will see it inside Karnak's inner chambers and the Valley of the Kings as **The Fake Temple Guide**, where a freelancer in a galabiya and a homemade Ministry lanyard narrates ten minutes of hieroglyphs before demanding 200 pounds at the tomb exit. You will see it at the Egyptian Museum as **Fake Photo Fee at Monuments**, where someone in a navy polo demands 50 pounds per shot for photography that is already included in your ticket. You will see it most aggressively at Hurghada Airport as the **Airport Visa Counter Scam**, where a man in a Coral Travel or Alltours lanyard processes your visa for $35 ten meters before the bank counter that sells the same sticker for $25. And you will see it at the Qaitbay Citadel and the Bibliotheca Alexandrina as the **Qaitbay Citadel & Bibliotheca freelance-guide tail**, where a self-appointed guide walks beside you through the queue and names a fee at the end.

The pattern breaks on a single move. If anyone in casual clothes — or even a uniform — approaches you with a fee, a registration, or a "photography permit," ask to see the official sign or the printed price card. There never is one. Walk to the nearest marked official counter and verify what you owe with the staff inside it. Real Egyptian tourist police will be standing at the gate, not following you across a parking lot. The Tourist Police hotline is **126** and the Cairo line is **02-3376-7888** — save both before you arrive.

## Pattern 3: The Currency Mismatch

The third pattern is everywhere money changes hands. Egypt operates in three currencies at once — Egyptian pounds (EGP), US dollars, and the British pound for the UK package-tourism market — and almost every overcharge in this book exploits the gap between them.

At the airport, the visa-on-arrival is officially $25 in cash; freelancers in lanyards charge $35 and pocket the difference. In the arrivals corridor, drivers quote in dollars instead of pounds because $50 sounds like a fixed rate when 250 EGP would be the correct metered fare. At the only ATM in Borg El Arab Terminal 2 — a Euronet machine — the dynamic-currency-conversion screen marks every withdrawal up before the cash leaves the slot. In Aswan and Cairo souks, the **Spice Souk Switch** adds "free" hibiscus and seven-spice to your bag that arrive as line items on the bill. In Hurghada, **Currency Confusion and Street Exchange** swaps counterfeit British pounds for Egyptian, slips Moroccan dirham coins into your change, and exploits the visual similarity between the 50-piastre note and the 50-pound note. In Dahab, the **Assalah Souk Currency Short-Count** counts twenty 100-EGP notes into your hand at high speed when the stack is actually eighteen. In Aswan, **The Taxi Fare Bait-and-Switch** quotes "25 pounds" at the curb and demands "25 dollars" at the destination — the same word, two currencies, six times the fare.

The pattern breaks with three habits. Withdraw cash only from a CIB, QNB, or Banque Misr ATM and decline every dynamic-currency-conversion offer. Pay every formal charge — visa, hotel, dive operator, organized tour — by credit card on a platform that supports chargebacks. State every taxi, caleche, and felucca price in writing on your phone screen, including the literal word "Egyptian pounds" and the Arabic phrase *gineih masri*, and have the driver photograph it back. Familiarize yourself with the EGP note colors before you arrive: the 50-piastre is brown, the 10-pound is bright red, the 50-pound is purple, the 100 is olive, the 200 is reddish-brown. Count change in front of the cashier before stepping back from the window.

## Pattern 4: The Commission Detour

The fourth pattern runs on a fixed kickback. Almost every taxi or tour driver in Egypt earns somewhere between 30 and 50 percent of whatever you spend at the shops they "happen" to suggest. The shops are real businesses with real demonstrations and real product — the carving in front of you is genuine alabaster, the papyrus press is real plant fiber, the perfume is real essential oil. Only the price is engineered.

You will see this in Cairo as the **Papyrus and "Government Shop" Trap**, where a driver detours to an "Egyptian Papyrus Institute — Government Licensed" en route to Khan el-Khalili and a man in a starched lab coat sells $5 banana-leaf prints for $150. You will see it on the Luxor West Bank as **The Alabaster Factory Tour**, where the five-minute "free demonstration" between the ferry landing and the Valley of the Kings ends with a 200-pound vase priced at 1,500. You will see it in Hurghada as the **Perfume and Papyrus Factory Tour**, where the "government-certified" stop on a Luxor day trip sells $3 fragrance oil in vials priced at $80. You will see it in Aswan as the curated commercial loop inside **The Nubian Village Tourist Trap** at Gharb Soheil, where the crocodile photo is 50 EGP, the henna is 200, and the tea is 100, all at multiples of the going Aswan-souk rate. You will see the same pattern dressed up as the **All-Inclusive Wristband Shop Scam** along Hurghada's El Mamsha promenade, where shopkeepers reverse-engineer your hotel from your wristband color and pose as "front desk" staff with a guest-only deal.

The pattern breaks before the engine starts. Tell every driver before departure: "No shops. Direct to the site only." Reputable drivers respect this. If a driver insists, withhold or reduce his tip — the commission shops are how he supplements income, and a real customer is worth more than a shop kickback. If you genuinely want papyrus, alabaster, or fragrance oil, buy from the Egyptian Museum gift shop, a Khan el-Khalili stall, or a Luxor-town pharmacy, and compare prices across at least three sellers before any money changes hands. And never accept a "guest-only" deal from a shop two blocks from your hotel — every legitimate hotel partnership routes through the front desk and is billed to your room.

## Pattern 5: The Closure Redirect

The fifth pattern is a piece of theater. Halfway through the drive, a friendly stranger or your own driver tells you that the temple, the park, the ferry, or the museum is closed today — and they have an alternative ready before you have finished processing the news.

In Luxor, the **Closed Attraction Redirect** at the West Bank ferry landing tells you the Valley of the Kings is "closed for restoration" and steers you to an alabaster workshop where prices run 500 percent above street rates — meanwhile, the Valley is open, as it has been almost every day for decades. In Sharm El Sheikh, **The Ras Mohammed Closure Con** has your taxi driver receive a performative phone call halfway down the SH-65 desert road, announce that the park is "closed for sharks" or "closed for weather," and pivot to his cousin's mediocre private beach where entry is 300 EGP. The closure is fiction; Ras Mohammed almost never closes. The same pattern shows up in milder forms when a felucca captain announces "the wind has died" (a charge for the motor), a guard inside a tomb whispers about the "inspector" (a 200-pound shakedown for the photo he just authorized), or a boatman at the Shellal dock dismisses the posted 10-EGP Philae rate as "outdated."

The pattern breaks on verification. Call the site's visitor center directly before you depart — Ras Mohammed, the Valley of the Kings, the Bibliotheca, Philae — or have your hotel concierge confirm opening hours that morning. Insist your driver continues to the official gate so you can see the closure for yourself. Download an offline map so you can tell whether you are being driven the right way. And when a stranger volunteers a closure that no posted sign or barrier confirms, walk past them to the ticket office and verify with the staff inside.

## Pattern 6: The Bait-and-Switch Bundle

The sixth pattern is the bundle whose inclusions evaporate. A glossy brochure, a friendly tout, a too-good-to-be-true price for a multi-activity day, paid in cash, no receipt — and once you board the boat, the minibus, or the dive truck, the inclusions are renegotiated one by one.

In Sharm El Sheikh, **The Desert Safari Bait-and-Switch** sells a $30 quad-camel-dinner-stargazing bundle on the Naama Bay promenade and turns into a $120 evening once each promised inclusion is re-priced as an extra. **The Snorkeling Boat Hustle** off the Sharks Bay marina sells a $20 "Ras Mohammed and Tiran Island" trip whose first stop is a floating souvenir platform and whose actual snorkeling is twenty rushed minutes at a no-name reef. In Hurghada, the **Snorkeling Boat Trip Bait and Switch** at the marina sells a "luxury yacht" trip and produces an overcrowded fishing boat with no life jackets and a cascade of dock-side surcharges. In Dahab, the **Blue Hole Dive-Op Safety Cuts** advertises an introductory dive at $25 and rents you a regulator that fails at twelve meters with a "single-page waiver" that establishes nothing, on a reef with a documented death toll measured in the hundreds. In Sharm El Sheikh, **The Dodgy Dive Operator** runs the same pattern at $15. And in Dahab, the **Bungalow Hidden-Charge Trap** quotes 200 EGP "all-inclusive" per night and adds 600 EGP in undisclosed towel, AC, water, and "service" fees on checkout.

The pattern breaks before any cash changes hands. Book Red Sea dive trips, snorkeling, desert safaris, and Sinai excursions only through GetYourGuide, Viator, your hotel concierge, or an established named operator (Reef 2000, Big Blue, or H2O for Dahab diving; Camel Dive Club, Sinai Divers, or Red Sea Diving College for Sharm). Pay by credit card on a platform that supports chargebacks; never hand cash to a kiosk agent without a printed receipt naming the boat and the license number. Verify the lead instructor's PADI or SSI number on the certifying body's online check tool before the dive day. Inspect every regulator and BCD on land before you board the truck. Get a written list of inclusions in your booking confirmation, and treat anything not on it as a negotiation rather than an obligation. For accommodation, insist on a printed itemized rate sheet at check-in — towel, AC, water, service, all in Egyptian pounds — and pay only the printed total on checkout.
