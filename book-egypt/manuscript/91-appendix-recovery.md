# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are ordered by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — a souk shop, a taxi cab still parked, a "government papyrus institute," a snorkel boat at the dock — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including credit-card slips and "receipts" written on a torn paper pad. A signed slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, this is unlawful confinement under Egyptian law and the correct response is to dial **122** (police) or **126** (Tourist & Antiquities Police) and stay on the line. Tourist Police operators in Cairo, Luxor, Aswan, Hurghada, and Sharm El Sheikh are trained to respond in English; in smaller towns ask the dispatcher for an English speaker (*"Lo samaht, hadd biyetkallem ingilizi?"* — *"Please, anyone speaks English?"*).

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection, the business license (often displayed on the back wall), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward. If you suspect you are being followed by a handler or a tout team, walk to the nearest hotel lobby (any four-star or above will let you stand inside for a few minutes) before pulling out your phone to screenshot.

If your bag, wallet, or phone was just stolen, shout *"Haramy!"* (*"Thief!"*) — Egyptian bystanders, especially in tourist areas like Khan el-Khalili, Naama Bay, and the Luxor Corniche, are surprisingly responsive. Tourist Police presence is highest at the major monuments (Giza, Karnak, the Valley of the Kings, the Bibliotheca Alexandrina) and lower in market and corniche areas, so you may need to walk a few blocks toward a landmark before you find a uniformed officer.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest Tourist & Antiquities Police kiosk. Every major Egyptian tourist site has one. They are the single most useful resource for English-speaking travelers and are specifically chartered to handle tourist-scam complaints — far more so than the regular police, who do not always have an English speaker on shift. Say *"Ana et'naseebt"* (*"I was scammed"*) or *"Ana et'sara'na"* (*"I was robbed"*). Ask for a *mahdar* — the formal written police report. You will need the *mahdar* number for every subsequent step: the card chargeback, the travel-insurance claim, the embassy replacement passport. You do not need perfect Arabic for this; English will work in any tourist-area Tourist Police office.

Egypt does not have an online crime-reporting portal equivalent to Italy's *denunceonline*. The *mahdar* must be filed in person, and you should expect to spend thirty to ninety minutes at the station. Bring your passport, your hotel address, and the screenshots from the previous step. Decline any informal "settlement" the officer offers to broker between you and the scam venue — this is a known pattern that ends with the scammer paying a small bribe and no formal record being filed. Insist on the written *mahdar* with a numbered case reference.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from Egypt and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call. If the scam was a restaurant overcharge, a taxi rip-off, or a souk swap, also call your bank's fraud line directly — the number on the back of your card — because fraud desks sometimes freeze faster than the Visa or Mastercard emergency lines.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the *mahdar* number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Visa and Mastercard chargeback rules give you strong rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend credit over debit cards for every Egyptian trip.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved (a "guide" who held it as collateral; a hotel that copied it during check-in and then declined to return it without payment of an inflated bill; an airport "official" who took it during a fake registration), notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later, and your embassy will advise on whether a replacement is needed. The US Embassy in Cairo (+20 2-2797-3300) handles US-citizen passport replacement for the entire country; the UK, Canadian, Australian, German, and French embassies in Cairo handle their own nationals (numbers in Appendix C).

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the *mahdar* number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position.

If the scam happened to be an off-platform rental, an unverified Nile-cruise booking, or a wire-fraud hotel booking, open the dispute on the booking platform (Booking.com, Hotels.com, Viator, GetYourGuide, Airbnb) as well as with your card issuer. Platforms can sometimes recover deposits the card company cannot, and several Egypt-specific cruise-and-tour aggregators (Memphis Tours, Travco, Memphis Travel) will engage with disputes if you have written evidence of misrepresentation.

## Within the first week

File a formal complaint with the **Egyptian Tourism Authority** (Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities) at **[egypt.travel](https://egypt.travel)** — they maintain a tourism-complaints intake email and they coordinate with the Tourist Police on patterns of repeated complaints against named operators. A single complaint will not directly recover your loss, but it feeds the data that drives Tourist Police enforcement against specific bad operators.

For Tourist Police–level scams (rather than airport/customs scams), your hotel concierge can also escalate on your behalf — most four-star and above hotels have a direct line to the local Tourist Police office and will call them in your presence. This is especially useful in Hurghada, Sharm El Sheikh, and Luxor, where hotel concierges deal with these complaints on a near-weekly basis and know which operators are repeat offenders.

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business's name, the address, the exact transaction details, the *mahdar* number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name and location only.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of English-speaking hospitals in each Egyptian city is in Appendix C — the Anglo-American Hospital (Zamalek, Cairo), Hurghada International Hospital, Sharm International Hospital, and Luxor International Hospital are all reliable English-speaking options. At the hospital, ask specifically for a *toxicology screen* — most Egyptian hospitals do not run one as part of standard intake and you will need to request it explicitly.

In Egypt, the ambulance number is **123** (or **122** for general emergencies, which will dispatch an ambulance). If you are inside a venue and drink-spiking is suspected — particularly relevant in the Sharm El Sheikh and Hurghada nightclub circuits documented in this book — have a friend call for an ambulance *before* you leave, not after. The aggressive bill-pressure pattern documented in those chapters often escalates once the scammer realizes you are about to walk out.

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate a replacement. Consular registration (STEP for US citizens, Smartraveller for Australians, the FCDO Travel Aware app for UK citizens, and equivalents for other nationalities) is worth doing before you travel, specifically for situations like this. Egypt's tourist hotline at **126** can also dispatch a Tourist Police officer to your hotel or the hospital if you ask — and this is the lowest-friction way to make a formal report when you are physically in distress.
