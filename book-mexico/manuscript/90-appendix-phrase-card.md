# Appendix A: Spanish Exit-Phrase Card {-}

These are the phrases most likely to end a scam in Mexico. They are arranged by situation. For each phrase you will see the **English translation**, the **Mexican Spanish** spelling, and a practical pronunciation guide in italics.

A practical suggestion: before your trip, take a screenshot of this appendix on your phone, and bookmark any phrase you think you are most likely to need. Mexican vendors in tourist areas almost always speak some English — Cancún and Cabo are bilingual by default, and most CDMX restaurants in Roma and Polanco are too — but saying even a single Spanish phrase ("*no, gracias*" and "*el taxímetro, por favor*" especially) signals you are not an easy mark.

## A note on pronunciation

Mexican Spanish is **phonetic**: every letter is always pronounced the same way, and stress is usually on the second-to-last syllable (or marked with an acute accent when it isn't). Once you know the alphabet, you can read Spanish aloud. The tricky letters for English speakers:

- **c** before **e** or **i** is "s" as in "sister" — *centro* = "SEHN-tro"; before **a/o/u** it is hard "k" — *Cancún* = "kan-KOON"
- **ch** is "ch" as in "chair" — *muchacho* = "moo-CHA-cho"
- **g** before **e** or **i** is the soft "h" of *gente* = "HEN-teh"; before **a/o/u** it is hard — *gato* = "GAH-toh"
- **h** is **silent** — *hola* = "OH-lah", *hotel* = "oh-TEL"
- **j** is the soft "h" — *Juárez* = "HWAH-res", *México* = "MEH-hee-ko"
- **ll** is "y" as in "yes" — *llamar* = "yah-MAHR"
- **ñ** is "ny" as in "canyon" — *señor* = "seh-NYOR"
- **r** is tapped lightly; **rr** is rolled — *perro* (dog) = "PEH-rro"
- **x** varies: in *México* and *Oaxaca* it is "h" (Mexican-indigenous derivation); in most other words it is "ks" — *taxi* = "TAH-ksee"
- **z** is "s" in Mexico — *Veracruz* = "veh-rah-KROOS"

Stress: when not marked, on the second-to-last syllable. *Hola* (hello) is "**OH**-lah." When marked with an acute accent, on the marked syllable. *México* is "**MEH**-hee-ko" (stress on the first syllable, marked).

## A note on politeness

Mexican politeness is warm and unhurried. **Por favor** ("please") at the start or end of a sentence, and **gracias** ("thank you") at the end, are enough to flag you as polite. Adding **señor** or **señora** (sir/madam) when speaking to anyone older than you, or in any service exchange, is appreciated. Mexican Spanish uses *usted* (the formal "you") in commercial situations by default — every phrase below is written that way.

## Asking before you commit

**How much is it?**  
¿Cuánto cuesta?  
*KWAHN-toh KWES-tah?*

**How much does it cost in pesos?**  
¿Cuánto cuesta en pesos?  
*KWAHN-toh KWES-tah en PEH-sos?*

Use this whenever a price is quoted in dollars but the menu is in pesos. The conversion the vendor offers is almost always worse than your card's interbank rate.

**Please start the meter.**  
Prendan el taxímetro, por favor.  
*PREN-dahn el tahk-SEE-meh-tro, por fah-VOHR.*

Say this the instant you get into a taxi. If the driver refuses, get out. Alternatively, use **Uber** or **Didi** — both work in all major Mexican cities and pre-quote the fare.

**Please show me the menu in pesos.**  
Muéstreme la carta en pesos, por favor.  
*MWES-treh-meh lah KAR-tah en PEH-sos, por fah-VOHR.*

**Do you have an itemized price list?**  
¿Tiene una lista de precios desglosada?  
*TYEH-neh OO-nah LEES-tah deh PREH-syos des-glo-SAH-dah?*

Use before any transaction that might be priced by weight or "special": fish, lobster, mezcal flights.

**What is the price per kilo?**  
¿Cuál es el precio por kilo?  
*KWAHL es el PREH-syo por KEE-lo?*

Use before fish, seafood, *al pastor* charged by weight, or any per-kilo item.

**Do you accept credit cards?**  
¿Aceptan tarjeta de crédito?  
*ah-SEP-tahn tar-HEH-tah deh KREH-dee-toh?*

Most Mexican venues larger than a *taquería* accept Visa and Mastercard (Amex coverage is patchier outside resort zones). If a shop insists on cash, ask why — it is frequently a chargeback-avoidance tell.

**I want a receipt with your tax ID, please.**  
Quiero una factura con su RFC, por favor.  
*KYEH-roh OO-nah fac-TOO-rah kon soo EH-reh-EH-feh-SEH, por fah-VOHR.*

Mexican retail is required to issue an itemized invoice (*factura*) with the seller's RFC tax-ID number. A vendor who refuses is unregistered and a *factura*-less transaction means PROFECO has no jurisdiction over your dispute.

## Declining and leaving

**No, thank you.**  
No, gracias.  
*NO, GRAH-syas.*

The single most useful phrase in this appendix. Firm, polite, understood everywhere. Walking away is not rude.

**I'm not interested.**  
No me interesa.  
*NO meh een-teh-REH-sah.*

Stronger than *no, gracias*. Use when an approach keeps talking after the first refusal. The phrase is the standard Mexican way to close an unwanted sales pitch.

**Please leave me alone.**  
Por favor, déjeme en paz.  
*Por fah-VOHR, DEH-heh-meh en PAS.*

Used firmly but without raised voice. The verb *dejar en paz* (literally "leave in peace") is the standard Spanish formula.

**Don't touch me.**  
No me toque.  
*NO meh TOH-keh.*

Step back. Most touts disengage at this point.

**I'm waiting for someone.**  
Estoy esperando a alguien.  
*es-TOY es-peh-RAHN-doh ah AHL-gee-en.*

Socially-acceptable pause while you wait out an approach.

## Responding to a scam in progress

**I'm calling the Tourist Police.**  
Voy a llamar a la Policía Turística.  
*VOY ah yah-MAHR ah lah po-lee-SEE-ah too-REES-tee-kah.*

This is the firmest card in the deck. **911** is the universal Mexican emergency line (police, ambulance, fire) and operates nationwide with English-capable dispatchers in major tourist cities. **Policía Turística** desks operate in Mexico City (Florencia 20, Col. Juárez, +52 55 5208 1030) and Cancún (+52 998 884 1107); other cities rely on the regular *Policía Estatal* dispatched via 911. Say the phrase, hold up your phone, and dial 911.

**I'll call my bank.**  
Voy a llamar a mi banco.  
*VOY ah yah-MAHR ah mee BAN-ko.*

Say this if a restaurant, shop, or taxi driver disputes a credit-card transaction.

**I was scammed. Please help.**  
Fui estafado. Por favor, ayúdeme.  
*FWEE es-tah-FAH-doh. Por fah-VOHR, ah-YOO-deh-meh.*

(For a female speaker: *Fui estafada* — *FWEE es-tah-FAH-dah*.) The verb *estafar* ("to scam / to defraud") is the specific Spanish term. It communicates what happened with more urgency than any English translation. Use this with a police officer, a hotel front-desk clerk, or any bystander you want to enlist.

**I'd like an itemized bill.**  
Quiero una cuenta desglosada, por favor.  
*KYEH-roh OO-nah KWEN-tah des-glo-SAH-dah, por fah-VOHR.*

Forces the establishment to account for every item — a legitimate place hands it over; a tourist trap stalls.

**I didn't order this.**  
No pedí esto.  
*NO peh-DEE EH-stoh.*

Use calmly about any unrequested dish, bottle, or "service" that appears on your bill.

**This is not what we agreed.**  
Esto no es lo que acordamos.  
*EH-stoh no es lo keh ah-kor-DAH-mos.*

For taxi fares over a quoted price, jet-ski "damage" claims, and tour-operator price-creep.

## Getting help

**Where is the nearest police station?**  
¿Dónde está la estación de policía más cercana?  
*DOHN-deh es-TAH lah es-tah-SYOHN deh po-lee-SEE-ah mahs ser-KAH-nah?*

**I want to file a report.**  
Quiero presentar una denuncia.  
*KYEH-roh preh-sen-TAR OO-nah deh-NOON-syah.*

The formal complaint is a *denuncia*, filed at a *Ministerio Público* (MP) office — produces a stamped report (*acta*) with a case number that your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will require.

**Does anyone speak English?**  
¿Hay alguien que hable inglés?  
*EYE AHL-gee-en keh AH-bleh een-GLEHS?*

Major Mexican tourist-police desks and most 911 operators in CDMX, Cancún, Cabo, and PV have at least one English-speaking officer on duty.

**Please call 911.**  
Por favor, llame al 911.  
*Por fah-VOHR, YAH-meh ahl NWEH-veh OO-no OO-no.*

**Help! Thief!**  
¡Auxilio! ¡Ladrón!  
*ow-KSEE-lyo! lah-DROHN!*

Shouted in a crowded *mercado* or street, this is understood by every bystander. The word *ladrón* (thief) carries immediate legal weight; *auxilio* (help) is the Spanish equivalent of "help!"

## Reading peso amounts

Mexican prices are written with the abbreviation **MX$** before the number when distinguishing from US dollars, or with the bare **$** when the context is unambiguous. Pesos use a dot for thousands and a comma for decimals (1,500 MX$ = one thousand five hundred pesos; 4.50 MX$ = four pesos fifty *centavos*) — the same convention as English.

- Small-denomination coins: 5, 10, 20, 50 *centavos*; 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 peso coins
- Paper notes: 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000 pesos
- One US dollar is roughly 17–20 pesos as this edition goes to press; verify on xe.com in the week before you travel.

| Amount | Written | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 peso | un peso | *OON PEH-so* |
| 5 pesos | cinco pesos | *SEEN-ko PEH-sos* |
| 10 pesos | diez pesos | *DYES PEH-sos* |
| 50 pesos | cincuenta pesos | *seen-KWEN-tah PEH-sos* |
| 100 pesos | cien pesos | *SYEHN PEH-sos* |
| 500 pesos | quinientos pesos | *kee-NYEN-tos PEH-sos* |
| 1,000 pesos | mil pesos | *MEEL PEH-sos* |

If a vendor quotes a figure you don't understand, ask them to write it:

**Please write the number down.**  
Escriba el número, por favor.  
*es-KREE-bah el NOO-meh-ro, por fah-VOHR.*

## A note on tone

The phrases above are polite, not aggressive. Mexican conflict-resolution culture rewards calm — raising your voice at a *mercado* shopkeeper or a taxi driver almost always makes things worse. A calm *no, gracias* is already a complete refusal. If you need to escalate, *no me interesa* ("not interested") and *por favor, déjeme en paz* ("please leave me alone") are both firm without being rude, and *voy a llamar a la Policía Turística* ("I'm calling the Tourist Police") is the firmest card in the deck.

One last thing worth saying out loud: you do not have to be fluent to use any of this. You just have to say the key word clearly, keep your voice level, and not walk back your no. Scammers read your body language before they read your grammar — and a calm *no, gracias* with the Spanish on your lips is, in itself, proof you know enough to make the interaction harder than they expected.
