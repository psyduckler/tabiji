# The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns {-}

The one hundred and fourteen scams in this book may sound different from each other, but most of them are variations on just six patterns. Learn the patterns and you do not need to memorize every scam. You will recognize a new one the moment it starts to unfold, even if it has never been written down.

Read each of these once. Then let them settle in your head the way you remember a home-safety tip or a road sign.

## Pattern 1: A friendly stranger who approaches you first is running a script

This is the single most important habit you can take to Mexico. The instant a warm, English-fluent man — it is almost always a man, and almost always smiling — steps into your path near the Mexico City Zócalo, the Cancún Hotel Zone arrival lobby, the Tulum ruins parking lot, the Puerto Vallarta Malecón, the Cabo San Lucas marina, or the cruise-port exit at Cozumel, the interaction that follows is a rehearsed script. You are not being greeted. You are being sorted.

The **Zócalo Aztec-dancer "tip" approach** sends a feathered ceremonial dancer across your path who insists on taking a photo with you and then demands MX$500 per person — including children — at the moment the camera comes down. The **Cancún Hotel Zone "free breakfast" hand-out** runs at the Punta Cancún strip and the airport lobby: a friendly representative offers a $50 USD breakfast voucher in exchange for "ninety minutes" at a time-share presentation that runs four hours, blocks your transport home until you sign, and produces contracts that PROFECO has annulled hundreds of times. The **Playa del Carmen Quinta Avenida "bracelet for the lady"** approach drops a string bracelet onto a tourist's wrist, ties it tight, and demands US$20 cash. The **Tulum cenote "park ranger"** approach intercepts you on the dirt access road and charges a "park entry fee" that does not exist; the actual *Parque Nacional* and ejido fees are paid at the visible booth at the gate. The **Cabo marina "captain Pedro" timeshare-broker** at the harbor sells "discount yacht charters" that turn out to be a presentation room for a different operator.

None of these interactions start with you asking for help. That is the tell.

The defense is one habit, applied automatically: when any stranger starts a conversation with you near a famous site or in a tourist corridor, reply "*no, gracias*" — "no, thank you" — and keep walking. Do not break stride. Do not let them walk with you. If they follow, step into the nearest OXXO, Starbucks, Sanborns, or any large chain and use the staff as a buffer. Walking away is not rude. The interaction was not a social invitation.

*You will see this pattern in: the Zócalo Aztec-dancer photo-tip extraction (Mexico City), the Cancún Hotel Zone time-share "free breakfast" approach, the Playa del Carmen Quinta Avenida bracelet drop, the Tulum cenote-road "park ranger" entry-fee shakedown, the Cabo San Lucas marina yacht-charter broker, the Puerto Vallarta Malecón "free tequila tasting" tout, the Cozumel cruise-port "official guide" intercept, and the San Miguel de Allende Jardín "free walking tour" approach that pulls you into a kickback gift shop.*

## Pattern 2: "The meter is broken" and the airport-taxi flat-rate

The second pattern is taxis. Mexico has metered taxis, regulated *sitio* (taxi-stand) services with posted flat rates, and Uber, Didi, and inDriver legal in most major cities — but at **Benito Juárez International (MEX) in Mexico City**, at **Cancún International (CUN)**, at **Puerto Vallarta International (PVR)**, at **Los Cabos International (SJD)**, and at the cruise terminals in **Cozumel** and **Mazatlán**, a meaningful share of tourist rides begin with a tout in a vest claiming to be the "authorized" or "official" taxi service and quoting a flat price two to four times the legitimate rate.

At **Benito Juárez (MEX)**, the legitimate "Sitio Taxi" desks inside Terminals 1 and 2 charge prepaid flat rates of MX$250–MX$330 to Polanco/Roma/Condesa as this edition goes to press; touts walking the arrivals hall quote MX$800–MX$1,500 for the same trip. The Metrobús Line 4 from MEX Terminal 2 to the Centro Histórico costs MX$30 and takes 50 minutes. At **Cancún International (CUN)**, the regulated flat from the airport to the Hotel Zone is roughly US$45–US$65 prepaid; touts in white guayabera shirts inside the arrivals lobby quote US$85–US$120 with a "free welcome drink" upsell that ends at a time-share desk. At **Puerto Vallarta (PVR)**, the regulated flat to the Romántica Zone is roughly MX$300–MX$400; touts quote MX$600–MX$1,000.

The "*el taxímetro está descompuesto*" phrase — "the meter is broken" — is the specific tell. Mexican meters are not broken. The flat-rate variant is its more common cousin: the driver simply refuses to use the meter and quotes a fixed price.

The defense is short and universal. At Mexico City's airport, take the Metro Line 5 from Terminal 1 (Terminal Aérea station) or Metrobús Line 4 from Terminal 2 — both cost under MX$30 and are scam-immune. At Cancún International, use the official **ADO** bus to the Hotel Zone (~US$15) or pre-book a private transfer through your hotel. For city rides anywhere in Mexico, **Uber** and **Didi** work in all major cities (CDMX, Cancún, Playa del Carmen, Mérida, Guadalajara, Puerto Vallarta, Cabo, Mazatlán, Oaxaca, Puebla, Querétaro, Monterrey) with the fare quoted up front. If you must take a street taxi, step into the car and say "*prendan el taxímetro, por favor*" — "please start the meter." If the driver refuses, get out before the door closes.

*You will see this pattern in: the Benito Juárez airport flat-rate taxi tout (CDMX), the Cancún International "authorized taxi" overcharge, the Puerto Vallarta airport flat refusal, the Los Cabos shuttle-tout cycle, the Cozumel cruise-arrival taxi flat, the Mazatlán cruise-port "Old Town tour" overcharge, the Tulum-to-Cancún airport private-transfer markup, and the general meter-refusal pattern at every Mexican airport in this book.*

## Pattern 3: Prices that aren't posted will surprise you later

The third pattern is the unposted price. Mexican tourist-facing restaurants in the Cancún Hotel Zone, on Quinta Avenida in Playa del Carmen, on Tulum's beach road, on Puerto Vallarta's Malecón, and around the Mexico City Zócalo will sometimes — not always — quote you a price that is deliberately vague. "Special for you." "*Por kilo*" with no posted per-kilo rate. "Market price." "It depends on the catch." Each of these phrases hides a later surprise.

This pattern accounts for a meaningful share of the overcharging scams in this book. In **Tulum's beach-road "boutique" restaurants**, a menu listing "*pescado del día*" with no per-kilo price often produces bills of US$80–US$120 for fish portions worth a quarter of that at a posted-menu *cocina económica* in Tulum Pueblo two kilometers inland. On **Cancún's Hotel Zone strip**, a "house margarita" ordered at a beach club with no posted price arrives at US$25 when the published mixed-drink list is US$8–US$12. In the **Mexico City Mercado Roma** and the surrounding Roma Norte block, a "tasting menu special" from a chalkboard with no list prices runs MX$1,800–MX$2,500 against an *à la carte* equivalent of MX$700. In **Playa del Carmen's Quinta Avenida**, a "two-for-one beer" promotion turns out to be valid only with a US$15 cover charge that the tout did not mention. In **Puerto Vallarta's Olas Altas** restaurant strip, a "lobster combo" priced "by weight" is presented after cooking at MX$3,500 against an unposted base of MX$2,400.

The defense is a single habit, repeated before every transaction: ask for the price in writing, or point to a posted menu with numbers. For fish or seafood "by the kilo," confirm the per-kilogram price in pesos before the fish is cooked, and ask the kitchen to weigh it in front of you on a visible scale. For beach clubs, day passes, sunset cruises, and "private" cabanas, ask for the *desglose* — itemized — price up front, with the cover charge, the minimum spend, and any service fee written down. For restaurants without posted prices on the window menu or on the chalkboard, keep walking — there is always another restaurant within 100 meters in any Mexican tourist corridor.

*You will see this pattern in: the Tulum beach-road no-price seafood overcharge, the Cancún Hotel Zone "house margarita" trick, the Mexico City Roma chalkboard tasting-menu inflation, the Playa del Carmen Quinta Avenida hidden-cover scam, the Puerto Vallarta Olas Altas lobster-by-weight markup, the Mérida Plaza Grande "Yucatecan special" overcharge, the Cabo Marina sunset-cruise unposted minimum, and the general menu-without-prices pattern at every tourist corridor in this book.*

## Pattern 4: The "scratched ATV," the "broken jet ski," the "cracked snorkel mask"

The fourth pattern is the damage claim. You rent something — an ATV in Cabo, a jet ski in Cancún, a scooter in Cozumel, a snorkel set in Playa del Carmen, a paddleboard at Puerto Escondido — or you touch something — a *huipil* in San Cristóbal, a piece of obsidian in the Templo Mayor area, a hand-painted *talavera* tile in Puebla — and when you return or decline it, the operator points at an imaginary or pre-existing issue and demands US dollars (almost always dollars, not pesos) for "damage."

**Cancún, Cozumel, Playa del Carmen, Tulum, and Cabo San Lucas** are the epicenters of the jet-ski-and-ATV variant. The operator takes a US$300–US$500 cash deposit on a jet ski, lets you ride for an hour, and on return points at a scratch near the engine well that was already there and demands US$200–US$400 "for repair." The Cabo and Cozumel **dune-buggy / ATV** version runs identically: US$200 cash deposit, disputed scratch, US$100 deducted; sometimes the contract is in Spanish and signed in English without translation. The **Playa del Carmen snorkel-set rental** at the Cozumel-ferry-terminal beach charges a US$30 "cracked-mask fee" for normal wear. The **Puerto Vallarta and Sayulita paddleboard rentals** charge "fin damage" deposits routinely. The **Mexico City Centro Histórico jewelry-shop "you touched the silver, you cracked it"** version runs the same psychology on a piece of *Taxco* silver.

The defense is photographic and verbal. Before you accept any rental, take date-stamped video of the equipment from eight angles — front, back, left, right, underside, the engine well, the places where scratches typically appear, and a close-up of the meter or odometer. Hand over a photocopy of your passport rather than the real one; keep the real passport in the hotel safe. Pay every rental with a chargeback-able credit card — never the cash deposit alone. At an artisan or *mercado* shop, keep your hands to yourself unless you are buying. If a damage claim is made at return, produce the photos on the spot. If the operator still refuses to return the deposit, call the **Policía Turística** (Tourist Police) where it operates — Cancún at +52 998 884 1107, CDMX at 55 5208 1030 — or the universal **911** line, and wait for them to arrive.

*You will see this pattern in: the Cancún jet-ski damage deposit, the Cozumel scooter-scratch scam, the Playa del Carmen snorkel-set "cracked-mask" fee, the Tulum-area ATV jungle-tour deposit-hold, the Cabo San Lucas dune-buggy contract trick, the Puerto Vallarta paddleboard fin-damage hold, the San Cristóbal de las Casas textile-shop "you stained it" pressure, and the Puebla *talavera*-shop breakage claim.*

## Pattern 5: A "policeman," an "INAH inspector," or a "guide" is legitimate only with a real ID

The fifth pattern is the counterfeit authority. Real Mexican police — *Policía Federal*, *Policía Estatal*, *Policía Municipal*, and the dedicated **Policía Turística** in Mexico City and Cancún — do not conduct currency checks on the sidewalk, do not demand cash fines payable on the spot, and do not inspect your wallet at a *cantina*. Real INAH (*Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia*) inspectors at Teotihuacán, Chichén Itzá, Palenque, Monte Albán, and Tulum carry visible federal IDs and operate from posted booths. Real *Tour Operador Federal*–licensed guides carry an ID with a Ministry of Tourism (*SECTUR*) registration number. Real silver from Taxco, *talavera* from Puebla, *huipiles* from Chiapas, and Mayan-replica obsidian from Mexico City museums come with provenance receipts that a reputable shop can produce on demand.

The pattern runs in two forms. The first is the **fake officer**: a man in a vest or polo shirt claims to be "tourist police" or an "*inspector de INAH*" and wants to see your wallet for a "counterfeit-peso check" or to inspect your "permit" to take photos at the ruins. The second is the **fake expert**: a "guide" at Teotihuacán, a "*maestro talaverero*" in Puebla, an "antique" silver dealer in Mexico City's Centro, or a "Mayan blessing" *chamán* at the Tulum cliffside who demands a "donation" of US$50 after a thirty-second prayer. Both work on the same instinct — authority-of-stamp psychology — and both collapse when you politely say "*quisiera llamar a la Policía Turística*" — "I would like to call the Tourist Police."

The defense is simple and universal. If anyone on the street identifies themselves as an official and wants to examine your wallet, passport, phone, or belongings, refuse and walk into the nearest large chain (OXXO, Starbucks, Sanborns) or a hotel lobby. Dial **911** or the local Policía Turística line and put the call on speaker. For "certified" silver, *talavera*, *huipil* textiles, or "antique" coins and ceramics, insist on an itemized invoice (*factura*) with the shop's RFC tax-ID number, and buy only from shops that accept a chargeback-able credit card — the chargeback right is worth more than any certificate. At INAH archaeological sites, buy only the guide options sold at the official ticket booth at the gate.

*You will see this pattern in: the Mexico City Centro fake-tourist-police currency check (CDMX), the Teotihuacán "INAH inspector" photo-permit shakedown, the Tulum cliffside "Mayan blessing" donation scam, the Puebla *talavera*-shop "master artisan" markup, the Mexico City Centro Taxco-silver "appraisal" con, the Cancún Hotel Zone "fake INAH agent" Chichén Itzá tour upsell, the Oaxaca Templo de Santo Domingo "free history guide" tip extraction, and the Mérida central plaza "free Mayan ceremony" donation push.*

## Pattern 6: If you pay before you have the thing, you probably won't get the thing

The sixth pattern is the one you can only catch by reading the fine print after you have committed — so this pattern is really about what to pin down before you click *Confirmar* or hand over cash.

You sign up for a **Cancún or Cabo time-share presentation** on the promise of a "$50 USD breakfast voucher" and "ninety minutes" of your time, and four hours later you are signing a US$25,000 contract that PROFECO will spend a year unwinding. You book a **Tulum cenote-tour** through a roadside chalkboard for MX$800 per person; the "tour" is downgraded to a 30-person budget version at the last minute and the MX$800 becomes unreclaimable. You hand US$300 cash to a **Mexico City Centro silver shop** for a "Taxco antique" piece that is a recent reproduction worth a fraction. You agree to a **Puerto Vallarta Marietas Islands boat tour** at US$80 per person; on board you are charged US$20 separately for each of the "free lunch," "free snorkel," and "free open bar" the tout promised.

All of these work because you committed before you knew the terms — and then the terms, the vendor, the venue, or the platform were not what you thought they were.

The defense is a set of habits that apply to every transaction you make in Mexico:

- **Book accommodation** only through platforms that offer pay-through-platform guarantees and English-language dispute resolution (Booking.com, Expedia, Airbnb, Hotels.com, Hostelworld). The Mexican Ministry of Tourism (*SECTUR*) maintains a registry of *Distintivo H* food-safety-certified and *Distintivo S* sustainable-tourism-certified accommodations; PROFECO publishes a black list of repeat-offender hotels at **profeco.gob.mx**.
- **Decline every time-share approach**, full stop. The Cancún, Cabo, and Puerto Vallarta time-share industries are responsible for a meaningful share of foreign-tourist consumer complaints to PROFECO every year. There is no "free breakfast" worth ninety minutes — let alone four hours — in a sales room.
- **Book Tulum, Chichén Itzá, and Palenque tours** only through licensed operators on Viator, GetYourGuide, Klook, or directly through your hotel concierge. Never pay cash to a roadside chalkboard or a street tout.
- **For Marietas Islands, Whale-Shark, and Cenote-Cluster boat tours** in Puerto Vallarta, Holbox, and the Riviera Maya: book through the marina's official booking office or through Viator/GetYourGuide, not a beach tout.
- **Pay every rental, excursion, hotel, or tour** by credit card (Visa, Mastercard, American Express). Only a credit card gives you a chargeback right that works from abroad; cash and bank transfers to a personal CLABE do not.
- **For silver, *talavera*, *huipil* textiles, or Mayan-replica artifacts**: insist on a *factura* with the shop's RFC tax-ID number and pay by credit card. If the price is over US$200, photograph the certificate and the item side-by-side before leaving the shop.

### One more: the peso–dollar currency-switch game

Mexican tourist venues play a currency-switch game that specifically targets foreign visitors. A menu displays prices in pesos, but the waiter quotes the bill in US dollars using the "restaurant's exchange rate" — which is worse than the xe.com rate, often by 8–15%. A boat-tour operator quotes US$100 on a chalkboard and then charges MX$2,400 (which is roughly US$135 at the real rate). The defense is to insist on paying in the currency of the posted price. If the menu is in pesos, pay in pesos. If the price is in dollars, pay in dollars via credit card — the card processor will use the actual interbank rate, not the venue's. ATMs in OXXO, banks (BBVA, Banorte, Santander), and 7-Eleven dispense pesos at the official exchange rate; airport ATMs from operators like *Cibanco* and *Banco Azteca* charge fees three to five times higher than the bank ATMs five minutes outside the terminal.

*You will see this pattern in: the Cancún and Cabo time-share "free breakfast" sign-up, the Tulum cenote-tour roadside booking, the Mexico City Centro Taxco-silver "antique" bait-and-switch, the Puerto Vallarta Marietas Islands hidden-fee boat, the Playa del Carmen Quinta Avenida "free salsa class" up-sell, the Oaxaca mezcal-tour kickback shop, the airport ATM fee-stacking, and the general peso–dollar currency-switch pattern at every tourist-facing restaurant and shop in this book.*
