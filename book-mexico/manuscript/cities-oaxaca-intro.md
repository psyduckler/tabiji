Oaxaca de Juárez is Mexico's craft and culinary capital — *mezcal*, *mole negro*, *tasajo*, *huipil* weaving, the carved *cantera verde* of the Templo de Santo Domingo — and its scam ecosystem concentrates along three corridors: the **Andador Macedonio Alcalá** pedestrian strip from the Zócalo to Santo Domingo, the **Mercado 20 de Noviembre** food hall, and the **Valle de Tlacolula** loop through El Tule, Teotitlán del Valle, Mitla, Monte Albán, and the Santiago Matatlán *palenques*. *El Universal Oaxaca*, *NVI Noticias*, and *Milenio* have covered the Día de los Muertos accommodation cancellations and the mezcal-route commission pattern through 2025.

The **mezcal tour commission kickback** is the volume risk along the Alcalá. Touts sell "exclusive mezcal tour MX$1,800" packages that visit two *palenques* instead of the promised four or five and pad time at a "family friend" producer pushing MX$2,500 "premium *añejo*" bottles that retail at MX$800–$1,200 in town; the worm — moth larva, not a quality marker — is a marketing prop on unlicensed bottles, never on real *Denominación de Origen* mezcal. Book Coyote Aventuras, Experience Mezcal, or Mezcoaxaca direct (MX$800–$1,200 for a six-hour, four-*palenque* day with producers named in writing), or skip the tour and buy from Mezcaloteca (Reforma 506) or Mezcalería In Situ (Reforma 602), where staff price *joven*, *reposado*, and *añejo* transparently from MX$400 to MX$1,500.

The risk radiates out in three corridors. The **Monte Albán "mandatory guide" pitch** quotes MX$400 per person at the entrance — no guide is required, self-guided maps are free, and licensed optional guides charge MX$200–$350 *per group*; the ticket itself is MX$95, the Tule Tree MX$10 (ignore the MX$50 "donation" tables), and the rug-shop pressure at Teotitlán del Valle runs MX$12,000–$35,000 for natural-dye wool that should price at MX$1,200–$2,500 per square meter. The **Pasillo de Humo overcharge** at Mercado 20 de Noviembre sells *tasajo* and *chorizo* to tourists at MX$350–$380 per kilo (locals pay MX$120–$180), then layers an undisclosed MX$80 grill fee and a MX$180 "tortillas + salsas" bundle — ask prices before the *carnicero* cuts and a fair dinner for two lands at MX$150–$200. And the **Día de los Muertos accommodation pattern** (October 28 to November 3) sees Airbnb hosts cancel six-month bookings and relist at MX$8,000–$15,000 a night, with "VIP cemetery tours" and "rooftop viewing packages" sold at MX$2,500–$3,500 for parades and *panteón* visits that are free public events.

A safe default: book accommodations nine to twelve months ahead through Booking.com, Hyatt, or Marriott direct (chains have recourse against cancellation in a way Airbnb rarely does); take Uber or Didi from Xoxocotlán Airport (OAX) for MX$180–$250, or the official Transporte Terrestre booth at MX$220–$280; eat at Casa Oaxaca Café (Constitución 104), Zandunga (García Vigil 512), or Criollo (Francisco I. Madero 129) for full meals at MX$250–$450 with posted menus; rent cars only from airport-branch Hertz, Europcar, or Budget after photographing every scratch, dent, and tire. Call **911** for any emergency, the **Policía Turística Oaxaca** on **+52 951 501 5000 ext. 23406** (English available), and file a *denuncia* at the **Fiscalía General del Estado de Oaxaca** *Ministerio Público* on +52 951 514 3822 within 48 hours.
