# Appendix C: Emergency Contacts {-}

Every phone number in this appendix was verified current at the time of publication, but phone numbers do change, consulates move, and hospitals occasionally renumber their switchboards. If a number below fails to connect, search the organization's name plus *México* or the city name, and you will almost always find the current line on the first page of results.

**Before you travel**, we strongly recommend you double-check your own embassy or consulate's current number directly on its official website — travel.state.gov (US), gov.uk (UK), travel.gc.ca (Canada), dfa.ie (Ireland), smartraveller.gov.au (Australia). Save the main switchboard and the after-hours duty-officer line on your phone before departure, and screenshot this appendix so you can read it on the Mexico City Metrobús or in a *palapa* on Holbox where your signal cuts out.

## The numbers that work everywhere in Mexico

These are the lines to dial from any phone — mobile or landline, Mexican SIM or roaming — anywhere in the country. All of them are free.

- **911 — universal emergency:** police, ambulance, and fire. Operational nationwide since 2017. English-capable dispatchers in CDMX, Cancún, Cabo, Puerto Vallarta, Mérida, Guadalajara, and most major tourist cities.
- **065 — Cruz Roja:** Mexican Red Cross ambulance dispatch. Still operational; redundant with 911.
- **078 — SECTUR national tourist-assistance line:** Spanish and English. 24/7. Can dispatch to local authorities and arbitrate with SECTUR-registered operators (hotels, tour packagers, licensed guides).
- **089 — anonymous tip line:** *Denuncia anónima*. For reporting incidents you witness, not for filing your own complaint.
- **800 468 8722 — PROFECO:** Procuraduría Federal del Consumidor. Consumer-protection complaints, time-share rescission, *factura* disputes, merchant misrepresentation.
- **800 999 8080 — CONDUSEF:** financial-services consumer protection (banks, ATMs, cards).
- **800 903 9200 — INM:** Instituto Nacional de Migración. Visa, FMM card, and immigration issues.

## If you lose your passport

1. **Report it first** — go to the nearest *Ministerio Público* and file an *acta de denuncia* (*pasaporte extraviado* — lost passport). You will leave with the signed and stamped report. This is the document your embassy wants to see before issuing an emergency replacement.
2. **Call your embassy's main switchboard** — or its after-hours line if it is a weekend — and request an emergency passport appointment. US, UK, Canadian, Irish, and Australian embassies in Mexico City and the major consulates in Cancún, Guadalajara, Mérida, Puerto Vallarta, and Cabo all issue same-day or next-day emergency travel documents; the fee varies.
3. **Bring** the *acta de denuncia*, any photo ID you still have, passport photos (photo booths are at every Mexican OXXO), and the payment method your embassy specifies.
4. **Exit-stamp clearance**: before flying out with an emergency travel document, airlines departing CDMX (MEX), Cancún (CUN), Guadalajara (GDL), Puerto Vallarta (PVR), Los Cabos (SJD), and Mérida (MID) see emergency documents regularly; INM (Mexican immigration) knows the format. You may also need to replace your **FMM tourist card** at the same time — the INM office at the airport processes this.

## If your phone is stolen

1. **Freeze the SIM** by calling your carrier from any other phone: Telcel **\*111** or +52 55 2581 4811, AT&T **\*611**, Movistar **\*611**. If you use a foreign SIM or eSIM, use the app to suspend.
2. **File an *acta de denuncia*** at the nearest MP with your device's IMEI (if you recorded it) and serial number.
3. **Lock and wipe remotely** via Apple Find My or Google Find My Device.
4. **Change passwords immediately** for any account whose app was installed on the phone. Start with email.

## If your credit card is stolen or skimmed

1. **Freeze the card** by calling the number on the back (or use the issuer's app's "lock card" feature). Every minute matters.
2. **Report to police** with an *acta de denuncia*. Insurance reimbursement and the card issuer's fraud-dispute process both want the case number.
3. **Dispute the charges** with your issuer. The *acta de denuncia* speeds the process.
4. **Watch for follow-on phishing** — a skimmed card often leads to SMS and WhatsApp phishing pretending to be your bank. Never click a link in these messages; go to your bank's app directly.

## A closing note

This appendix is meant to be read once on the plane, screenshot for offline use, and forgotten about for the rest of your trip. Phone numbers change. Consulates move. Hospitals renumber their switchboards. If any number in this list fails, **911** will still work, and your embassy's website will still have the current line for your country.

Travel safely, travel calmly, and if the worst happens, remember: a US$300 Tulum cenote-tour overcharge is an inconvenience, not a crisis. Filing an *acta de denuncia*, opening a PROFECO case, freezing a card, and calling a consulate are ordinary procedures — Mexican authorities and consular staff handle these cases every day, and most travelers resolve everything within a few hours and return to their trip. Mexico is not a dangerous country for the great majority of tourist itineraries. Help is close, and English is closer than you think.
