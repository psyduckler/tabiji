# How to Use This Book {-}

Mexico is extraordinary. It is also the third-most-visited country in the Americas — over forty million international tourists annually as of this edition — and that volume has produced an unusually well-organized layer of tourist scams. Mexico City's Benito Juárez airport runs a "*sitio* taxi" overcharge cycle that *Reforma* and *El Universal* have covered for over fifteen years. Cancún's Hotel Zone runs a time-share-presentation circuit that PROFECO opens hundreds of consumer-protection cases on every quarter. Tulum's beach roads run a "park entry" cash-shakedown pattern at the entrance to the Mayan ruins and the cenotes that *Riviera Maya News* has documented repeatedly since 2019. The downtown Zócalo in Mexico City runs an "Aztec dancer" tip-extraction script that the Policía Turística have been issuing English-language warning cards about for years.

PROFECO (*Procuraduría Federal del Consumidor*) and SECTUR (the *Secretaría de Turismo*) jointly log tens of thousands of foreign-visitor complaints every year, most of them concentrated in airport corridors, the Riviera Maya, the Pacific resort coast, and the Centro Histórico of major colonial cities.

We want you to come home with stories about the *taquerías* in Roma Norte, the cenote dives in the Yucatán jungle, the El Arco sunset in Cabo, and the pink salt flats of Las Coloradas — not about a 4,000-peso airport-taxi bill or a $2,500 USD time-share contract you signed during a "free breakfast."

That is why this book is short and practical. It documents one hundred and fourteen specific scams that trip up first-time visitors to Mexico, and it tells you the exact moves that stop them. Nothing in it is theoretical. Every scam has been reported in Mexican press (*Reforma*, *El Universal*, *Milenio*, *La Jornada*, *Riviera Maya News*, *The Yucatan Times*, *Mexico News Daily*), cross-referenced against PROFECO and SECTUR bulletins, state *fiscalía* records, and recent traveler accounts on r/mexico, r/MexicoCity, r/cancun, r/oaxaca, r/Yucatan, and r/travel.

## How each scam is written

Every scam entry follows the same short format. First, the name of the scam, the category it falls into (transport, overcharging, ATM-skim, time-share, tourist trap, and so on), and how serious and how common it is. Then two short sections: **How this scam works** — a plain narrative of how it unfolds in real time, with real Mexican peso (MX$) or US dollar amounts where we have them — and **How to avoid it**, the specific moves that shut it down. Each entry ends with where it happens and a four-panel comic — drawn in the *Lotería tarjeta* tradition of the Don Clemente deck — showing the exchange so you can spot the pattern visually, not just in words.

## The single most important chapter

The next chapter, **The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns**, is the most important part of this book. Most scams in Mexico are variations on six patterns. Learn the patterns and you will spot a new scam the moment it starts — even one that is not in this book. Spend ten quiet minutes on that chapter before your trip. The rest of the book will then read as practical examples of patterns you already understand.

## The three bonuses at the back

After the city chapters, you will find three appendices that we hope you treasure more than the city chapters themselves.

The first is the **Spanish exit-phrase card**. Short phrases you can say, word for word, at the moments when your politeness would normally get you into trouble. Mexican Spanish is one of the world's friendliest dialects, but tourist scams everywhere live in the second after a vendor reads "I don't want to be rude" on your face. The phrases on that card are the polite, firm refusals — *no me interesa, gracias*; *prendan el taxímetro, por favor*; *llame a la Policía Turística* — that close the door without raising the temperature.

The second is the **recovery playbook**. A step-by-step flow of what to do in the first fifteen minutes, hour, day, and week after a scam. Most scam books only tell you how to avoid scams. If the worst happens, you want this — including how to file a *denuncia* at the *Ministerio Público*, when to call PROFECO instead of police, and how to use your credit card's chargeback right against a Cancún time-share contract.

The third is the **emergency contacts appendix**. SECTUR Tourist Assistance lines, the Policía Turística desks in Mexico City and Cancún, hospitals with English-speaking care, US and Canadian consulates in every major destination, and the Visa, Mastercard, and Amex 24/7 lines that work from inside Mexico — organized so you can find what you need in seconds.

## A note on currency

Mexico's currency is the **Mexican peso** (MX$, MXN). The peso is freely convertible; ATMs across the country dispense it; almost every tourist-facing venue accepts it. US dollars are widely *accepted* in tourist zones — Cancún, Playa del Carmen, Tulum, Cabo, Puerto Vallarta — but accepting them at a tourist menu is almost always done at a marked-up "house exchange rate" that costs you 8–15% more than paying in pesos. In this book we write peso amounts as **MX$** and US-dollar amounts as **$** or **US$**; the symbol convention is what you will see on Mexican menu boards, taxi meters, and PROFECO bulletins.

## A suggested way to use the book

A week or two before your trip, read the first two chapters and skim the three appendices. Read the chapters for the cities and beach destinations you will be visiting. Take a screenshot of the phrase card and the emergency contacts on your phone. If anything in this book turns out to apply during your trip, open it again on a Mexico City Metrobús or in your hotel room and check what to do.

You will almost certainly not need most of it. That is the point.
