Mérida is the white-stone capital of Yucatán — the *Centro Histórico* of low *cantera* houses around the Plaza Grande, the long Paseo de Montejo with its 19th-century mansions, and the gateway to Chichén Itzá and the cenote ring 90 kilometres east. Yucatán has consistently ranked among Mexico's safest states by the federal *Secretariado Ejecutivo*, with violent-crime indices several multiples below the national average; the scam picture is therefore tourist-trap economics rather than predation. The risk concentrates in three corridors: the **Calle 60 and Paseo de Montejo** craft-shop strip through the *Centro Histórico*, the **Mérida-Pisté-Valladolid highway loop** that funnels day-trippers to Chichén Itzá, and the **Mérida International (MID) airport** ground-transport zone with its pre-paid-only flat-rate system. *Diario de Yucatán*, the local Spanish-language daily, and *Yucatan Times*, the English-language site read by the area's expatriate community, both cover the highway-restaurant pattern regularly, and Mexico's tourist-assistance line **078** logs the entrance-ticket scams at the Chichén Itzá gate.

The **Chichén Itzá "skip-the-line" ticket fraud** is the volume risk. Online resellers sell entrance "skip-the-line upgrades" at MX$1,500-2,500 for a UNESCO site whose actual entry — MX$614 total for foreigners, split as MX$90 INAH federal plus MX$524 Yucatán state across two separate counters — is paid at the gate with a 5-15 minute queue at the 8 AM opening. There is no skip-the-line ticket. The same scam is layered with roadside touts on Route 180 approaching Pisté who flag rental cars into "park here cheap" lots far from the official entrance (the official lot is MX$80, 24-hour) and shuttle drivers who add MX$100 per person for the walk back. The defense: book entrance via boletomuseo.inah.gob.mx or pay at the gate, park only at the official lot, and arrive at 8 AM before the Cancún tour buses swell the crowd tenfold by 11 AM.

The risk radiates out in three corridors. The **"Mi Lindo Yucatán" highway-restaurant kickback** on Route 180 between Mérida and Valladolid is the canonical named trap — tour buses and rental-car drivers are routed in for a "free *cochinita pibil* demonstration" that becomes a MX$350-600 lunch at twice the fair Valladolid price, plus a "traditional Mayan craft" shop attached at three to five times market rates. The **Calle 60 "authentic Mayan cooperative" shops** mark up *huipiles*, hammocks, and amber jewellery at MX$1,500-4,000 for items that cost MX$200-600 at Mercado Municipal García Rejón two blocks away; genuine hand-loomed hammocks run MX$600-1,800 direct from the Tixkokob village cooperatives 25 kilometres east. And the **MID airport pre-paid kiosks** quote MX$500-800 for the 20-minute drive into Centro when the official Transporte Terrestre booth charges MX$275-350 flat-rate with a printed receipt.

A safe default: take the official Transporte Terrestre booth at MID arrivals (MX$275-350), or book Uber and meet the driver at parking level P2 rather than the kerb. Eat in Valladolid at Yerbabuena del Sisal, Conato 1910, or Mesón del Marqués (MX$150-250 per person) rather than any "recommended" highway stop. Shop crafts at Mercado Municipal García Rejón on Calle 65 — comparison-shop the same item there before buying anything on Calle 60 or Paseo de Montejo. Visit community-cooperative cenotes (Yokdzonot MX$150, San Antonio Mulix MX$450 combo, Cuzamá MX$300) rather than tour-package "VIP private access" pitches at MX$1,800. For trouble, **911** is universal, the *fiscalía* in Yucatán handles the *denuncia*, and the SECTUR tourist-assistance line **078** runs 24/7 in English and Spanish — Mérida itself is small enough that it relies on the state *fiscalía* and 078 rather than a dedicated tourist-police desk.
