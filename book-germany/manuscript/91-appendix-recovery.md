# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are organized by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — a carpet shop in Berlin Mitte, a Christmas-market stall at Nuremberg's Christkindlesmarkt, a tourist restaurant off Munich's Marienplatz, a tailor on the Cologne Hohe Straße, an Oktoberfest tent with a suddenly inflated bill — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. A signed credit-card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, dial **110** (Polizei) — English-capable dispatchers are standard in Berlin, Munich, Frankfurt, Hamburg, Cologne, Düsseldorf, Stuttgart, and Dresden; in smaller cities say "English, please" and wait.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection, the shop's business registration plaque (*Gewerbeanmeldung*), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. German consumer-protection law takes photo evidence seriously; a clear shot of a non-posted price or an inflated bill is often enough to win a chargeback on its own.

If your bag or wallet was just stolen in the street, shout "**Haltet den Dieb!**" (*HALL-tet den deep*, "stop the thief") — German bystanders typically respond, and every major German city has a strong bystander-intervention culture on street theft.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest **Polizeirevier** (district police station) or **Bundespolizei** office if you are inside a Hauptbahnhof or an airport. Say "*Ich möchte eine Anzeige erstatten*" — "I want to file a report." You will walk out with a signed **Anzeige** — the German police report document — and a case number (*Aktenzeichen*). This is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

Germany also operates a free online pre-filing portal for non-urgent reports at **onlinewache.polizei.de**, with separate portals for each state (Bavaria, Berlin, Brandenburg, Hamburg, NRW, etc.). The online system lets you file in English and print the confirmation — useful if you are already in transit when the scam becomes clear.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the **Anzeige** reference number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later.

Call **115** (*Behördennummer*, the German general government-services line, 08:00–18:00 Mon–Fri) if you are unsure which agency to escalate to. The 115 operators speak English in most cases and can route you to the correct consumer-protection desk.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. German hotels are legally required to register your passport at check-in (*Meldepflicht*), but they should never hold the original; a photocopy or scan is all the law requires.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary, timestamps, the Anzeige reference number, and photographs of the bill.

If the scam involved an off-platform Airbnb or a wire-fraud hotel booking, open the dispute on the booking platform as well.

## Within the first week

File a formal consumer-protection complaint with the **Verbraucherzentrale** (consumer protection agency) of the state where the scam occurred. Each German state operates its own Verbraucherzentrale; find the right one at **verbraucherzentrale.de** and file online. The Verbraucherzentrale does not itself prosecute — it escalates documented complaints to the state consumer-protection authority and mediates with the merchant.

For **transport overcharging** — taxi, FREE NOW, Uber, tour minibuses — file a parallel complaint with the local **Ordnungsamt** (municipal regulatory office) of the city. Every city has a public taxi-complaint desk; in Berlin it is the LABO (Landesamt für Bürger- und Ordnungsangelegenheiten); in Munich the Kreisverwaltungsreferat; in Frankfurt the Ordnungsamt on the Mainkai.

For **banking and payment disputes** — escalate to **BaFin** (the German Federal Financial Supervisory Authority) at **bafin.de** if the merchant-bank side is not responsive. BaFin takes foreign-visitor complaints seriously.

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Do not name individual staff members; describe the business by its official name only. German defamation law is moderate — stick to verifiable facts and quote specific euro amounts and dates.

## If your phone or SIM was stolen

Call your carrier immediately to suspend the line. The major German carriers:
- **Deutsche Telekom (T-Mobile / Telekom)** — dial **2202** from the affected line, or **+49 2203 451 45 26** internationally
- **Vodafone Germany** — dial **1212** from the affected line, or **+49 172 12 12** internationally
- **O2 / Telefónica** — dial **0176 88 85 5282**, or **+49 176 88 85 5282** internationally
- **1&1 / Drillisch** — dial **0721 9600**

If you are on a tourist eSIM (Airalo, Saily, Holafly), the app will let you suspend the line in a few taps.

File a police report including your phone's **IMEI** number (print it from Settings → About → IMEI before you travel). The IMEI lets the Polizei and your carrier flag the device and is required for any insurance claim.

Lock and wipe remotely via Apple's Find My or Google's Find My Device.

## If the scam involved drink-spiking or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window — typically under 12 hours for benzodiazepines and GHB. A list of 24/7 hospitals with English-speaking emergency staff in each German city is in Appendix C.

**Emergency numbers in Germany:**
- **110** — Polizei (police, all states)
- **112** — Rettungsdienst / Feuerwehr (ambulance / fire, pan-European)
- **116 117** — Ärztlicher Bereitschaftsdienst (non-emergency medical on-call, nationwide)
- **0800 1110 111** — Telefonseelsorge (crisis counseling, English available, 24/7 free)

Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate an emergency replacement. The U.S. Embassy in Berlin is at Pariser Platz 2 (open Mon–Fri); regional U.S. Consulates General operate in Munich, Frankfurt, Hamburg, and Düsseldorf.
