# Appendix A: German Exit-Phrase Card {-}

This is the single most useful page in the book. Take a screenshot on your phone and add it to your Favorites album before you fly. The phrases are short, the pronunciations are rough but workable, and each one is tuned for the exact moment when your English politeness would otherwise cost you money.

Germans in tourist zones almost all speak some English — but saying the decisive phrase in German collapses a scam script faster than English will. It signals that you are not a first-day arrival and that you are prepared to escalate. That is the whole point.

## Pronunciation notes (one minute)

German spelling is almost perfectly phonetic once you know four rules:

- **ei** rhymes with "eye" (*nein* = "nine")
- **ie** rhymes with "ee" (*Bier* = "beer")
- **ch** is a soft throat-clear, like the *ch* in Scottish "loch" (*ich* = "ikh")
- **w** is pronounced like English *v* (*Wasser* = "VAH-sair")
- **z** is pronounced like English *ts* (*zehn* = "tsayn")
- The umlauts **ä ö ü** are roughly "ay," "er" (no r), and "ew"
- **ß** (*eszett*) is pronounced as a double *s*

That's it. German pronunciation is more predictable than French or English.

## 1. At the bazaar, Christmas market, or gift shop

When a stranger approaches you with a clipboard, a prayer card, a bracelet, or a "free sample":

> **Nein, danke.** (*nine DAHN-keh*) — No, thank you.

Keep walking. Do not slow down. Do not make eye contact with the clipboard.

If they follow:

> **Bitte lassen Sie mich in Ruhe.** (*BIT-teh LAH-sen zee mikh in ROO-heh*) — Please leave me alone.

If they persist at a Christmas market stall:

> **Ich rufe die Polizei.** (*ikh ROO-feh dee POH-lee-tsai*) — I am calling the police.

The moment "Polizei" enters the conversation, 95% of street scammers disappear.

At a shop, to ask the price before the merchant wraps anything:

> **Wie viel kostet das?** (*vee FEEL KOS-tet das*) — How much does that cost?

To ask for the price in writing:

> **Können Sie mir den Preis aufschreiben?** (*KER-nen zee meer den PRICE OWF-shry-ben*) — Can you write the price down for me?

To decline after the salesperson has invested time:

> **Ich möchte nicht kaufen.** (*ikh MERKH-teh nikht KOW-fen*) — I don't want to buy.

You do not owe the shopkeeper anything for their time. Walking away without buying is a normal German shopping interaction.

## 2. At a restaurant or beer hall

Before you sit down, ask to see the menu with prices:

> **Kann ich die Speisekarte mit Preisen sehen?** (*kahn ikh dee SHPY-zeh-KAR-teh mit PRY-zen ZAY-en*) — May I see the menu with prices?

If a waiter tries to add a cover charge or "service fee" that isn't on the menu:

> **Das steht nicht auf der Speisekarte.** (*das shtate nikht owf dair SHPY-zeh-KAR-teh*) — That is not on the menu.

To ask for the bill:

> **Die Rechnung, bitte.** (*dee REKH-noong BIT-teh*) — The bill, please.

To signal you are paying by card (and to decline dynamic currency conversion):

> **Mit Karte, in Euro, bitte.** (*mit KAR-teh in OY-roh BIT-teh*) — By card, in euros, please.

If the bill contains items you did not order:

> **Ich habe das nicht bestellt.** (*ikh HAH-beh das nikht beh-SHTELT*) — I did not order this.

## 3. With a taxi or rideshare

Before you get in a taxi, confirm the meter will run:

> **Taxameter, bitte.** (*tahk-SAH-meh-tair BIT-teh*) — Meter, please.

If the driver says the meter is "broken":

> **Dann nehme ich ein anderes Taxi.** (*dahn NAY-meh ikh ine AHN-der-es TAHK-see*) — Then I will take a different taxi.

There is always another taxi in the German rank. The "meter is broken" line is the specific tell.

If you notice the meter is on Tariff 2 during daytime:

> **Tarif eins, bitte.** (*tah-REEF ines BIT-teh*) — Tariff 1, please.

At the end of the ride, to request a receipt:

> **Quittung, bitte.** (*KVIT-toong BIT-teh*) — Receipt, please.

A printed receipt with the taxi license number is your ticket to a consumer complaint and, if needed, a credit-card chargeback.

## 4. At a train station, tram, or U-Bahn

If a man in a vest claims to be a *Kontrolleur* (ticket inspector) and demands a cash fine:

> **Zeigen Sie mir bitte Ihren Dienstausweis.** (*TSY-gen zee meer BIT-teh EER-en DEENST-ows-vice*) — Please show me your service ID.

Real inspectors have a photo ID with the DB, BVG (Berlin), MVV (Munich), HVV (Hamburg), or other regional transit logo. They are never alone; they always work in pairs of two or three. They never demand cash — the €60 fine is always invoiced by post or payable by card.

If they refuse to show ID:

> **Dann rufe ich die Polizei.** (*dahn ROO-feh ikh dee POH-lee-tsai*) — Then I am calling the police.

To ask for the ticket office:

> **Wo ist der Fahrkartenschalter?** (*voh ist dair FAR-kar-ten-SHAHL-ter*) — Where is the ticket counter?

Buy your regional and long-distance tickets from the DB counter or the official DB Navigator app — never from a stranger who "can help" at the machine.

## 5. At your hotel

To decline a hotel-concierge "special tour" or "recommended restaurant" pitch:

> **Danke, ich habe schon gebucht.** (*DAHN-keh ikh HAH-beh shone geh-BOOKHT*) — Thank you, I have already booked.

Hotel concierges often receive kickbacks from tour operators and restaurants; their recommendation is not neutral. Booking.com and TripAdvisor are better neutral filters.

If a room "upgrade fee" is demanded at check-in:

> **Ich habe die Buchungsbestätigung.** (*ikh HAH-beh dee BOO-khoongs-beh-SHTAY-tee-goong*) — I have the booking confirmation.

Show the printed or screenshot confirmation. A legitimate hotel will not charge an "upgrade fee" on a room you already paid for through Booking.com or Expedia.

## 6. In a genuine emergency

Call **110** for the Polizei (police) or **112** for medical/fire emergencies. Both numbers work from any phone — no SIM card required — and almost every dispatcher in cities over 100,000 people speaks English. Say:

> **Hilfe, bitte — ich bin überfallen/bestohlen/verletzt worden.** (*HILL-feh BIT-teh — ikh bin ü-ber-FAH-len / beh-SHTOH-len / fer-LETZT VOR-den*) — Help, please — I was attacked / robbed / injured.

Give your location as precisely as you can:

> **Ich bin in [neighborhood], in der Nähe von [landmark].** (*ikh bin in [neighborhood] in dair NAY-heh fon [landmark]*) — I am in [neighborhood], near [landmark].

Stay on the line. German emergency dispatch is fast and professional.

## 7. At the Polizei station — filing a report

To request to file a police report:

> **Ich möchte eine Anzeige erstatten.** (*ikh MERKH-teh ine-eh AHN-tsy-geh air-SHTAH-ten*) — I want to file a report.

The word for a formal police report is **Anzeige**. You will walk out with a signed document, the Polizei's case number, and a contact officer. This document is what your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will ask for.

To ask for a copy in English:

> **Haben Sie das auf Englisch?** (*HAH-ben zee das owf ENG-lish*) — Do you have this in English?

Large-city Polizei stations in Berlin, Munich, Frankfurt, Hamburg, Cologne, and Düsseldorf can usually produce an English-language summary alongside the German original. If they cannot, use your phone's camera OCR and Google Translate to read what you are signing — never sign a German-only document you don't understand.

## 8. A few warm phrases to soften the rest

Not every interaction is a scam. Most Germans, even in tourist zones, are direct, helpful, and proud of their city. A brief German greeting will open doors that English alone will not.

> **Guten Tag.** (*GOO-ten tahk*) — Hello / good day. (The default greeting.)
> **Guten Morgen.** (*GOO-ten MOR-gen*) — Good morning.
> **Guten Abend.** (*GOO-ten AH-bent*) — Good evening.
> **Auf Wiedersehen.** (*owf VEE-dair-zay-en*) — Goodbye.
> **Entschuldigung.** (*ent-SHOOL-dee-goong*) — Excuse me / sorry.
> **Sprechen Sie Englisch?** (*SHPREH-khen zee ENG-lish*) — Do you speak English?
> **Ich verstehe nicht.** (*ikh fair-SHTAY-eh nikht*) — I don't understand.
> **Danke schön.** (*DAHN-keh shern*) — Thank you very much.
> **Bitte schön.** (*BIT-teh shern*) — You're welcome / please.

In Bavaria and the south, you will hear **Grüß Gott** (*grooss got*) instead of Guten Tag. Reply with the same phrase. In Berlin you will sometimes hear **Moin** (*moyn*) — a casual "hi" imported from the north. Reply with Moin back.

*Gute Reise* — have a good trip.
