# How to Use This Book {-}

Germany is, on paper, one of the safest travel destinations in Europe. Violent crime is rare, public transit is punctual, police are professional, and Google Maps works. But every year about thirty-five million international tourists pass through Germany's airports, train stations, Christmas markets, and beer halls — and where there are thirty-five million tourists, there is a well-rehearsed scam economy running quietly in parallel with the legitimate one. The Bundespolizei logs tens of thousands of foreign-visitor theft and fraud incidents a year, most of them concentrated on a short list of predictable corridors: the Brandenburger Tor clipboard plaza, the Munich Marienplatz and Viktualienmarkt, the Cologne Cathedral square, the Hamburg Reeperbahn and Hauptbahnhof, the Frankfurt banking district near the main station, the Oktoberfest beer tents during the two-week Wiesn run, and the Neuschwanstein ticket counter.

The German press — *Der Spiegel*, *Süddeutsche Zeitung*, *Bild*, *Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung*, *Die Welt*, *Tagesspiegel* — covers these scams every tourist season. Deutsche Welle and *The Local Germany* run regular English-language warnings. The Bundespolizei (federal police) publishes incident bulletins at *bundespolizei.de*, and the consumer-protection agencies (*Verbraucherzentrale*) keep running registers of known fraud operators.

We want you to come home from Germany with stories about the Brandenburger Tor at sunset, a Weißwurstfrühstück in Munich, a Rhine cruise past the castles at Loreley, and a *Glühwein* at a Nuremberg Christkindlesmarkt — not about a €400 taxi from Berlin Hauptbahnhof to the hotel, or a €120 Oktoberfest bill for two *Maß* of beer you never ordered.

That is why this book is short and practical. It documents eighty-eight specific scams that trip up first-time visitors to Germany, and it tells you the exact moves that stop them. Nothing in it is theoretical. Every scam has been reported in German press (*Der Spiegel*, *Süddeutsche Zeitung*, *Bild*, *Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung*, *Die Welt*, *Tagesspiegel*, *Deutsche Welle*) or in Bundespolizei and Landespolizei incident records, and confirmed by recent traveler accounts on r/germany, r/berlin, r/munich, r/cologne, and r/solotravel.

## How each scam is written

Every scam entry follows the same short format. First, the name of the scam, the category it falls into (pickpocket, transport, overcharging, tourist trap, ticket fraud, and so on), and how serious and how common it is. Then two short sections: **How this scam works** — a plain narrative of how it unfolds in real time, with real euro (€) amounts where we have them — and **How to avoid it**, the specific moves that shut it down. Each entry ends with where it happens and — for Germany — a four-panel Heinrich-Zille-style pen-and-ink comic showing the exchange so you can spot the pattern visually, not just in words.

## The single most important chapter

The next chapter, **The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns**, is the most important part of this book. Most scams in Germany are variations on six patterns. Learn the patterns and you will spot a new scam the moment it starts — even one that is not in this book. Spend ten quiet minutes on that chapter before your trip. The rest of the book will then read as practical examples of patterns you already understand.

## The three bonuses at the back

After the city chapters, you will find three appendices that we hope you treasure more than the city chapters themselves.

The first is the **German exit-phrase card**. Short phrases you can say, word for word, at the moments when your politeness would normally get you into trouble. German shares Latin-alphabet roots with English, and almost everyone in tourist zones speaks some English — but saying the three or four decisive phrases in German will collapse a scam attempt faster than any English refusal will. Each phrase appears in English, in the German spelling, and with a practical pronunciation guide. Take a screenshot on your phone.

The second is the **recovery playbook**. A step-by-step flow of what to do in the first fifteen minutes, hour, day, and week after a scam. Most scam books only tell you how to avoid scams. If the worst happens, you want this.

The third is the **emergency contacts appendix**. Polizei stations, Bundespolizei offices, hospitals with English-speaking emergency rooms, consulates, and the major foreign-card 24/7 lines that work from inside Germany, organized so you can find what you need in seconds.

## A note on currency

Germany's currency is the **euro (€)**. Unlike some of Europe's weaker-currency countries, euros are the posted price at essentially every tourist-facing venue — carpets, Christmas-market stalls, Neuschwanstein tickets, Oktoberfest *Maß* (liter steins), Rhine cruises. You will not encounter a lira-euro currency-switch game the way you would in Istanbul. What you will encounter: ATM-rate "dynamic currency conversion" (DCC) at point-of-sale, which lets the merchant or ATM operator pick a worse exchange rate than your home bank would. The defense is a single habit — always choose to be charged in euros (EUR), not in your home currency, when a German ATM or card terminal asks.

## A note on cash

Germany is more cash-intensive than most American travelers expect. Smaller restaurants, bakeries, Weihnachtsmarkt stalls, older Bavarian beer halls, and some taxis still operate cash-first. Carry €50–100 in cash at any given time and a chargeback-able credit card as your main payment method. Giro-card (EC-card, the German domestic debit standard) is accepted almost everywhere but will not work with foreign accounts — use a Visa or Mastercard instead.

## A suggested way to use the book

A week or two before your trip, read the first two chapters and skim the three appendices. Read the chapters for the cities you will be visiting. Take a screenshot of the phrase card and the emergency contacts on your phone. If anything in this book turns out to apply during your trip, open it again on the U-Bahn or in your hotel room and check what to do.

You will almost certainly not need most of it. That is the point.
