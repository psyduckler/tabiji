Nafplio is the first capital of modern Greece — a Venetian-fortified harbor town on the Argolic Gulf, two hours southwest of Athens by KTEL Argolida bus, and the standard base for Mycenae and Epidaurus archaeological day trips. The old town's Plateia Syntagmatos, the Vasileos Konstantinou pedestrian strip, and the Bouboulinas waterfront form the tourist core. The scam economy here is quieter than Athens or Mykonos — but the financial bite per incident is substantial because the targets are well-prepared travelers booking weeks in advance for what they expect to be the safe, gentle, "off-cruise" Greek experience.

The risk radiates from three corridors. At Plateia Syntagmatos, the kiosk-style Euronet ATMs run dynamic-currency-conversion markups at 6–10% above the wholesale euro rate — every other Greek-bank-branded ATM on the same square (Piraeus, Alpha, Eurobank, NBG) charges nothing extra if you select "charged in euros." On the Athens-to-Nafplio transfer route, open-marketplace driver bookings (Booking.com's driver-listing tier) target solo female travelers with sustained personal interrogations, unmarked Corinth Canal "photo stops" at isolated lay-bys, and unsolicited sales pitches for €600 private day tours. At Mycenae and Epidaurus, rental-car break-ins are the single most damaging incident — Athens-area thieves work the unattended archaeological car parks while owners walk the citadel-and-museum loop. A safe default: take the KTEL Argolida bus from Athens Kifissos (€15.70 single, €29 return per ktelargolidas.gr), withdraw cash only at Greek bank-branded ATMs, refuse "charged in your home currency" at every prompt, and carry passports and electronics on your person at every sightseeing stop.
