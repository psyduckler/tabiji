# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are organized by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was, working through the first two timelines will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind.

## In the first fifteen minutes

Leave the venue. If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — a clip-joint bar, a restaurant with an inflated bill, a rental shop demanding damage payment — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second form of payment. The single most important thing is to get yourself out of the environment and somewhere quiet.

Do not sign anything, including receipts. A signed credit-card slip is much harder to dispute than one you refused to sign. If staff are blocking your exit, dial **171** — the Tourist Police, staffed with English-speaking dispatchers. If 171 is slow to connect, **100** is the Hellenic Police general emergency line. **112**, the European emergency number, works from any mobile phone in Greece and routes to the appropriate service.

Screenshot everything you can, as discreetly as possible: the bill, the sign outside the venue, the street intersection (Google Maps dropped-pin is ideal), the shop's tax registration number (AFM, usually displayed near the register), the card terminal, and the face of the person who served you if you can do it without escalating. The information you capture in these fifteen minutes is the evidence you will use for weeks afterward.

If your bag or wallet was just stolen, shout — shopkeepers and passersby in Greece will often help. If you are at a metro station, the station supervisor's office is the closest authority; staff can call police and request security-camera footage.

## Within the first hour

Walk to the nearest *Tourist Police office* if one is within ten minutes; otherwise, the nearest *Hellenic Police station* (*astinomikó tmíma*). Tourist Police offices exist in Athens (Syntagma), Thessaloniki, Mykonos, Santorini, Rhodes, Corfu, Heraklion, and Chania. Say "I want to file a report" and you will receive a *vevéosi* (police report) with an official stamp. This is the document your credit-card issuer and travel insurer will demand.

Freeze the card that was used. International emergency lines for the major card issuers are listed in Appendix C. These numbers work from Greece and route you to an English-speaking representative. Have your card number (or at least the last four digits) and the approximate transaction amount ready before you call.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

File a chargeback with your card issuer. Use the police-report reference number as documentation. Chargebacks initiated within twenty-four hours of the transaction have a significantly higher success rate than those filed a week later. Visa and Mastercard both offer strong chargeback rights for unauthorized or materially misrepresented charges.

Call **1572** — the Greek National Tourism Organisation (EOT) *Tourist Information* line — for advice on next steps if you are not sure how to escalate. EOT is not a fraud-report line, but the dispatchers are trained on the common tourist scams and will direct you to the correct office.

If your passport was photographed, held, or taken by anyone involved, notify your embassy or consulate immediately. Passport data tied to a scam can show up in fraud attempts weeks later.

Email your travel-insurance provider with a summary of what happened, timestamps, the police-report reference number, and photographs of the bill. Even if you are not sure whether the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position.

If the scam involved an off-platform rental or a wire-fraud hotel booking, open the dispute on the booking platform (Booking.com, Airbnb, Hotels.com) as well as with your card issuer.

## Within the first week

File a formal consumer-protection complaint with the **Hellenic Consumer Ombudsman** (*Synigoros tou Katanaloti*) via its website **synigoroskatanaloti.gr** or hotline **210 646 0862**. The Ombudsman is the Greek analog of the UK's Trading Standards. It does not recover individual losses, but its regulatory actions against repeat-offender restaurants, rental shops, and tour operators are one of the few effective pressures on the scam economy.

For overcharging by licensed taxis, file a complaint with the **Ministry of Transport** taxi-complaint line at the regional level — the Tourist Police can provide the correct number for your island or city.

Submit the full travel-insurance claim with all evidence gathered so far. Include the business's name, the address, the exact transaction details, the police-report reference number, and the chargeback reference.

Consider leaving a neutral, factual review on Google Maps or TripAdvisor. Describe what happened, the amounts involved, and the date, without emotional language or accusations. Reviews from recent victims are the single most reliable way future travelers avoid the same scam. Greek defamation law exists — stick to verifiable facts and receipts.

## If your phone or SIM was stolen

Call your carrier immediately to suspend the line. If you are on a tourist eSIM (Airalo, Saily, etc.), the app will let you suspend the line in a few taps. If your SIM was the target — for example, a SIM-swap scam to intercept your bank's one-time codes — contact your bank's fraud desk before anything else.

File a police report and include your phone's **IMEI** number (the fifteen-digit serial; find it on the original box, in your Apple or Google account, or by dialing \*#06# *before* the theft).

## If the scam involved drink-spiking or physical harm

Go to a hospital before you go to the police. Toxicology tests have a short window and cannot be run retroactively. A list of hospitals with English-speaking staff in each city is in Appendix C.

In Greece, the medical-emergency number is **166** (EKAB national ambulance). **112** works for everything as a fallback. Your embassy can help coordinate English-speaking medical care and, if your passport was involved, coordinate a replacement.
