# How to Use This Book {-}

The United States is enormous. Times Square at midnight, the French Quarter during Mardi Gras, the Las Vegas Strip, the cable cars climbing out of Fisherman's Wharf, the Walk of Fame, a fall afternoon in Central Park, the neon of Nashville's Broadway — there is a lifetime of travel packed inside one country. It is also full of practiced, well-rehearsed scams that target tourists precisely because tourists are distracted, rushed, and far from home. A "monk" presses a shiny bracelet onto your wrist near a Manhattan landmark, then blocks your path for a donation. A costumed Elmo on the Strip poses for a photo with your kids and then demands $40. A "broken-meter" cab from the airport quotes a flat $90 for a ride that should run $35. A friendly stranger outside a Bourbon Street bar bets he can guess where you got your shoes. A rideshare driver you never ordered waves you into his car at arrivals and runs the fare to triple.

We want you to come home with stories about the view from the top of the Empire State Building, a po'boy eaten standing up at a French Market stall, the first time you saw the Grand Canyon drop away beneath you, and the sound of a brass band spilling out of a Frenchmen Street doorway — not about a $90 airport cab, a $400 "found money" hustle, or a timeshare pitch that ate the only free afternoon of your trip.

That is why this book is short and practical. It documents 238 specific scams that trip up visitors across forty-one American cities, and it tells you the exact moves that stop them. Nothing in it is theoretical. Every scam has been reported by recent travelers, cross-referenced against local TV-news investigations, police and tourism-board advisories, and consumer-protection alerts, and confirmed against the patterns that show up again and again in city after city. Where dollar amounts appear, they are the amounts the scam actually charges. Where a fair price appears, it is what the metered, posted, or going rate should be.

## How each scam is written

Every scam entry follows the same short format. First, the name of the scam, the category it falls into (transport, overcharging, tourist trap, counterfeit, distraction theft, and so on), and how serious and how common it is. Then two short sections: **How this scam works** — a plain narrative of how it unfolds in real time, with real dollar amounts where we have them — and **How to avoid it**, the specific moves that shut it down. Each entry ends with where it happens. If you can read one entry, you can read them all.

## The single most important chapter

The next chapter, **The Six Universal Red-Flag Patterns**, is the most important part of this book. The 238 scams across these forty-one cities may sound different from each other, but almost all of them are variations on six patterns: the "free" gift or service that is pressed on you before any price is mentioned; the stranger in an official-looking costume, badge, or lanyard; the broken meter, the surprise surcharge, and the bait price that climbs at the end; the distraction — the spilled drink, the dropped ring, the friendly bump — that empties a pocket; the unsolicited "helper" who steers you toward a partner business for a cut; and the high-pressure pitch that traps you in a room or a vehicle until you sign or pay. Learn the six patterns and you will spot a new scam the moment it starts — even one we have not written down. Spend ten quiet minutes on that chapter before your trip. The rest of the book will then read as practical examples of patterns you already understand.

## The three bonuses at the back

After the city chapters, you will find three appendices that we hope you treasure more than the city chapters themselves.

The first is the **exit-line card**. Short lines you can say, word for word, at the moments when your politeness would normally get you into trouble — the line for "no thank you, I'm not interested," the line for the costumed character who wants you to pay, the line for "I didn't order this, take it off." When you are caught off guard, having the exact words ready is what breaks the script. Take a screenshot of it on your phone.

The second is the **recovery playbook**. A step-by-step flow of what to do in the first fifteen minutes, hour, day, and week after a scam. Most scam books only tell you how to avoid scams. If the worst happens — a cab driver who won't unlock the doors until you pay the inflated fare, a fake ticket that won't scan at the gate, a credit-card number skimmed at a counterfeit ATM — you want this.

The third is the **emergency contacts appendix**. The national emergency number (911), the non-emergency police line concept and how to find each city's, the FTC consumer-fraud report line (1-877-FTC-HELP) and ReportFraud.ftc.gov, your card issuer's fraud department, and how to reach tourist-assistance and local consumer-protection offices in each of the forty-one cities, organized so you can find what you need in seconds.

## A suggested way to use the book

A week or two before your trip, read the first two chapters and skim the three appendices. Then skip straight to the chapters for the cities you will actually be visiting — there is no need to read about a scam in a city you are not going to. Take a screenshot of the exit-line card and the emergency contacts on your phone. If anything in this book turns out to apply during your trip, open it again in the cab or in your hotel room and check what to do.

You will almost certainly not need most of it. That is the point.

Travel smart, and enjoy the trip.
