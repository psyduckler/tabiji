# Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook {-}

If something in this book has happened to you, this appendix is where you start. The actions below are ordered by urgency, not by severity. Even if you are not sure yet how serious the scam was — a padded restaurant check on the Vegas Strip, a fake "city pass" booth at Fisherman's Wharf, a surprise charge for damage on a rental car you did not cause, a timeshare "survey" in Orlando that turned into a deposit — working through the first hour in order will put you in the best possible position to recover your money, your evidence, and your peace of mind. The single most important rule in the United States is this: **the recovery path is completely different depending on how you paid.** A credit-card charge is highly recoverable. A debit-card charge is recoverable but slower. A Zelle, Venmo, or Cash App transfer you sent yourself is, in most cases, gone the moment it lands. Read the section that matches your payment method first.

## In the first fifteen minutes

**Leave the situation.** If you are physically inside a place where you were scammed — a Times Square "costumed character" who surrounded you for a tip, a Strip nightclub running up a tab, a "CD artist" who put an album in your hands on Hollywood Boulevard, a counter agent demanding a fee that was not on the booking — do not argue, do not try to negotiate, and do not pay with a second card to "sort it out later." Get yourself out and somewhere quiet. In the United States you are almost never in physical danger from a tourist scam, but you can be talked into a second mistake while you are flustered.

**Do not sign anything you disagree with**, including a credit-card slip, a tablet signature pad, or a paper receipt for an amount you dispute. A charge you signed for is harder to dispute than one you refused — though, importantly, in the U.S. an unsigned card charge is still fully disputable, so refusing to sign does not waive any rights. If a merchant is physically blocking your exit or threatening you, that is unlawful — call **911**, state your location, and stay on the line.

**Screenshot and photograph everything**, right now, while you are still on site: the bill or receipt, the posted price board, the menu, the storefront and its street number, the business name on the window or the door, the card terminal screen, the contract or "waiver" you were handed, and any name badge or vehicle number (a taxi medallion number, a rideshare plate, a tour-bus operator number). The evidence you capture in these fifteen minutes is what your card issuer, the FTC, and the police will rely on for weeks afterward. If you paid through an app, screenshot the transaction detail screen — the recipient handle, the amount, the timestamp, and the confirmation number — before you do anything else, because that screen is your only proof of who received the money.

**If your wallet, phone, or bag was just stolen** rather than scammed, that is a theft, not a billing dispute, and the timeline below for *card disputes* still applies to any charges that appear afterward. Report it to the nearest police as described under "When to call local police" and, separately, freeze your cards and your credit as described in the dedicated sections.

## Within the first hour: stop the bleeding

What you do in the first hour depends entirely on how you paid. Do these in order.

### If you paid by credit card

Call the number on the back of your card and **report the charge as disputed or fraudulent.** Have the merchant name, the amount, and the date ready. Credit cards give you the strongest protection of any payment method in the United States: under the **Fair Credit Billing Act** (implemented by the Federal Reserve's **Regulation Z**), you are liable for at most **$50** of unauthorized charges, and in practice every major issuer waives even that with a **$0 fraud liability** policy. Just as important, Regulation Z also covers *billing errors* — which include goods or services you never received, or that were "not as described" — so an overcharge, a phantom fee, or a tour that was nothing like what was sold is disputable even though you handed over the card yourself.

You do not have to resolve anything on this first call beyond getting the card flagged. If the physical card was taken or skimmed, ask the issuer to **freeze or replace the card** so no further charges can post. Write down the name of the representative and any reference number they give you.

### If you paid by debit card

A debit-card charge pulls money directly from your checking account, so the urgency is higher — that is real cash already gone, not a line on a statement you can withhold. Call your bank's number on the back of the card and **report the unauthorized transaction immediately.** Debit cards are governed by the **Electronic Fund Transfer Act** and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's **Regulation E**, and the protection is strong but strictly time-sensitive:

- Report within **2 business days** of learning of the loss or theft, and your liability is capped at **$50**.
- Report after 2 business days but within **60 days** of the statement that shows the transaction, and your liability can rise to **$500**.
- Wait longer than **60 days** after that statement, and you can be held liable for the **full amount** of anything taken after the 60-day mark.

The clock is the reason to call within the hour, not the week. Ask the bank to **block the card and issue a new one**, and ask whether they will place a temporary credit on the account while they investigate (see the dispute section for the bank's investigation deadlines).

### If you paid by Zelle, Venmo, or Cash App

**This is the hardest money to get back, and speed is your only lever.** A peer-to-peer transfer you authorized and sent is treated very differently from a stolen card. Because *you* approved the payment — even though you were tricked into it — it generally does **not** qualify as an "unauthorized" transfer under Regulation E, which means your bank and the app are usually **not legally required to reimburse you.** Once a Zelle, Venmo, or Cash App payment is sent and accepted by the recipient, it typically **cannot be reversed or canceled.** Treat any "send money to this handle" instruction from a tour seller, a "landlord," a parking-app text, or a counter agent as money you may never see again.

That said, do all of the following immediately, because a small fraction of cases are recoverable if the funds have not yet been claimed:

- **Open the app and look for a Cancel option.** If the recipient has not yet enrolled or accepted (most often on Zelle, sometimes on the others), an unaccepted payment can occasionally be canceled from the transaction screen.
- **Report the transaction as a scam inside the app** (Venmo and Cash App both have a report-a-problem flow; Zelle directs you to your bank). File it as fraud, not as a "wrong amount," and keep the case number.
- **Call your bank's fraud line** and report it as a scam regardless of the above. If the scammer gained *access to your account* and sent the money without your involvement, that *is* unauthorized and Regulation E protections apply — so describe exactly what happened and let the bank classify it.
- **Do not send a second payment** to "verify," "release," or "unlock" the first one. That instruction is always part of the scam.

### If you paid by wire transfer

A bank wire (for a "deposit" on a vacation rental, a too-good-to-be-true car, or a "title company") is also very hard to recall, but unlike an app transfer it is worth an immediate call to your bank to request a **wire recall** — occasionally possible within a short window if the receiving bank has not released the funds. Report it as fraud at the same time.

## Within the first twenty-four hours

**File the formal dispute with your card issuer in writing.** The first-hour phone call flags the charge; the written dispute is what creates your legal protections and the paper trail you will need.

- **Credit card (Regulation Z):** you must dispute a billing error **in writing within 60 days** of the date the first statement showing the charge was sent to you. The issuer must **acknowledge your dispute within 30 days** and **resolve it within two billing cycles (no more than 90 days).** While the dispute is open you may **withhold payment on the disputed amount**, and the issuer **may not report that amount as delinquent** to the credit bureaus. Most issuers now let you open the dispute in the app or website; do that, then follow up with a written or secure-message confirmation that includes your evidence.
- **Debit card (Regulation E):** once you notify the bank, it generally has **10 business days** to investigate (**20 business days** if the account has been open less than 30 days). If the investigation runs longer, the bank must usually issue a **provisional credit** for the disputed amount (minus up to $50) while it continues. Send your evidence in writing and keep the confirmation.

Attach the screenshots from the first fifteen minutes: the receipt, the price board, the contract, and a short factual description of what was sold versus what you received. For a "not as described" dispute (a tour, a show ticket, a rental), state plainly what you were promised and how the actual service differed — that framing is what moves a billing-error claim.

**Open the dispute on the platform too, if you booked through one.** If the scam was an off-platform "deal," a vacation rental, an activity, or a hotel booked through a marketplace, file a claim with **Airbnb, Vrbo, Booking.com, Expedia, Viator, GetYourGuide, StubHub, or Ticketmaster** in parallel with your card dispute. Platforms can sometimes refund a deposit the card network cannot, and a platform record strengthens the card claim.

**Notify your travel-insurance provider** if you have a policy (including the coverage bundled with many premium credit cards). Email a summary with timestamps, the merchant name, the amount, and your screenshots. Even if you are unsure the loss is covered, opening the claim early protects your position.

## Within the first week: report it to the authorities

Reporting a U.S. tourist scam to the federal government will rarely refund you directly, but it does two things that matter: it creates an official record that can support your card dispute, your insurance claim, and any police report; and it feeds the databases that let regulators and the FBI build cases against repeat operators. File both reports below — they go to different agencies and serve different purposes.

### File with the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov

The Federal Trade Commission's official reporting site is **[reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov)**. It takes about ten minutes and covers essentially every scam in this book — deceptive pricing, fake tickets, bogus "official" fees, imposter sellers, and unfair business practices. The FTC **does not resolve individual complaints or recover your money for you**; instead it shares reports with thousands of federal, state, and local law-enforcement partners, and patterns of complaints against a named business are what trigger enforcement. After you file you can download a copy of your report — keep it with your dispute paperwork.

### File with the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov for online fraud

If any part of the scam happened **online** — a fake booking site, a phishing text, a counterfeit-ticket listing, a "rental" advertised on a marketplace, a payment-app fraud, an email impersonating a hotel or a host — file with the **FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center at [ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov)**. IC3 is the FBI's central hub for cyber-enabled crime. For fraud involving a wire or an app transfer, filing at IC3 **quickly** matters: in some cases the FBI's recovery process can help intercept funds that have not yet been withdrawn, and a same-week filing has a far better chance than one filed weeks later. Include every detail — recipient handles, account numbers, the website URL, email headers, and the exact amounts and timestamps.

### Consider your state and local consumer-protection agencies

For a deceptive in-person business — a padded check, a phantom resort fee, a bait-and-switch tour, an aggressive timeshare pitch — your **state Attorney General's consumer-protection division** is the right venue, and many states have an online complaint form. Tourist-heavy jurisdictions also run local channels worth using: Nevada's Attorney General Bureau of Consumer Protection for Las Vegas Strip disputes, the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services for Orlando and Miami, the New York State Attorney General for New York City. A neutral, factual complaint naming the business and the date adds to the same evidence trail.

## Freeze your credit if your identity was exposed

If the scam involved more than a single charge — if anyone obtained your **Social Security number, your date of birth, your driver's license, or your full card and billing details** (a fake "hotel registration" form, a phishing site that captured your data, a skimmer, a "rental application") — assume your identity is at risk and **place a credit freeze.** A freeze is free, it does not affect your existing accounts or your credit score, and it stops anyone from opening new credit in your name until you lift it.

You must freeze your file at **all three** nationwide credit bureaus separately — a freeze at one does not carry to the others:

- **Equifax** — [equifax.com/personal/credit-report-services/credit-freeze](https://www.equifax.com/personal/credit-report-services/credit-freeze/) or 1-800-685-1111.
- **Experian** — [experian.com/freeze](https://www.experian.com/freeze/) or 1-888-397-3742.
- **TransUnion** — [transunion.com/credit-freeze](https://www.transunion.com/credit-freeze) or 1-888-909-8872.

Each bureau gives you a PIN or login to lift the freeze later, temporarily or permanently — keep those somewhere safe. If you want monitoring short of a full freeze, you can instead place a free **one-year fraud alert** (renewable) by contacting any one of the three, which is then required to notify the other two. If your identity was actually used to open accounts, also start an official recovery plan at the FTC's **[IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov)**, which generates a personalized checklist and an Identity Theft Report you can hand to creditors.

## When to call local police for a report number

Most tourist overcharges are civil billing disputes, not crimes the police will actively investigate — an officer will not march you back to a restaurant to claw back a padded check. But there are clear situations where you want a **police report number**, because your card issuer, your insurer, or the bureaus may require one:

- **Theft, pickpocketing, or a stolen wallet or phone.**
- **Outright fraud** — a counterfeit ticket, a fake "official" who took cash for a nonexistent fee, identity theft, a skimmer.
- **Any threat, intimidation, or being physically prevented from leaving.**
- **Any loss large enough that your insurer or card issuer asks for a report**, which many do above a certain dollar amount.

For an **emergency or a crime in progress**, dial **911**. For a **non-emergency report after the fact**, use the local non-emergency line — most U.S. cities use **311** for non-emergency city services, or you can search "[city name] police non-emergency number." Many departments (including the **NYPD**, **LAPD**, and others in major tourist cities) let you **file a minor-crime or theft report online**, which gives you the report number without a station visit. Ask specifically for the **report number** (sometimes called the incident or case number) and write it down — that number is what links your police report to your card dispute and your insurance claim. Bring or have ready a government ID, the location and time, and your screenshots.

A practical note for international visitors: U.S. police reports are filed with **city or county police, not a national tourist-police force** — there is no equivalent of the dedicated tourist-police kiosks found in some countries. If you are a foreign national and your **passport** was lost, stolen, or taken, contact your **embassy or nearest consulate** in addition to the police; passport replacement and a police report are separate processes, and your embassy will walk you through the first.

## A note on what to expect

Recovery in the United States is a paperwork process, and the paperwork rewards speed and specificity. The traveler who calls the card issuer within the hour, files the written dispute within a day, reports to the FTC and IC3 within the week, and keeps every screenshot and reference number in one place recovers far more often than the one who waits to "see if the charge sticks." Card disputes succeed at a high rate when the evidence is clean. App and wire transfers succeed rarely, which is exactly why the section above tells you to stop them in the first hour rather than the first week. Work the list in order, keep your records, and do not send a second payment to anyone — that last instruction, in every version, is the scam trying to finish the job.
