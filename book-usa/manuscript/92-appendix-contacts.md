# Appendix C: Emergency Contacts {-}

Every phone number in this appendix was verified current at the time of publication (verified 2026-06-01). Phone numbers and web addresses do change — if a number below fails to connect or a link is dead, search the organization's name (the *Federal Trade Commission*, the *FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center*, *AARP Fraud Watch Network*) and you will almost always find the current line on the first page of results. Where a number could not be verified against an official source, we have omitted it rather than guess; the entry instead points you to the search you should run before you travel.

**Before you travel**, save the two emergency lines below on your phone, and read the one section that applies to you. Most readers of this book are US residents, for whom **911** is the only number you truly need to memorize. If you are visiting the United States from abroad, also save your own country's embassy or consulate number — see *For foreign visitors* at the end of this appendix. Even a verified number can become a wrong number between editions, so confirm anything critical against the official source before you rely on it.

## Emergency (anywhere in the United States)

- **911** — the single nationwide emergency number for **police, ambulance, and fire**. Free from any phone, including a cell phone with **no active SIM, no service plan, or zero balance**, as long as there is any network signal. This is the only number to call when there is an immediate threat to your safety — a scam that has turned into a robbery, someone blocking your exit, drink-spiking, or any medical emergency. If you cannot speak, many US areas now support **Text-to-911**; if it is not available where you are, your text will bounce back telling you to call.
- **112** — if you dial 112 on a cell phone in the United States, the network routes you to **911**. You do not need to remember it; 911 is the number Americans use. It is listed here only so that visitors arriving with the European emergency number in muscle memory know it will still reach help.

When you call 911, **state your location first** — a cross-street, a landmark, a hotel name, or a venue ("the boardwalk by Pier 39," "the lobby of the Bellagio," "Times Square, near the TKTS booth") — before you explain what happened. If you are calling from a cell phone the dispatcher can often see your approximate location automatically, but a spoken landmark is faster and more reliable. Stay on the line until the dispatcher tells you to hang up.

## Reporting fraud and scams (not an emergency)

For a scam that is over — an overcharge you already paid, a fake "ticket" you bought, a deposit a fake host pocketed, a card charge you did not authorize — **911 is not the right number**. Use the channels below. None of them recovers your money on the spot, but they create the official record your card issuer and travel insurer will ask for, and they feed the data that drives enforcement against repeat-offender operators.

- **Federal Trade Commission (FTC) — consumer fraud:** **1-877-382-4357** (1-877-FTC-HELP) or file online at **[reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov)**. The FTC is the central US clearinghouse for consumer-fraud and scam reports of every kind — deceptive charges, fake-ticket and fake-tour rip-offs, imposter scams, identity theft. The phone line is staffed in English and Spanish; the website is the faster route and lets you upload evidence.
- **FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) — online and wire fraud:** file at **[ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov)**. Use IC3 for anything that happened **online or by wire transfer** — a fraudulent vacation-rental or hotel booking site, a "host" who pushed you to wire a deposit, a phishing message, a gift-card or crypto payment to a scammer. IC3 takes complaints online only; there is no public hotline for filing, so treat the website as the official channel. Reports help the FBI link scam networks across state lines.
- **Your state Attorney General — consumer protection:** every US state has an Attorney General's office with a consumer-protection division that takes scam and unfair-business complaints, and it is the right place to report a scam tied to a physical business. We do not list fifty numbers here because they change; instead, search **"[state] attorney general consumer protection"** — for example, *"Florida attorney general consumer protection"* or *"New York attorney general consumer protection"* — and file with the office in the state **where the scam happened**, which may not be the state where you live.
- **AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline:** **1-877-908-3360**. A free helpline (you do not need to be an AARP member) staffed by trained fraud specialists who will talk through what happened and what to do next. Especially useful if you are shaken, unsure whether you were scammed, or helping an older relative who was targeted.

If your card was charged fraudulently, **freeze the card first** (see below) and **then** file the report. The fraud teams and the FTC will both ask for the details — the merchant name, the amount, and the date and time — so have them ready.

## Card issuer emergency lines

The fastest way to freeze a compromised card is to **call the number printed on the back of the card** (or the one inside your bank's app). That line routes straight to your own issuer's fraud desk, which can freeze the card and start a dispute in under a minute. Save that number, or a photo of the back of each card you travel with, somewhere you can reach it even if the wallet itself is gone.

If you cannot reach the back-of-card line — the card was stolen and you have no record of the number — go to the issuer's official website (search the bank or card-network name) for its 24/7 fraud and lost-card line. We do not reprint network or bank fraud numbers here because they differ by issuer and change without notice, and a wrong number at that moment is worse than none; the number on your card, or the one on the issuer's own site, is always the authoritative one.

For the dispute itself, the major US card networks give you strong chargeback rights for unauthorized and materially misrepresented charges — one reason we recommend paying with a credit card rather than a debit card while you travel. The step-by-step is in **Appendix B: Post-Scam Recovery Playbook**.

## For foreign visitors

If you are visiting the United States from another country, **911** still covers every emergency exactly as it does for residents. But for a lost or stolen passport, an arrest, a hospitalization far from home, or any situation where you need your own government's help, you will want your country's **embassy in Washington, DC, or its nearest consulate** in the US.

Look this up and save it **before you travel** — embassy switchboards and after-hours emergency lines change, and the authoritative source is always your own government's foreign-ministry website. For step-by-step guidance on locating the right office and its emergency line, see Tabiji's **[find your embassy guide](https://tabiji.ai/embassies)**, or search **"[your country] embassy United States"** and save both the main switchboard and the after-hours emergency line. Most embassies operate a separate 24/7 line for citizens in distress that is not the same as the daytime visa switchboard — save that one too.

Two notes that matter for the scams in this book. First, no legitimate US official — police, immigration, customs, or "tourist police" — will ever demand a cash payment on the street, by gift card, or by wire transfer; that demand is itself the scam, and the answer is 911 if you feel unsafe or the FTC report above if you do not. Second, if anyone holds your passport "as a deposit" — a tour operator, a rental host, a hotel that copied it at check-in and then won't return it — that is unlawful, and your embassy will want to know, because passport data tied to a scam can resurface in fraud attempts weeks later.
