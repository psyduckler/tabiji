Washington is a low-violence, high-confidence city: the seven scams that recur here are all classic tourist traps, run by operators who count on visitors being unfamiliar with how the capital actually works. Nobody is going to mug you on the Mall in daylight. Instead they'll exploit the gaps in your local knowledge — that the DC DMV doesn't issue parking tickets, that pedicabs can't legally pick up on Park Service land, that every Smithsonian museum is already free. The risk is moderate and almost entirely financial, but the losses stack up fast when you're moving between monuments with your phone out and your guard down.

Three zones do the heavy lifting. The National Mall is the densest: between the Washington Monument and the Lincoln Memorial you'll find Unlicensed Pedicab Price Gouging (no fare quoted, then $50–$100 demanded), the Friendship Bracelet and CD Hustle near the Smithsonian entrances, and Fake Tour Guide Solicitation working the White House and Capitol steps with $20–$50 "personalized" walks that recycle the free NPS plaque text. The Metro is the second hot zone — Smithsonian, Gallery Place-Chinatown, Metro Center, and Union Station all see the Metro Station Change Scam (the two-tens-for-a-twenty switch) and far more costly Distraction Pickpocketing on crowded Orange, Blue, and Silver line trains and escalators. Third, Georgetown and the Dupont Circle metered spots are where the Fake Parking Ticket QR Code lands on out-of-state plates, plus Three-Card Monte near Union Station and along Pennsylvania Avenue.

Three defaults cover most of it. Never scan a QR code on a parking ticket — go to dmv.dc.gov and search by plate; if it's not there, it's fake. Never agree to anything before a price is fixed: get the pedicab fare verbally confirmed before you sit down, and don't pay strangers for tours that are free through the National Park Service. And on the Metro, keep your wallet in a front pocket and your bag zipped in front of you — never make change for anyone.
