Las Vegas runs on a single hustle category, and it runs it relentlessly. All seven documented scams here are tourist traps, and what drives them is the city's own design: millions of out-of-towners in party mode, walking pedestrian corridors lined with people whose income depends on stopping you. There are no pickpockets or fake-cop cons in the playbook — the danger is softer and more legal. It's the manufactured transaction, the verbal price that triples at the destination, the fee buried in fine print. Nobody is going to rob you. They're going to get you to hand over the money yourself.

The Strip is the engine room. The pedestrian crossings and bridges near Caesars Palace and the LINQ Promenade are where the Costumed Character Photo Shakedown operates — a showgirl drapes an arm over your shoulder, an accomplice snaps the shot, and suddenly two performers want $20 each. The same walkways near Planet Hollywood and MGM run the Free CD / Mixtape Hustle, where a "free" CD pressed into your hand becomes a $20 "donation." Working the Strip sidewalks and Convention Center perimeter are the clipboard promoters behind the Timeshare Vacation Club Pitch, whose "90 minutes" for free buffet vouchers stretches into a four-to-six-hour high-pressure room. Downtown shifts the threat: Fremont Street after dark and the dim side streets off the Strip host Three-Card Monte and Rigged Street Games, run by crews of shills you cannot mathematically beat. At Harry Reid Airport and the hotel curbs, limo drivers quote a fair fare and bill triple via the Overpriced Limousine bait-and-switch.

Two defaults protect a first-timer. First: keep your hands in your pockets and never let a stranger put something in them or an arm around you — every "free" object on the Strip has a price. Second: never agree to a number out loud. Get the limo fare in writing or just use Uber and Lyft from the marked airport rideshare zones, and check a hotel's all-in total on ResortFeeChecker before you book, because that $49 rate becomes $189 at checkout.
